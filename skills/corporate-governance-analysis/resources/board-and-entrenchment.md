# The board, the tenure clock and the entrenchment package

The board is the stockholders' only standing check on management. Grading it is concrete
work, and most of it is counting. The counting is necessary and not sufficient. In problem
companies the recurring theme is not a composition failure. It is an ineffective board that
will not ask hard questions of a powerful chief executive.

## The three CalPERS tests

```
Test 1  Are a majority of directors outside directors?
Test 2  Is the chair independent of the company, meaning not the chief executive?
Test 3  Are the compensation and audit committees composed entirely of outsiders?
```

Inside directors are current employees plus former managers of the firm. One insider on
either committee fails Test 3. Failing all three is rare and severe; Disney in 1997 was the
only firm in the S&P 500 to do it.

## Going past the labels

For every nominal outsider, hunt for ties the proxy does not compute for you: business or
consulting relationships, legal fees, charitable donations to the director's institution,
personal relationships, and board interlocks where chief executives sit on each other's
boards. Then check three things the numbers do show.

- **Stake against fee.** Compare each director's shareholding value to the annual director
  fee. When the fee is larger, the director's economic interest is aligned with whoever
  sets the fee.
- **Bought or granted.** Shares granted by the firm are weaker alignment than shares the
  director bought.
- **Who nominates.** A nominating committee of independent directors is the reform. Chief
  executive recommendation is the failure mode.

Board size benchmarks against the modern range of 9 to 11. Smaller boards are more
effective in the evidence, and that is close to the only composition finding the data
supports.

| Board characteristic | 1970s | 1998 |
|---|---|---|
| Median board size | 16 to 20 | 9 to 11 |
| Typical insider directors | 6 or more | 2 |
| Directors paid in stock or options | 4% | 78% |
| Boards with nominating committees | 2% | 75% |

## The board table against peers

The deliverable is a table with the peer average beside every company value. The rows fall
into three groups. Executives: headcount, average reported compensation, average age,
average tenure. Board: headcount, non-employee directors and percentage, members owning
shares and percentage, shares held by the board, average meeting attendance. Commitments:
directors sitting on peer boards, directors sitting on other boards, board average age,
board average tenure.

Report director ownership two ways. The share of directors who own stock says little on its
own. A board where every director owns shares and the board holds 0.6 percent of the
company is not an owner board.

A vendor score such as ISS Governance QuickScore is a cross-check, on a 1 to 10 scale where
1 is best. Pull the sub-scores for audit, board, shareholder rights and compensation, then
read the underlying policies. Override the score in either direction when the policies
justify it, and say why.

## The red-flag checklist

Score shareholder power down for each of these:

- a staggered board;
- no majority-vote requirement in uncontested director elections, so a single vote in
  favor can reappoint a director;
- takeover defenses in force;
- weak policies on removing non-performing executives;
- independence defined only by exchange listing rules, which are not stringent;
- a pay package where the performance-linked share is extreme during rapid growth, which
  rewards growth and can encourage excessive risk-taking.

Look for the opposite signal too. A management change made in response to shareholder
dissatisfaction is direct evidence that the board answers to owners.

## Signals from the annual meeting

Unvoted proxies count as votes for incumbent management, so an unvoted proxy is not
neutral. The visible signal of anger is the withhold or against percentage on directors
and on say-on-pay.

| Reading | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| Withhold votes in low single digits | Normal; no signal |
| Withhold votes above 20 to 30 percent | Serious revolt signal |
| Two or three revolt signals together | Change usually follows |

Institutional support for management resolutions ran between 90.9 and 93.5 percent across
2004 to 2008, and support for shareholder resolutions ran between 25 and 32 percent. A
large institutional register is not a monitoring register.

## The tenure clock

Governance is a cycle, not a state. The cycle repeats, and chief executive tenure is the
clock.

```
1  Chief executive succeeds early and accumulates authority
2  Chief executive shapes the board; the board becomes captive
3  Major decisions go unchallenged; value-destroying investments follow
4  Performance deteriorates
5  Shareholders revolt: protest resignations, hostile bid, mass withhold vote
6  The board reforms: independence, separated chair, executive sessions
7  A new chief executive succeeds, tenure lengthens, and step 1 returns
```

Disney ran the full cycle. The board was captured through the 1990s and rubber-stamped the
ABC purchase in 1996. It was ranked the worst board in America in 1997. The stock halved
between 1998 and 2002. Two directors resigned in protest in 2003. A hostile bid and a 43
percent withhold vote landed in 2004, and by 2008 the board was independent. Then the drift
began. The chair and chief executive roles were recombined in 2011. The heir apparent left
in 2016. The term was extended again in 2017, because the incumbent was deemed essential to
a pending merger.

Drift signals worth naming: a chair and chief executive role separated and then recombined,
an heir apparent departing, repeated term extensions, and the absence over many years of
any public friction over a major capital allocation decision.

A reformed board adopts seven changes. Use them as a checklist.

1. At least two executive sessions a year, with no management present.
2. A non-management presiding director who leads those sessions and helps set the agenda.
3. A stricter definition of director independence.
4. A requirement that a substantial majority of directors meet it.
5. Smaller committees, with chairs rotated among independent directors.
6. Succession planning, plus evaluation of both the board and management.
7. Continuing education for directors.

## The entrenchment inventory

Sort the devices by whether stockholders had to approve them. The ones adopted without a
vote are the stronger signal, because owners never consented.

| Mechanism | Stockholder approval | Why it entrenches |
|---|---|---|
| Greenmail | Not needed | Corporate cash buys off a disciplining threat |
| Golden parachutes | Not needed | Managers are paid to lose, so resisting a good deal costs them nothing |
| Poison pills | Not needed | Makes an unwanted bid prohibitively dilutive, so no bid arrives |
| Shark repellents | Required | Same aim, but with consent, so it carries legitimacy the others lack |
| Overpaying on takeovers | Not needed | Builds an empire and raises the cost of acquiring the acquirer |

```
Greenmail transfer = (buyback price per share − raider's average cost) × shares bought back
```

Size each parachute as a multiple of salary plus bonus and total the package. Read a
parachute as removing the personal cost of losing a control contest, which is exactly the
cost that would otherwise deter resistance.

Where a pill and a staggered board are both in force, the market for corporate control is
not a live counter-force at this company, however badly it performs.

## The acquisition record

Overpaying needs no charter provision, and it is the quickest way to impoverish
stockholders.

```
Premium ($)     = bid value − target market value 30 days before the first bid or rumor
Premium (%)     = premium / that prior market value
Market's verdict = acquirer announcement abnormal return × acquirer market cap
Recovery ratio  = proceeds from later divestitures / original price paid
```

When the acquirer's own value falls by roughly the whole premium, the market expects zero
synergy and reads the premium as a straight transfer to the target's holders. A recovery
ratio below one on a later divestiture is value destroyed and admitted.

**Worked read — Kodak and Sterling Drug.** Sterling traded near $40 when bidding started.
The rival dropped out at $72. Kodak won at $89.50, a $5.1 billion bid against a $3.0
billion market value 30 days earlier, so the premium was $2.1 billion. Kodak's own market
value fell about $2.2 billion on the announcement. Over the next five years Sterling's
revenues grew and its operating earnings did not. Kodak later sold the pieces for $1.68
billion and $2.9 billion, a recovery ratio of 0.90, and its stock rose on the news.

Two measurement cautions. Take the premium against the pre-rumor price, not against an
intermediate bidding-war price, which understates it badly. And judge the deal on operating
earnings against the peer group, not on combined revenue growth.

## Pitfalls

- Stopping at the CalPERS tests. A board can pass all three and still be a rubber stamp.
- Using board composition to predict value. Use investor-protection provisions instead.
- Reading a director's shareholding without comparing it to the director's fee.
- Treating a vendor governance score as the answer rather than as a cross-check.
- Missing over-boarding, where most directors serve on several other boards.
- Grading the board once and never re-dating it.
- Waiting for a single revolt signal. Change usually needs two or three at once.
