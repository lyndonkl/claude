# The failure gallery

Three ways a narrative goes wrong. Each has a different tell, a different screen, and a
different defence. The third is the one that gets past careful people.

---

## 1. The runaway story

A runaway story is one you want to be true, so you stop asking questions. Three ingredients
make it run.

```
runaway story = charismatic and likeable narrator
              + a disliked status quo being disrupted
              + a claimed societal benefit
```

Once all three are present, the questions stop, because the answers might put the story at
risk. That is the whole mechanism. It is not gullibility; it is the cost of asking.

### Theranos, October 2015

Valued at $9 billion, one of roughly 125 venture-backed companies then worth $1 billion or
more. The story was Elizabeth Holmes and the nanotainer: a drop of blood replacing a full
draw. All three ingredients were present. A founder cast as a role model. A status quo
everybody dislikes, in invasive and expensive blood testing. A clear societal benefit in
cheap diagnostics for everyone.

The apparent check was the board.

| Board member | Designation |
|---|---|
| Henry Kissinger | Former Secretary of State |
| Bill Perry | Former Secretary of Defense |
| George Schultz | Former Secretary of State |
| Bill Frist | Former Senate Majority Leader |
| Sam Nunn | Former Senator |
| Gary Roughead | Former Navy Admiral |
| James Mattis | Former Marine Corps General |
| Dick Kovocovich | Former CEO of Wells Fargo |
| Riley Bechtel | Former CEO of Bechtel |
| William Foege | Epidemiologist |

One epidemiologist. Nobody else with a background in blood diagnostics. The board was
prestigious and domain-irrelevant, and it substituted for a genuine check on the central
technical claim.

### What it teaches about valuation

No discount rate saves you here. A higher cost of capital prices the risk that a real
business underperforms. It does not price a claim that cannot be true. The Theranos claim
belonged to the **impossible** class, and the only defence was a first-principles question:
is this even possible? Not "will they execute?"

### The defence, as procedure

1. Score the three ingredients. Two or three yeses means you are in the danger zone.
2. Write down the questions you have not asked — the ones whose answers would hurt. Ask them.
3. Test the core claim against first principles rather than execution risk.
4. Count board members with real domain knowledge of the central claim. Prestige is not
   expertise.
5. Compare the story to the numbers the company itself reports.
6. Track the narrator's record for broken promises and evasive answers.
7. Look for a business-model flaw plus denial. A flaw management acknowledges is a risk. A
   flaw management denies is an ingredient.

When the meltdown equation reaches two of three — untrustworthy storyteller, story at war
with its own numbers, bad business model plus denial — mark the story down before the
market does. Treat it as a narrative **break**, not a shift.

The pattern only bites on stories you find appealing. Running the checklist exclusively on
companies you already dislike wastes it.

## 2. The big market delusion

Every company chasing a huge market can be individually defensible and collectively
impossible. Venture investors fund many entrepreneurs going after the same market, and each
is priced on the potential of that whole market. Add up what all those valuations imply
about future revenue, and the total exceeds any plausible size for the market itself.

This is the **implausible** class, and it is invisible from inside a single valuation. You
only see it by aggregating across the sector.

counted. Add Alibaba, Baidu, Yahoo! and Twitter and the running total passes $464 billion,
with fifteen companies still to go. The aggregate was about $522 billion against $146.6
billion of actual revenue in 2015. No individual valuation looked silly.

### The tells

- The story justifies a company's growth with a macro market forecast, and nobody ever
  aggregates.
- The test was run on domestic players only. The 2015 example needs the non-US names to
  reach $522 billion.
- Each company's own bullish margin was used to impute its breakeven revenue, which
  understates the revenue required and hides the problem.
- Someone concluded the biggest player is therefore safe. In a big market delusion the
  whole sector is priced on the same double-counted pot.

### The response

Act on the sector, not the name. Reduce exposure across it rather than picking the one you
like best. Then run the same arithmetic on your own model: translate the growth rate into
year-N dollar revenues and divide by market size.

## 3. The improbable but not impossible case

The dangerous one. No single line trips a screen. The combination is the problem, and it
sits on the growth, risk and reinvestment triangle.

### "Willy Wonkitis" — a sell-side Tesla model, mid-2013

A fifteen-year DCF covering FY2013 to FY2028.

| Line | 2013 | 2028 |
|---|---|---|
| Unit volume | 24,298 | 1,137,780 |
| Revenue per unit | $93,403 | $59,554 |
| Total sales | $2,478M | $68,059M |
| EBITDA margin | 6.0% | 17.8% |
| EBIT margin | 1.8% | 15.3% |
| Net income | $44M | $9,050M |
| Unlevered free cash flow | $78M | $8,005M |

Annual unit growth ran 52%, 75%, 34%, 73%, 43%, 36%, 32%, 21%, 18%, 17%, 13%, 13%, 12%,
12%, 10%. Capital spending settled at 3% of sales, with negative working-capital changes.
Exit assumptions: an EBITDA multiple of 8.0x–12.0x, perpetuity growth of 3.0%–5.0%, price
to sales of 130%–180%, and a discount rate of 9.0%–13.0%.

Read line by line, every input is arguable. Read together, the model claims enormous
growth, expanding margins and almost no reinvestment at the same time — all three corners
of the triangle, for free. The exit perpetuity growth of 3% to 5% also trips the impossible
screen wherever it exceeds the economy's growth rate.

The screen that catches it is marginal ROIC. Capital spending at 3% of sales against that
revenue path implies a return on incremental capital far above what any automaker has
sustained.

### The mirror image: a scenario that justifies any price

The same logic runs backwards. Amazon in January 2000, value per share by compounded
revenue growth and target operating margin:

| Growth \ Margin | 6% | 8% | 10% | 12% | 14% |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 30% | $(1.94) | $2.95 | $7.84 | $12.71 | $17.57 |
| 35% | $1.41 | $8.37 | $15.33 | $22.27 | $29.21 |
| 40% | $6.10 | $15.93 | $25.74 | $35.54 | $45.34 |
| 45% | $12.59 | $26.34 | $40.05 | $53.77 | $67.48 |
| 50% | $21.47 | $40.50 | $59.52 | $78.53 | $97.54 |
| 55% | $33.47 | $59.60 | $85.72 | $111.84 | $137.95 |
| 60% | $49.53 | $85.10 | $120.66 | $156.22 | $191.77 |

At the market price of $84, only the most aggressive cells reach it: 60% growth with an 8%
margin or better, 55% growth with 10% or better, 50% growth with 14%. The scenario exists.
It always does. The question is never whether a scenario justifies the price. It is whether
that scenario is **probable**.

### The defence

- Compute marginal ROIC over the forecast and compare it with the best firms in the
  business.
- Compute the terminal excess return and either name the moat or set it to zero.
- Translate the growth path into absolute dollars and ask who loses that revenue.
- Ask the three triangle questions. Is the risk consistent with how and where you are
  growing? Is the reinvestment enough for the growth? Is the risk consistent with the
  reinvestment strategy?
- When a screen fails, change the assumption that caused it and let value fall where it
  falls.
