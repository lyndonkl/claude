# Two worked examples

Uber is the case where market definition dominates: a young private company, no useful
history, a contested market. Tesla is the case where scaling and margins dominate: a liquid,
heavily argued stock at the scaling-up stage. Both run the same six steps.

---

## Uber, June 2014

### Step 1 — the landscape, in six boxes

Drivers: anyone with a car in a covered city can apply, and approved drivers get an Uber
iPhone. Riders: they request rides in the app and watch the car approach. Pricing: Uber
sets the fare, with surge pricing at peak demand. Payment: riders pay Uber by credit card,
not the driver. The slice: Uber keeps about 20% of receipts, already cut in some cities
under pressure from Lyft and Hailo. Reinvestment: Uber owns no cars, so it spends on
technology plus the occasional local acquisition to enter a market.

Each box became a driver. That is what a good landscape survey does.

### Step 2 — the narrative, in five parts

An urban car-service business. It expands the market moderately, by roughly 40% over ten
years, by bringing in new users. It has local networking benefits only — being big in one
city does not help it enter the next. It holds the 20% revenue slice on first-mover
advantages. It keeps the low-capital model, with drivers as contractors.

### Step 3 — the grading

| Claim | Grade | Device |
|---|---|---|
| The urban taxi market | Probable | total market, revenues, earnings |
| Suburban car service and rental | Plausible | a higher growth rate |
| The car-ownership market | Possible | option value on top of the DCF |

### Step 4 — the drivers

| Narrative claim | Driver | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Urban car service competing with taxis and limos, possibly expanding demand | Total market | $100 billion in 2013, growing 6% a year |
| Advantages against incumbents and newcomers, but no global network effects | Market share | 10% target share |
| Keeps 20% of payments even under competition; low-infrastructure model | Operating margin | 40% target pre-tax margin |
| Owns no cars and little infrastructure | Reinvestment | sales-to-capital 5.00 |
| Young company still proving its model | Discount rate | 12% initially, declining to 8% |
| Has cash and capital but could still fail | Failure | 10% probability |

### Step 5 — the schedule

All figures in $ millions.

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Overall market | 106,000 | 112,360 | 119,102 | 126,248 | 133,823 | 141,852 | 150,363 | 159,385 | 168,948 | 179,085 |
| Share of gross market | 3.63% | 5.22% | 6.41% | 7.31% | 7.98% | 8.49% | 8.87% | 9.15% | 9.36% | 10.00% |
| Revenue | 769 | 1,173 | 1,528 | 1,846 | 2,137 | 2,408 | 2,666 | 2,916 | 3,163 | 3,582 |
| Operating margin | 7.00% | 10.67% | 14.33% | 18.00% | 21.67% | 25.33% | 29.00% | 32.67% | 36.33% | 40.00% |
| Operating income | 54 | 125 | 219 | 332 | 463 | 610 | 773 | 953 | 1,149 | 1,433 |

Trace year 1. The market is 100,000 × 1.06 = $106,000M. A 3.63% share of gross bookings
gives $3,847M, and the 20% slice gives revenue of $769M. A 7.00% margin gives operating
income of $54M, and tax at 31% leaves $37M. Revenue rose from a base of $302M, so
reinvestment is the revenue change divided by 5.00, or $94M. FCFF is 37 − 94 = −$57M.

Negative in year 1 is the correct output, not an error. The growth is paid for inside the
same model that produces the value.

Terminal year: EBIT(1−t) of $881M, reinvestment of $88M, FCFF of $793M. Stable growth
2.50%, stable cost of capital 8%, stable return on capital 25%, so the stable reinvestment
rate is 2.5% / 25% = 10%.

```
terminal value = 793 / (0.08 - 0.025) = $14,418M
operating assets                      = $6,595M
expected value = 6,595 x 0.90         = $5,895M
```

The June 2014 funding round imputed roughly $17 billion for the same operating assets. That
is a price, set by a negotiation, not a value.

### Step 6 — the counter-narratives, valued

| | Gurley | Gurley modified | Damodaran |
|---|---|---|---|
| Total market | $300bn, growing 3%/yr | $300bn, growing 3%/yr | $100bn, growing 6%/yr |
| Market share | 40% | 40% | 10% |
| Revenue slice | 20% | 10% | 20% |
| Value | $53.4bn plus $10bn+ option value | $28.7bn plus $6bn+ option value | $5.9bn plus $2–3bn option value |

Nothing in the model changed. Three story choices moved the value about ninefold. That is
the point of valuing a counter-narrative instead of arguing with it: the disagreement is
now located in three named drivers.

The full twelve-cell narrative grid ran from $799M to $90,457M — a spread of about 113x,
driven entirely by narrative choices across four dimensions: total market definition, the
growth effect on that market, network effects, and competitive advantage.

### What the case teaches

- Argue about drivers, not values.
- A 20% revenue slice is a claim about competition, and halving it roughly halves the value.
- Local network effects and global ones are worth billions apart.
- The failure adjustment on a young private company cost 10% of value here.
- The car-ownership market belongs in option value. Putting it in the cash flows is how the
  impossible enters a spreadsheet.

## Tesla, November 2021

### Step 1 — the base year

Trailing twelve months: revenue $46,848M and EBIT $4,586M. R&D capitalized over a five-year
life, with current-year R&D of $2,375M, gives a research asset of $5,261.4M and amortization
of $1,310.6M. The EBIT adjustment is 2,375 − 1,310.6 = +$1,064.4M, so adjusted EBIT is
$5,650.4M and the base margin is 12.06%. No operating leases to convert.

Invested capital: 28,494 book equity + 10,158 book debt − 16,095 cash + 5,261.4 research
asset = $27,818.4M. Base ROIC 17.88%, sales-to-capital 1.68.

Skipping the R&D capitalization leaves EBIT at $4,586M, and both the base margin and
invested capital are then wrong.

Perspective against the industry:

| Metric | Tesla | US industry | Global industry |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue growth, most recent year | 69.50% | 14.31% | 4.97% |
| Pre-tax operating margin | 12.06% | 3.41% | 4.79% |
| Sales to capital | 1.68 | 0.87 | 1.06 |
| Return on invested capital | 17.88% | 2.89% | 4.60% |

The largest global automakers earn operating margins between 0.3% and 8.5% on low
single-digit growth. That is the reference class any margin claim argues against.

### Step 2 and 3 — the narrative and its grade

"The Payoff to Flexibility — A Plausible Path to Auto Dominance." The title states the
grade. Tesla sits at life-cycle stage 4, the scaling-up test, so the dominant question is
not whether the idea works but whether it scales. That is why the contested inputs are the
growth rate and the sales-to-capital ratio, and why the failure probability is zero.

### Step 4 — five levers and two overrides

| Driver | Value | Story link |
|---|---|---|
| Compounded revenue growth, 5 years | 35.00% | growth in the EV market plus early-mover advantage |
| Target pre-tax operating margin | 16.00% | continued economies of scale and brand |
| Year of convergence to target | 5 | — |
| Sales to capital, years 1–5 | 4.00 | capacity already built, so less reinvestment near-term |
| Sales to capital, years 6–10 | 2.67 | — |
| Initial cost of capital | 6.00% | moves to the median company's cost of capital |
| Terminal cost of capital | 6.06% | — |
| Terminal return on capital (override) | 15.00% | cost of entry will limit competition |
| Probability of failure | 0.00% | — |
| Effective / marginal tax rate | 11.99% / 25.00% | the global tax rate |

Every number has a sentence. Every sentence has a number.

Terminal reinvestment rate = 1.56% / 15.00% = 10.40%, which is where the $5,250.28M comes
from. It is computed, never chosen.

```
terminal value      = 45,233.17 / (0.0606 - 0.0156) = $1,005,181.62M
PV(terminal value)                                  =   $560,336.04M
PV(cash flows, 10 years)                            =   $126,353.86M
value of operating assets                           =   $686,689.91M
- debt 10,158 + cash 16,095                         =   $692,626.91M equity
- employee options 51,070.25                        =   $641,556.66M
/ 1,123.00 million shares                           =       $571.29 per share
```

Price was $1,200.00, or 210.05% of value.

The option value needs iteration, not a single pass. The dilution-adjusted Black-Scholes
takes a stock price input, and the model's own value per share is that input, which in turn
nets out the option value. Solved to a fixed point: S_adj = 565.585, value per option
$502.561, total $51,070.25M — worth $45.48 per share.

### Diagnostics

Invested capital rises from $27,818.4M to $145,185.4M over ten years. Marginal ROIC 51.66%.
ROIC at year 10, 34.24%. Average compounded WACC 6.02%. Value at 47.61% of price, which
trips the "value seems low" flag and prompts a recheck of inputs rather than a conclusion
about the market.

More than 80% of the value sits in the terminal value. For a growth company that is normal,
not a defect.

### What the case teaches

- $571.29 is the value of one story. Move the growth lever to a 20% auto market share, or
  the margin lever to the software median, and the answer changes by hundreds of dollars a
  share. Say so.
- A sales-to-capital of 4.00 against an auto median of 1.37 implies a marginal ROIC of
  51.66%. Argue it or lower it.
- The terminal ROC override of 15% against a 6.06% terminal cost of capital claims perpetual
  excess returns. The sentence "cost of entry will limit competition" is the entire defence,
  and leaving the override on without it grants a moat by accident.
- The five-input model is the point, not a simplification. A reader can argue with five
  numbers. Nobody can argue with fifty.
