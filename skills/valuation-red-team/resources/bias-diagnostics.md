# Bias diagnostics

Every valuation is biased. The only open questions are how much and in which direction. A
fitted model passes every consistency check, because nothing inside it contradicts anything
else. So the tests here read the process rather than the arithmetic.

The organizing question: did the conclusion precede the analysis?

## The three chronology tests

**The chronology test.** Compare the order in which the artifacts were produced against the
order the pipeline requires. Narrative before drivers. Drivers before forecast. Forecast
before value. Price comparison last. A narrative that already contains the answer, or a
driver set written before any benchmarking, is a rationalization rather than a forecast.

In a deal context this test is sharper. A valuation dated after the price was discussed is
a defence of the price. Reconstruct the sequence from the artifacts, and say what the
sequence shows.

**The sensitivity test.** Ask which input was flexed to reach the conclusion. Compare each
driver against its sector benchmark and record the direction of every deviation. Individual
deviations are normal. A set of deviations that all point the same way is the signature of
a fitted model.

Useful sharpening: run the implied solve from A7 on the two drivers that carry the story.
If the base case lands within a couple of percent of the market price on a company the
write-up calls badly mispriced, something was tuned.

**The accountability test.** Name the person whose position depends on this number being
right. In an acquisition, name whoever is accountable for the promised benefits arriving.
Diffuse responsibility predicts no delivery. In an investment mandate, the equivalent
question is who already holds a public position on the name.

## The declared-bias block

The playbook asks for a bias audit before the model opens. Check that it happened. Four
items should be recorded somewhere in the prose artifacts.

| Item | Why it predicts direction |
|---|---|
| who pays for the valuation, and how much | fee structure sets the wanted answer |
| what answer they want | states the pull explicitly |
| any public position already taken on the company | a published view is defended, not revised |
| whether the market price was seen before the qualitative work | price anchors every driver that follows |

None of these is disqualifying. An undeclared one is a `medium` finding, because the reader
cannot correct for a pull nobody named.

## The three sides of the triangle

Valuations get lost three ways. Diagnose which side this one drifted toward, because the
repair differs.

| Side | Symptoms in the artifacts | Repair to suggest |
|---|---|---|
| Bias and preconceptions | a defended number, one-sided deviations, a price cited early | declare the priors, rebuild from the story forward |
| Complexity and detail | rows nobody can explain, many inputs with no story sentence | cut to the story levers; understanding falls as input count rises |
| Uncertainty and the unknown | no estimate at all, a range with no chosen cell, consensus copied | estimate anyway, then size the uncertainty with scenarios or simulation |

The complexity side has a specific tell worth checking. Count the model inputs that carry a
story sentence, and count the story claims that move an input. Both counts should be zero
when subtracted from their totals. Deliberately small driver sets are a strength, not a
shortcut: a reader can argue with five numbers and cannot argue with fifty.

## The runaway-story screen

Some stories stop the questions. Three ingredients make that happen: a charismatic and
likeable narrator, a disliked incumbent being disrupted, and a claimed societal benefit.
Two or three present means the analysis needed extra discipline, and you should check
whether it got any.

The matching meltdown pattern has three ingredients too: a narrator whose record makes them
untrustworthy, a story at war with the company's own reported numbers, and a business-model
flaw that management denies. Two of the three present is a reason to mark the story down
before the market does.

The defence here is not scepticism as a personality trait. It is asking the first-principles
question the story discourages. Not "will they execute?" but "is this even possible?"

Watch for a governance check that is only cosmetic. A prestigious board with no domain
expertise in the core claim supplies the appearance of a check and none of the substance.

## The unbiasedness test

This one runs across valuations rather than inside one. Over a record of estimates, upward
and downward revisions should be roughly symmetric. A one-sided revision record is evidence
of a biased process rather than of bad luck.

It is a `low` finding by nature, because it says nothing about this valuation in
particular. Record it when the history is available, and note that revising on price moves
rather than on business news is herding with extra steps.

## The tells that a valuation drifted to the dark side

These phrases appear when the information runs out and the story takes over. Each is worth
a sentence in `challenge.md` when you find it.

- A declared paradigm shift, or an argument that the old rules no longer apply.
- A newly invented metric with no link to cash flows.
- "Conservative" used as a justification, with no number attached to the conservatism.
- A discounted cash flow model invoked as a credential rather than shown as inputs.
- Earnings accretion offered as evidence of value.
- A point estimate quoted to the cent, which promises a precision no valuation has.
- A large gap read as proof the market is wrong, before the analyst's own inputs were
  rechecked.
