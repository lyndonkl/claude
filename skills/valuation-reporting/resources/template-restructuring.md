# Template: `restructuring` mode

The question is what this company is worth run differently. The terminal artifact is the
status quo value, the optimal value, and the value of control between them.

The company is valued twice on the same cash-flow engine. A single valuation on current
policy values a company that may not exist next year. A single valuation on optimal policy
values a company that does not exist yet. The report carries both, plus the probability
that the change actually happens.

The rule that governs the whole report: never quote the optimally-run value as the value
without a probability attached.

## Sections in order

### 1. Verdict

- The recommendation and who it is addressed to: an activist, an incumbent board, a
  minority holder, or a potential acquirer.
- Three numbers: status quo value per share, optimal value per share, expected value per
  share.
- The value of control, in total and per share, and as a percent of the current price.
- The probability of change used, and where it came from.
- The market-implied probability, beside it.
- The two or three changes that produce most of the gain.

### 2. Mandate and standing

Currency, valuation date, and the position the reader holds or contemplates. This matters
here more than in other modes. Control value is realizable only by someone who can force
the change, so a minority holder and a potential acquirer get different recommendations
from the same numbers.

### 3. Status quo valuation

The full discounted cash flow on current policy: actual reinvestment rate, actual return on
capital, actual debt ratio, actual payout. The bridge and the value per share.

Compare with the market price here, before any restructuring is discussed. The market is
usually pricing something between the status quo and the optimum, and the reader needs the
lower anchor first.

### 4. The diagnosis

Three questions, answered with numbers rather than adjectives.

- **Does it invest badly?** Return on capital against cost of capital, and the reinvestment
  rate against the sector. A firm earning below its cost of capital and reinvesting heavily
  is destroying value faster than a firm that does neither.
- **Does it earn too little on what it has?** Operating margin and capital turnover against
  sector benchmarks, which together decompose the return on capital.
- **Is it financed wrongly?** Current debt ratio against the optimum from the cost of
  capital schedule.

Benchmark each gap against the sector and, where this is a target, against the acquirer.
Rank the three by the value at stake, because that ranking is the recommendation.

### 5. Restructured valuation

The same model with the changes in place. Itemize every changed lever and the value each
one contributes. Four levers, and every change must land on exactly one of them.

| Lever | What changes |
|---|---|
| Cash flows from existing assets | margin, tax rate, capital discipline, working capital, divesting sub-hurdle assets |
| Expected growth | reinvestment rate and return on new capital |
| Length of the growth period | only where a named competitive advantage justifies it |
| Cost of capital | move to the optimal debt ratio, reduce operating leverage, match debt to assets |

Two disciplines the report must show. Raising the reinvestment rate while the return on
capital sits below the cost of capital accelerates value destruction; for such firms the
value-creating move is usually to shrink and return capital. And a return improvement on
existing assets enters as a transition term spread over a stated number of years, never as
a perpetual source.

Extending the growth period is the most common way to inflate a restructured value. If the
report extends it, it must name the competitive advantage: brand, legal protection,
switching costs, or a cost advantage.

The discount rate may differ between the two runs only through the deliberate capital
structure change. Any other difference is an error.

### 6. Value of control

The difference between the two valuations, in total and per share.

Then the delay discount. If the changes take three years to land, the gain is worth what it
is worth three years out, discounted back. State the number of years and the rate. A
turnaround that takes a decade is worth a fraction of the undiscounted gap.

State that a perfectly run firm has a control premium of zero. Where the analysis finds
little control value, that is a finding, not a failed analysis.

### 7. Probability of change and expected value

Your own estimate, built from evidence. The structural evidence is the ownership and voting
structure, the takeover defenses, the board composition, any activist on the register,
access to challenge funding, and firm size. The empirical turnover markers sit beside it:
underperformance, a small outsider-dominated board, high institutional and low insider
holdings, and a competitive industry.

Then the expected value: status quo weighted by one minus the probability, plus optimal
weighted by the probability.

Then the market's own odds, computed from the current price against the two valuations.
Compare the two probabilities and explain the difference. A market-implied probability at
or beyond the ends of the range means an input is wrong. Investigate before acting on it,
and say in the report what you found.

### 8. Allocation across claims

Where the share structure is not uniform, control value does not accrue evenly.

- Dual-class structures: the control value attaches to the voting class. Report the voting
  premium.
- Stakes: a controlling stake is priced off the optimal value, a minority stake off the
  status quo. Say which applies to this reader.

### 9. Implementation

The sequence of changes, the years each takes, and who has to act. Then the obstacles from
the governance work: staggered boards, takeover defenses, a majority holder, covenant
restrictions on payout or asset sales.

An excellent restructuring plan that management can ignore indefinitely is worth what the
probability of change says it is worth, and this section is where the reader sees why the
probability is what it is.

## What the reader will check

- The optimal value is never quoted alone.
- The control premium came from the two runs, not from a rule-of-thumb percentage.
- Margin improvement is backed by evidence of commitment and explicit targets, not assumed.
- The reinvestment rate was not raised while returns sit below the cost of capital.
- The value gain is discounted for implementation delay.
- Only the deliberate capital structure change moves the discount rate between runs.
