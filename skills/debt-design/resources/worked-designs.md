# Worked designs: profile, recommendation, gap

Six firms, each showing a different part of the method. Read them for the shape of the
reasoning, not for the numbers, which are dated snapshots.

## The intuitive route, business by business

A diversified entertainment firm, profiled without a single regression:

| Business | Project cash flow characteristics | Financing |
|---|---|---|
| Studio entertainment | Short-lived film projects; outflows mostly in dollars, inflows with a large foreign component; net cash flow driven by whether a film is a hit, which is close to unforecastable | Short-term, mixed-currency debt, ideally tied to film performance |
| Media networks | Short projects; primarily dollars, with a growing foreign component; driven by advertising and ratings | Short-term, primarily dollar debt, ideally linked to ratings |
| Park resorts | Very long-lived projects; currency depends on the region; affected by the studio and network businesses | Long-term, currencies mixed by the tourist make-up of each park |
| Consumer products | Short to medium term, tied to the film division because licensing derives from film | Medium-term dollar debt |
| Interactive | Short-term, high growth potential, significant risk; dollar cash flows at first, shifting as the business matures | Short-term, convertible, dollar debt |

Three things make this route work. Each line names a project length, a currency and a
driver. The interdependence between businesses is stated rather than ignored. And the
financing column follows mechanically from the characteristics column.

## Recommendation against actual: the gap table

Same firm, 2013. Recommended duration about 4.3 years, a significant floating-rate share
given demonstrated pricing power, and roughly 18% of debt in foreign currencies based on
revenue geography.

| Dimension | Recommended | Actual | Verdict |
|---|---|---|---|
| Duration / maturity | ~4.3 years duration | 7.92 years weighted-average maturity | Slightly too long, once the maturity-duration wedge is allowed for |
| Foreign currency | ~18%, including about 12% euro | 5.49%, in rupees and Hong Kong dollars, no euro | Too little, and in the wrong currencies |
| Floating rate | Significant | 5.67% | Too little given pricing power |
| Convertible | None | None | Matched |

Closure: swap fixed-rate dollar debt into floating-rate foreign-currency debt, which is
cheap for a firm of this standing, and issue new debt in that form.

Note the honest reading of the maturity gap. A 7.92-year maturity against a 4.3-year
duration is not a 3.6-year mismatch, because maturity always exceeds duration. Part of the
gap is arithmetic and part is a real mismatch. The long maturity may also reflect a
deliberate decision to lock in low long-term rates, which is a mispricing judgment rather
than a matching error. Name both possibilities.

## Four short cases

**Single-location retailer.** Revenues depend on one store in one city, in one currency,
with steady demand. Recommendation: long-term, dollar-denominated, fixed-rate debt. Actual:
a long-term operating lease on the store. That lease is debt, and it is a good match. The
finding is that the firm is better designed than its balance sheet suggests.

**Global miner.** Mines with very long lives and large up-front investment, 37% of revenues
from one country, costs in local currencies and revenues in dollars. Recommendation:
long-term dollar debt, local-currency cost exposure hedged, and payments tied to commodity
prices where the structure can be arranged. Actual: 65% dollar debt, 14.7-year average
maturity, entirely fixed rate, no commodity linkage. Duration and currency match well. The
missing piece is the commodity link, which is exactly the feature that would keep the firm
solvent through a price trough.

**Diversified manufacturer.** Roughly 24% of revenues each from two large emerging markets,
the rest spread across developed markets. Recommendation: medium- to long-term fixed-rate
debt in a currency mix matching operations. Actual: about 71% domestic currency and 29%
euro, 5.33-year average maturity, almost entirely fixed. Maturity and rate type match; the
currency mix ignores half the revenue base.

**Young technology firm.** Little debt, high growth, cash flows in the home currency.
Recommendation: convertible debt in the home currency. Actual: 82% of debt in dollars and
euros, 5.80-year average maturity, a small floating portion, almost no convertible. A poor
match on every dimension, which is the common outcome for young firms that borrow where the
market is deepest rather than where their revenues are.

## When the historical route fails: a four-firm study

A graded project team ran firm-level macro regressions on four restaurant and food firms.
Almost nothing was significant — one operating-income-versus-GDP relationship across the
whole set. They fell back on bottom-up sector coefficients.

| Factor | Eating and Drinking Places | Food and Kindred Products |
|---|---|---|
| Duration | 7.55 | 4.82 |
| Cyclicality | 0.59 | 0.79 |
| Inflation | 0.28 | 0.06 |
| Currency | −1.77 | −0.05 |

Reading: restaurants support roughly seven to eight year duration debt, food manufacturing
about 4.8 years. Both show low cyclicality. Restaurants carry meaningful currency exposure;
food manufacturers essentially none.

The four recommendations that followed:

- **Large coffee retailer.** Long-lived store expansion with steady earnings, so six to
  eight year duration. Expansion targets new markets, so borrow in the currencies where
  that revenue will arise. Pricing power supports a larger floating-rate share.
- **Large burger chain.** Similar duration and the same currency logic, but expansion is
  gradual because it is already present in most emerging markets. Size and stable earnings
  leave room to borrow with little rating risk.
- **Fast-growing chain.** A rapid expansion agenda argues for convertible debt, in the
  currencies of its target markets. High same-store-sales growth allows a slightly shorter
  duration. Little rating risk.
- **Meat processor.** Long-term domestic-currency debt is ideal, but the move should be
  gradual given acquisition risk and rating sensitivity. It sells to a handful of powerful
  retailers with little pricing flexibility, so it should hold a greater share of
  fixed-rate debt.

That last recommendation is the most instructive. Two firms in adjacent sectors get
opposite fixed-floating advice, and the reason is customer bargaining power rather than
anything in the financing markets.

## The deliverable sentence

Write each recommendation as one sentence naming maturity, currency, rate type and
features. Then attach the gap table and the closure instruments.

> Six to eight year duration, in a currency mix matching where the new stores will earn
> revenue, with a large floating-rate share given demonstrated pricing power, and no
> convertible component.

Against that, "improve the match between debt and assets" commits to nothing and cannot be
audited a year later.
