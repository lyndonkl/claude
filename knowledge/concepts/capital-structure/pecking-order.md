# The pecking order and what firms actually do

**Core idea:** Firms do not behave as if they optimize a debt ratio. They follow a financing hierarchy: retained earnings first, then debt, and new equity last. Two managerial preferences drive it — flexibility (using capital without restrictions and without explaining its use) and control (new equity dilutes control and invites the most scrutiny). Survey rankings, time-series data on US financing, and CFO priorities all line up with this hierarchy. The practical consequences are two. First, a firm's observed debt ratio may reflect its history of internal cash generation rather than any target. Second, the *choice* of security carries information: a firm reaching for the bottom of the hierarchy is signalling that better options are closed to it.

**Formulas:** None. The concept is behavioral, but it interacts with the arithmetic elsewhere: financing that comes from retained earnings lowers the debt ratio passively over time, which is why gradual adjustment plans have to be projected against a moving firm value ([[moving-to-the-optimal]]).

**Procedure:**
1. Before diagnosing a firm as under- or over-levered, ask how it got where it is. A firm that has funded itself from retained earnings for a decade will look under-levered without ever having decided to be.
2. Rank the firm's realistic financing options in pecking-order sequence and check which rungs are actually available to it.
3. Read new issues as signals. An issue near the bottom of the hierarchy (convertible preferred, for instance) suggests that retained earnings, straight debt and even common equity were unavailable or unattractive — a negative signal about financial health.
4. When recommending a move to the optimal, expect resistance grounded in flexibility and control, not in valuation. The CFO survey ranks "maintain financial flexibility" and "ensure long-term survival" (4.55 each) above "maximize stock price" (3.99).
5. Use the pecking order as a constraint on the *path*, not on the *target*. The target still comes from [[cost-of-capital-approach]] or [[apv-approach]].

**Reference data:** Manager preference rankings for long-term financing sources (survey scores):

| Ranking | Source | Score |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Retained earnings | 5.61 |
| 2 | Straight debt | 4.88 |
| 3 | Convertible debt | 3.02 |
| 4 | External common equity | 2.42 |
| 5 | Straight preferred stock | 2.22 |
| 6 | Convertible preferred | 1.72 |

External and internal financing at US firms, 1975–2018: internal financing dominated every single year, typically 60–85% of the total and roughly 75–80% in recent years. External debt supplied most of the remainder (roughly 15–30%). Common and preferred stock issuance was consistently the smallest slice, typically under 10%.

**Worked example:** You see a tombstone advertisement for a company selling convertible preferred stock. Convertible preferred sits last in the preference ranking (score 1.72). Under the pecking order, a healthy firm would have used retained earnings, then straight debt, then even common equity first. So the issue implies those rungs were closed. The inference is that the company is in more financial trouble than the average firm — not less.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: computing the internal-vs-external financing split for a firm or market from cash flow statements.
- JUDGMENT: reading a security issue as a signal, and deciding how much weight to give managerial preferences against the value-maximizing target.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating the pecking order as normative. It explains behavior; it does not prove that behavior maximizes value.
- Confusing "firms rarely issue equity" with "equity is expensive." The reluctance is about control and scrutiny.
- Ignoring that the pecking order and the flexibility argument are the same instinct wearing different clothes — do not count them as two independent reasons to avoid debt.
