# Value of control and merger synergy

**Core idea:** A stock can be mispriced because the firm is badly run, and that gap is itself valuable. The value of control is the difference between the firm run optimally and the firm run as it is. It is computed by running the DCF twice — once status quo, once with the value-enhancing change in place — and taking the difference. Synergy is the parallel idea for a combination: value the two firms standalone, value the merged firm with the synergy assumptions, and take the difference. Together they decompose the total value of a firm into three layers: status quo value, control value, and synergy value. The layers matter in acquisitions, because what an acquirer can pay is the whole stack while what the market prices is usually only the first layer.

**Formulas:**
- Value of Control = Value of the firm optimally managed − Value of the firm under current management.
- Value of Synergy = Value of the combined firm with synergies − (Value of firm A standalone + Value of firm B standalone).
- Total value of the firm = Status quo value + Value of control + Value of synergy.
- Combined unlevered beta = value-weighted average of the two firms' unlevered betas, weights = firm values. Then relever at the combined D/E to get the merged firm's levered beta, cost of equity and WACC.

**Procedure:**
1. **Value the firm status quo.** Full DCF with current management's policies — current capital structure, current ROC, current growth.
2. **Identify the value-enhancing change.** Common candidates: an under-levered balance sheet, sub-par returns on capital, excess cash, or a growth rate that better management could lift.
3. **Re-run the DCF with the change.** For a leverage change, recompute the levered beta at the new debt ratio, the new cost of equity, and the new WACC, then rediscount the same cash flow stream.
4. **Take the difference.** That is the value of control. Note it accrues to whoever gains control — incumbent management could capture it too, simply by acting.
5. **For a merger, value the target and acquirer standalone** on consistent assumptions.
6. **Set the synergy assumptions explicitly** — which growth rates change, which returns improve, and by how much — and say when they take effect.
7. **Build the combined firm's discount rate**: value-weight the unlevered betas, relever at the combined D/E, compute the cost of equity and WACC.
8. **Value the combined firm** and subtract the sum of the standalones. That is the operating synergy.
9. **State that the split is negotiated.** How the added value divides between acquirer and target shareholders is not a valuation output.
10. **Assemble the decomposition table**: status quo, control, optimally managed, synergy, total.

**Worked example:** Infosys carried no debt at all. Adding a modest 11.5% debt ratio raises beta from 2.09 to 2.25 and the cost of equity from 13.86% to 14.48%, but the cheaper after-tax debt pulls WACC down from 13.86% to 13.29%. Rediscounting the identical FCFF stream at the lower rate lifts firm value from Rs 27,727 crore to Rs 33,963 crore — from $6,027.61m to $7,383.26m. The value of control is $1,355.65m, and either incumbent or new management could capture it simply by changing the capital structure. The merger case then proposes ACS as a partner: similar businesses, with ACS able to adopt Infosys's off-shoring cost advantages. The synergy assumptions are that ACS's ROC rises from 8% to 15%, the combined firm grows at 15% for five years (an increase for ACS, a slight decrease for Infosys), and stable growth reaches 5% (an increase for ACS). The synergy is worth $299m — small next to the $1,355m of control value, which is the more important finding.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC — both DCF runs, the value of control as their difference, the value-weighted combined unlevered beta, the relevered beta and combined WACC, the combined-firm DCF, the synergy as a difference, and the decomposition table. All follow exactly from the stated inputs. JUDGMENT — identifying which change constitutes "optimal management," the target debt ratio, whether the synergies are real, how large the ROC and growth improvements are, when they take effect, and how the gains split between the parties.

**Pitfalls:**
- Adding control value to a valuation used for a minority-stake buy/sell call. Control value is realisable only by whoever can force the change.
- Computing synergy against inconsistently-built standalone values. Note that the ACS standalone here uses the *optimally-levered* 11.5% D/(D+E) assumption for both firms, so the comparison is like-for-like.
- Simple-averaging the two firms' betas instead of value-weighting the unlevered betas and relevering.
- Treating the synergy number as the acquirer's gain. The split is negotiated, and an acquirer that pays the full synergy value in the premium gains nothing.
- Claiming control value for a change that raises the discount rate's numerator and denominator equally. Infosys's control value comes from a genuine WACC reduction with the cash flows held fixed.
- Overstating operating synergies. Here they are worth $299m against a $12,729m combined base — roughly 2%.
