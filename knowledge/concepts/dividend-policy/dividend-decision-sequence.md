# The dividend decision sequence (equity as a residual claim)

**Core idea:** In the first-principles framework of corporate finance, the dividend decision comes third and last. Maximize the value of the business through three decisions: **invest** in assets that earn more than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate; **finance** with the right kind of debt and the right mix of debt and equity; and if you cannot find investments that clear the hurdle rate, **return the cash** to the owners. How much cash you can return depends on current and potential investment opportunities. How you return it — dividends or buybacks — depends on what stockholders prefer. The framing principle: "Companies don't have cash. They hold cash for their stockholders." Treating equity as a residual claim turns this into a waterfall in which the dividend is what is left over, not a starting commitment.

**Formulas:**
- Cash flow from operations = After-tax Operating Income + Depreciation
- Potential Dividends (FCFE) = Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash Working Capital − (Debt Repaid − Debt Raised)
  - Net Income = earnings to equity holders after interest and taxes
  - Cap Ex = capital expenditures, the long-term reinvestment
  - Change in non-cash Working Capital = the short-term reinvestment
  - Debt Repaid / Debt Raised = principal cash flows to and from lenders
- Cash paid out = Dividends + Stock Buybacks, split according to stockholder preference
- Cash retained = FCFE − Cash paid out. This accumulates on the balance sheet as cash and marketable securities.

**Procedure:**
1. Start with cash flow from operations = after-tax operating income + depreciation.
2. Pay the cash flows owed to debt: interest expense and principal repaid, net of new borrowing. The guiding question is "how much did you borrow?" What remains is cash flow from operations to equity investors.
3. Subtract reinvestment back into the business: capital expenditure on long-term assets and the change in non-cash working capital on short-term assets. The guiding question is "how good are your investment choices?" What remains is **potential dividends (FCFE)**.
4. Decide how much cash to hold back. The guiding question is "what is a reasonable cash balance?" The answer depends on current and potential investment opportunities, and on whether management has earned the right to hold cash ([[cash-trust-assessment]]).
5. Pay out the rest, splitting between dividends and stock buybacks according to what your stockholders prefer. The guiding question is "what do your stockholders prefer?" ([[clientele-effect]], [[cash-returned-dividends-and-buybacks]]).
6. Compare what this sequence produces with what the firm actually does. The gap is the subject of the whole assessment toolkit in this area.

**Reference data — the waterfall:**

| Step | Item | Question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Cash flow from operations = After-tax operating income + Depreciation | How much does the business generate? |
| 2 | − Principal repaid, − Interest expenses | How much did you borrow? |
| 3 | = Cash flow from operations to equity investors | — |
| 4 | − Cap ex (long-term assets), − Change in working capital (short-term assets) | How good are your investment choices? |
| 5 | = **Potential Dividends (FCFE)** | — |
| 6 | − Cash retained | What is a reasonable cash balance? |
| 7 | = Cash paid out, split between stock buybacks and dividends | What do your stockholders prefer? |

*Why the sequence is not what firms actually do.* Dividend policy in practice is dysfunctional. It does not begin with investment options and work down to a residual. Two forces dominate worldwide: **inertia** — companies hate to let go of their past dividend behavior — and **me-too-ism** — companies want to behave like their peer group. Every assessment tool in this area exists because of that gap.

**Worked example:** Microsoft, 1996. Net Income $2,176M; Cap Ex $494M; Depreciation $480M; Change in non-cash Working Capital $35M; no debt.
FCFE = 2,176 − (494 − 480) − 35 − 0 = **$2,127M** of potential dividends.
Microsoft paid nothing. The sequence stopped at step 6 with a 100% holdback, so the entire $2,127M accumulated as cash and marketable securities on the balance sheet. Repeat annually and you get the $43 billion cash balance Microsoft held by 2002 — and the $33 billion special dividend it announced in 2004 when it finally released the holdback.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: steps 1 through 3. Given net income, cap ex, depreciation, change in non-cash working capital and debt raised or repaid, FCFE is a mechanical calculation. So is the cash retained, once the payout is known.
- JUDGMENT: steps 4 and 5. Setting a reasonable cash balance requires assessing future investment opportunities and management quality. Splitting the payout between dividends and buybacks requires knowing the stockholder clientele's tax status and preferences.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating the dividend as a fixed commitment and the investment budget as the residual. The framework runs the other way.
- Skipping step 2 or double counting it. Interest is already deducted in net income; only principal flows are added separately in the FCFE formula.
- Assuming a zero cash holdback is optimal. A firm with real investment opportunities should hold cash, provided it has earned the trust to do so.
- Applying the sequence to a bank, where reinvestment means regulatory capital ([[fcfe-for-banks]]).
- Reporting FCFE as a recommendation. It is the *potential* dividend; whether it should actually be paid is decided in [[dividend-matrix]].
