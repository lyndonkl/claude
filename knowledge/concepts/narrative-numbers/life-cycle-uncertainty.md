# A life-cycle view of uncertainty

**Core idea:** Uncertainty does not just vary in size across companies. It changes in *kind* as a company ages. At each stage of the corporate life cycle there is one dominant question, and the honest valuation answers that question rather than pretending to answer all of them. At the very start and the very end of the life cycle the uncertainty is company-specific: does the idea work, and will management face reality? In the middle stages, macro worries dominate, because the business model exists and the open question is how the economy treats it. Knowing which stage you are in tells you which inputs deserve your effort and which uncertainty tool fits.

**Formulas:** None. The mapping is the content.

**Procedure:**
1. **Place the company on the life-cycle curve.** Use revenue level, revenue growth, margin sign and stability, and reinvestment intensity. The transition points Damodaran names are the Lightbulb/Idea Moment, From Idea to Business, the Bar Mitzvah, the Scaling-up Test, the Midlife Crisis and the End Game.
2. **Name the dominant question for that stage** from the table below, and make it the headline of your narrative.
3. **Decide whether your uncertainty is company-specific or macro.** Stage 1 and stage 6 are company-specific. Stages 2 to 5 are dominated by macro worries.
4. **Pick the matching tool.** Company-specific and binary — does the idea work at all — points toward a probability of failure and option value. Macro and continuous — how big does this get — points toward scenarios and simulations. See [[monte-carlo-valuation-simulation]] and [[narrative-scenario-grids]].
5. **Set the value drivers accordingly.** Early-stage: total market and survival probability dominate. Middle stages: market share, margin and reinvestment efficiency dominate. Late stage: whether management divests losing assets dominates.
6. **Expect the estimate to be imprecise where the stage is early,** and present a range. That is where the payoff to valuing at all is largest. See [[valuation-misconceptions]].

**Reference data:** Uncertainty by growth stage:

| Growth stage | Uncertainty about | Type and magnitude |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Start-up | Does the idea have potential? | Company specific |
| Stage 2: Young growth | Is there a business model to commercialise the idea? | Macro worries |
| Stage 3: High growth | Will the business model generate profits? | Macro worries |
| Stage 4: Mature growth | Can the business be scaled up? | Macro worries |
| Stage 5: Mature stable | Can the business be defended? | Macro worries |
| Stage 6: Decline | Will management face reality? | Company specific |

The same course cuts the problem a second way, by where the difficulty comes from.
- Across the life cycle: young growth firms have little history, small revenues, big operating losses and a high failure rate. Mature companies in transition force you to abandon history and re-estimate the parameters. Declining and distressed firms have long but irrelevant history, shrinking markets and heavy debt.
- Across markets: emerging-market companies bring structure, country-risk exposure and weak governance.
- Across sectors: financial service firms have opaque statements. Commodity and cyclical firms are hostage to prices and the cycle. Intangible-heavy firms break the accounting.

Failure risk is the concrete expression of stage-1 uncertainty. Long-run business survival rates by sector, US Bureau of Labor Statistics Business Employment Dynamics, 1994–2019 cohorts:

| Sector | Long-run survival rate |
|---|---|
| Management of companies and enterprises | 35.2% |
| Utilities | 28.4% |
| Health care and social assistance | 19.9% |
| Wholesale trade | 14.0% |
| Transportation and warehousing | 12.9% |
| Information | 11.5% |
| All-sector average | 16.6% |

**Worked example:** Tesla in November 2021 sits at stage 4, the scaling-up test. The dominant question is not "does the idea work" — it clearly does — but "can this be scaled up?" That is why the valuation's contested inputs are the 35% revenue growth rate and the 4.00 sales-to-capital ratio, and why the probability of failure is set to 0%. Contrast Uber in June 2014, at stage 2 to 3: the business model existed but its profitability was unproven, so the valuation carried a 10% probability of failure and a cost of capital of 12% falling to 8%. Same toolkit, different dominant uncertainty, different inputs given weight.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: nothing. The survival table is a lookup, and applying a survival rate to a value is arithmetic once the rate is chosen.
- JUDGMENT: placing the company on the curve, which needs revenue level, growth, margin trajectory, reinvestment intensity and age. Also choosing a failure probability, which the sector survival data anchors but does not settle.

**Pitfalls:**
- Applying a mature-company template to a stage-1 or stage-2 company, which produces false precision on inputs nobody can know.
- Ignoring stage-6 uncertainty. In decline the question is behavioural: will management divest assets earning below the cost of capital, or keep expanding and destroying value?
- Assuming uncertainty is always company-specific. In the middle stages the macro environment dominates.
- Using an all-sector average survival rate when sector rates range from 11.5% to 35.2%.
- Treating a stage as permanent. Companies move, and the narrative has to move with them. See [[narrative-updating-feedback-loop]].
