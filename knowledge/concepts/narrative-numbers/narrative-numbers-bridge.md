# Valuation as a bridge between narrative and numbers

**Core idea:** A good valuation is a bridge between two tribes that mostly talk past each other. **Number crunchers** work from accounting statements, spreadsheets, statistical measures and pricing data. Their three delusions are precision (the data is precise), objectivity (the data has no bias) and control (the data can control reality). **Story tellers** work from anecdotes, personal or borrowed experience, and behavioural evidence. Their three delusions are that creativity cannot be quantified, that a good story means a good investment, and that experience is the best teacher. Neither tribe is right alone. The working rule for a healthy valuation: every number in the model must be backed by a piece of story, and every piece of story must show up as a number.

**Formulas:** None. The bridge rule is a mapping constraint:
- For each narrative claim there is exactly one value driver it changes (market size, market share, margin, reinvestment efficiency, risk, or survival probability).
- For each input in the model there is a sentence of story that justifies it.

**Procedure:**
1. **Write the story first, in prose.** No spreadsheet open. See [[landscape-survey]].
2. **List every model input.** For each one, write the sentence of story behind it. An input with no sentence is either unnecessary or unexamined.
3. **List every story claim.** For each, name the driver it moves. A claim that moves no driver is decoration and should be cut, or it belongs in the option layer. See [[possible-plausible-probable]].
4. **Check the model for numbers-tribe delusions.** Are you quoting value to the cent? Are you treating an accounting figure as neutral fact? Have you cleaned the base year for R&D and leases before believing it?
5. **Check the story for story-tribe delusions.** Would you buy this at any price? If your answer does not depend on price, you have a story, not a valuation.
6. **Present both sides together.** Damodaran's one-page format puts the narrative at the top, the driver assumptions in the middle with a "link to story" note per row, and the value bridge at the bottom. That layout enforces the discipline.

**Reference data:**

| | Number crunchers | Story tellers |
|---|---|---|
| Tools | Accounting statements, Excel spreadsheets, statistical measures, pricing data | Anecdotes, experience (own or others'), behavioural evidence |
| Delusion 1 | Precision: the data is precise | Creativity cannot be quantified |
| Delusion 2 | Objectivity: the data has no bias | If the story is good, the investment will be good |
| Delusion 3 | Control: the data can control reality | Experience is the best teacher |

**Worked example:** Tesla, November 2021, one-page valuation. The narrative is "The Payoff to Flexibility — A Plausible Path to Auto Dominance." Each assumption row carries its story link. Revenue growth of 35% ← growth in the EV market and Tesla's early-mover advantage. Target margin of 16% ← economies of scale and brand. Tax rate rising to 25% ← the global tax rate. Sales-to-capital of 4.00 ← capacity already built, so less reinvestment in the near years. Terminal return on capital of 15% ← cost of entry limits competition. Cost of capital of 6.00% ← it moves to the median company's cost of capital. Every number has a sentence; every sentence has a number.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: nothing values anything here. The completeness check is mechanical — count inputs without a story link and story claims without a driver; both counts should be zero.
- JUDGMENT: writing the narrative; deciding which driver a claim belongs to; deciding whether a claim is quantifiable at all or belongs in option value.

**Pitfalls:**
- Numbers with no story: inputs inherited from a template or from last year's model.
- Story with no numbers: a compelling thesis that never constrains an input, so it can never be wrong.
- Believing that cleaned-up accounting data is objective. It is an estimate too.
- Confusing a good company with a good investment. The price still has to be wrong.
- Letting experience substitute for evidence, which is how a runaway story survives. See [[runaway-stories]].
