# Runaway stories and how they melt down

**Core idea:** A runaway story is one you want to be true, so you stop asking questions. Three ingredients make it run. First, a charismatic and likeable narrator you want to see succeed. Second, a target: disrupting a business whose status quo everybody dislikes. Third, a societal benefit as a bonus, so that if the story holds, humanity wins. Once all three are present, the questions stop, because the answers might put the story at risk. Runaway stories also end in a predictable way. An untrustworthy storyteller, plus a story at war with its own numbers, plus a bad business model, equals a meltdown. The defence is process, not scepticism as a personality trait: keep asking the questions the story discourages.

**Formulas:** Two stated equations, both qualitative.

Runaway story = Charismatic narrator + Disliked status quo being disrupted + Claimed societal benefit.

Meltdown story = Untrustworthy storyteller + Story at war with the numbers + Bad business model.
- Untrustworthy storyteller: a narrator whose words or actions have made them untrustworthy.
- Story at war with numbers: the company's narrative conflicts with its own actions and its reported results.
- Bad business model: a fundamental flaw threatens profitability or survival, and management denies it or is opaque about fixing it.
- Result: investors, lenders and observers question the story, refuse the company's spin on the numbers, and push the price down.

**Procedure:**
1. **Score the three runaway ingredients.** Is the narrator charismatic and personally likeable? Is the target a disliked incumbent industry? Is there a societal-benefit claim? Two or three yeses means you are in the danger zone and need extra discipline.
2. **Write down the questions you have not asked.** The ones whose answers would hurt. Then ask them.
3. **Test the core claim against first principles.** Not "will they execute?" but "is this even possible?" That is the check the Theranos board never applied.
4. **Audit the governance.** Prestige is not expertise. Count board members with real domain knowledge of the core technical claim, not the average résumé quality.
5. **Compare the story to the numbers the company itself reports.** Any conflict between what management says and what the filings show is the second meltdown ingredient.
6. **Track the narrator's record.** Broken promises and evasive answers are the first ingredient.
7. **Look for a business-model flaw plus denial.** A flaw management acknowledges and is fixing is a risk. A flaw management denies is the third ingredient.
8. **If two of the three meltdown ingredients are present, mark down the story before the market does.** Treat the narrative as at risk of a break, not merely a shift. See [[narrative-updating-feedback-loop]].

**Reference data:** Theranos, October 2015. The company was valued at $9 billion, one of about 125 VC-backed companies then valued at $1 billion or more. The story was Elizabeth Holmes and the nanotainer — a drop of blood replacing a full draw. The "check" was the board:

**Worked example:** Theranos scored on both equations. Runaway ingredients: a charismatic founder cast as a role model; a disliked status quo (invasive, expensive blood testing); a clear societal benefit (cheap diagnostics for everyone). All three present. Then came the meltdown ingredients, once journalists began asking. The storyteller's claims did not survive scrutiny. The story was at war with the numbers and with the company's own lab results. The core technology could not do what was claimed, and management met the doubts with opacity. The valuation lesson is that no discount rate saves you here. The claim was in the *impossible* class, and the only defence was refusing to let the story stop the questions.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: nothing. Both equations are checklists.
- JUDGMENT: all of it. Scoring the ingredients needs the narrator's public record, the company's filings and disclosures, independent technical assessment of the core claim, and the board's actual domain expertise.

**Pitfalls:**
- Mistaking a prestigious board for governance.
- Treating charisma or mission as evidence.
- Confusing "I want this to be true" with "this is probable". See [[possible-plausible-probable]].
- Waiting for the price to fall before marking down the story. By then the meltdown is public.
- Applying the checklist only to companies you already dislike. The runaway pattern only bites on stories you find appealing.
