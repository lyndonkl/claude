# The Bermuda Triangle of valuation

**Core idea:** Three forces sink valuations. They form a triangle with "valuation first principles and good sense" at the centre. The three sides are **bias and preconceptions**, **complexity and detail**, and **uncertainty and the unknown**. Each side has its own symptoms. Bias breeds denial, deception and self-delusion. Complexity breeds confusion, intimidation and blind faith in models. Uncertainty breeds paralysis, outsourcing (letting someone else set value for you), herding and mental accounting. Use the triangle to diagnose. A valuation that goes wrong has usually drifted toward one side, and the fix is always the same: go back to first principles.

**Formulas:** None. The triangle is a diagnostic map:

| Side | Failure symptoms | First-principles antidote |
|---|---|---|
| Bias & preconceptions | Denial, deception, self-delusion | Declare your priors and who pays you; do qualitative work before looking at price |
| Complexity & detail | Confusion, intimidation, blind faith in models | Cut inputs to the story levers; be able to explain every input in one sentence |
| Uncertainty & the unknown | Paralysis, outsourcing, herding, mental accounting | Estimate anyway; use ranges/simulations; separate "I don't know" from "it can't be estimated" |

**Procedure:**
1. **Name the side you are drifting toward.** Symptom test: Are you defending a number you decided before modelling (bias)? Have you built rows you cannot explain (complexity)? Are you refusing to put a number down, or copying someone else's (uncertainty)?
2. **For bias:** run the checks in [[valuation-misconceptions]]. Name the payer, your prior positions and the answer you want. Then build the model from the story forward, never backward from the price.
3. **For complexity:** strip the model to five story levers — revenue growth, operating margin, investment efficiency, cost of capital, failure risk. See [[narrative-to-value-drivers]]. Cutting an input that does not move the decision is always a gain.
4. **For uncertainty:** do not outsource and do not freeze. Estimate the input. Then size the uncertainty with scenarios ([[narrative-scenario-grids]]) or a simulation ([[monte-carlo-valuation-simulation]]). Uncertainty belongs to the asset. It is not a failing of the analyst.
5. **Re-anchor on first principles.** Value comes from cash flows, growth and risk. Growth needs reinvestment. Excess returns need a moat. Nothing grows faster than the economy forever. Any model line that breaks one of these rules is a defect. See [[narrative-consistency-checks]].

**Reference data:** None (framework). Companion framework for where uncertainty comes from at each stage of a company's life: see [[life-cycle-uncertainty]].

**Worked example:** Theranos, valued at $9 billion in October 2015, failed on the bias side. A charismatic narrator, a disliked status quo (blood draws) and a societal-benefit story made investors stop asking questions. A celebrity board with no scientific depth supplied the *appearance* of a check. Modelling skill was no help here. The defence was a first-principles question: is the core scientific claim even possible? See [[runaway-stories]].

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: nothing.
- JUDGMENT: classifying which side of the triangle a given valuation is failing on. Information needed: the analyst's incentives and prior statements; the model's input count and how many inputs the analyst can justify unaided; whether the analyst has produced an estimate at all or deferred to consensus/price.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating uncertainty as a reason not to value. Paralysis and herding are failures, not prudence.
- Adding detail to fight uncertainty — complexity does not reduce uncertainty, it hides it.
- Mental accounting: quarantining an uncomfortable part of the valuation ("I'll just add option value for that") instead of modelling it. See the double-counting warning in [[contingent-claim-valuation]].
- Blind faith in a model whose inputs you did not set yourself.
