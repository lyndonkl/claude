# Keeping the feedback loop open and updating the narrative

**Core idea:** A narrative is a working hypothesis, not a commitment. Two habits keep it honest. First, actively seek counter-narratives from people who know the business better than you, and *value* their story rather than arguing about it — knowing what someone else's story is worth is more useful than knowing that you disagree. Second, revise as events unfold. Narratives change in three distinct ways, and each has its own valuation response and its own tool. A break ends the story. A shift changes its magnitude. A change expands or contracts the story into new territory. Diagnosing which one happened tells you which tool to reach for.

**Formulas:**
- Break: Expected value = Value if the narrative holds × (1 − probability of break) + Value if it breaks × probability of break.
- Shift: re-estimate the drivers and re-run; quantify the new uncertainty with scenarios or a simulation.
- Change: re-do the valuation on a new market definition, and treat genuinely unassessable new markets as option value.
- Unbiasedness test: over a long record, the count of upward revisions should be roughly equal to the count of downward revisions.

**Procedure:**
1. **Solicit counter-narratives explicitly.** Ask what would have to be true for a much higher value, and for a much lower one.
2. **Value the counter-narrative with the same model.** Change only the drivers the alternative story changes. See [[narrative-scenario-grids]].
3. **When news arrives, classify it.** Break, shift, or change. That is the only diagnosis that matters.
4. **Break: estimate a probability and the consequences.** Legal, political or economic events externally; management, competitive or default events internally. Once a break occurs, the existing estimates of cash flows, risk, growth and value are no longer operative.
5. **Shift: modify the driver estimates** for the new data on market size, market share and profitability. Then re-quantify the range with Monte Carlo or scenario analysis. See [[monte-carlo-valuation-simulation]].
6. **Change: redo the valuation** with new overall market potential and characteristics, and use real options for the newly opened possibilities. See [[contingent-claim-valuation]].
7. **Expect to be wrong.** Value estimates change as information arrives about the company, the business and the economy. Changing your value is not a failing; it is what risk means.
8. **Audit yourself for bias.** Count your revisions in both directions. Persistent one-way revision means a biased process, not an unlucky one.
9. **Revise on business news, not on price moves.** Price moving against you is not information about the business.

**Reference data:** The three narrative changes and their tools:

| Change | What happened | Valuation response | Tool |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrative break / end | External events (legal, political, economic) or internal ones (management, competitive, default) end the story | Existing estimates of cash flows, risk, growth and value stop being operative | Estimate the probability that the break occurs, and its consequences |
| Narrative shift | The initial business model improves or deteriorates, changing market size, market share and/or profitability | Modify the estimates to reflect the new data | Monte Carlo simulation or scenario analysis |
| Narrative change (expansion or contraction) | Unexpected entry or success in a new market; unexpected exit or failure in an existing one | Redo the valuation with new overall market potential and characteristics | Real options |

**Worked example:** Uber, June 2014. Damodaran published a narrative and value, then listed the three strongest challenges to his own story.
1. **Not just a car service company.** Uber could become a car company, with consumers subscribing to an Uber service instead of owning cars. It could also expand into logistics, moving and transportation. That is a narrative *change*, handled with a bigger market definition and option value.
2. **Not just urban.** Uber can create new demand for car service where taxis are not used — suburbia and small towns. That is a *shift* in market size, handled by raising the total market.
3. **Global networking benefits.** By linking with technology and credit-card companies, Uber could gain global rather than merely local network effects. That is a *shift* in achievable market share.

Each challenge was then valued. Bill Gurley's version of these claims produced $53.4 billion against Damodaran's $5.9 billion. The feedback loop did not force agreement, but it made the disagreement precise: it lives in market size, share and slice, and nowhere else.

A second illustration of "you will be wrong": Amazon in January 2000 was valued at $35.08 per share against an $84 price. By 2001 the estimate was about $20 against a price of $12, in 2002 about $24 against $16, and in 2003 about $36 against $59. The value moved, and it moved for good reasons. The price moved far more.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: the probability-weighted expected value once you set the break probability and both values. The re-run of the model on revised drivers. The revision-count symmetry test.
- JUDGMENT: classifying the news as break, shift or change. Estimating the break probability and its consequences. Deciding which counter-narratives are worth valuing. That judgment needs the legal and regulatory picture, competitor behaviour, management's record and the company's own reported results.

**Pitfalls:**
- Defending the original narrative because it is yours. Sunk-cost reasoning applied to stories.
- Treating a break as a shift, so the model keeps running on a business that has fundamentally changed.
- Treating a shift as a break and abandoning a valuation over one bad quarter.
- Revising the narrative because the price moved. That is herding with extra steps. See [[bermuda-triangle-of-valuation]].
- Never revising at all, which looks like conviction and is actually a stale model.
- Only ever revising in one direction, which the unbiasedness test is designed to catch.
