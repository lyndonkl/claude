# Pricing vs. value: the essence of relative valuation

**Core idea:** In relative valuation ("pricing"), an asset's worth is inferred from the market prices of similar assets, not from its own cash flows. The process has three steps. First, identify comparable assets and obtain their market values. Second, standardize those prices into multiples, since raw prices are not comparable across firms of different sizes. Third, compare the target's multiple to the comparables', controlling for differences in fundamentals. Relative valuation dominates practice: roughly 85% of equity research reports and more than 50% of acquisition valuations rest on multiples. Many DCFs are relative valuations in disguise — analysts back into multiple-derived targets, and terminal values are often set with multiples. The critical limitation: a relative judgment says only that a stock is cheap or expensive *versus the comparable group*. It can be relatively cheap and still hopelessly overvalued in absolute terms. Relative valuation is pricing, not valuation.

**Formulas:** Multiple = Numerator (what you pay) / Denominator (what you get). No other formulas; see [[multiple-definition-tests]] for numerator/denominator construction.

**Procedure:**
1. Decide whether the task calls for pricing or intrinsic valuation. Pricing is preferred when: the objective is to sell at today's price (IPOs), the strategy is momentum-based, the analyst is judged on relative performance (portfolio managers), or information is scarce (multiples as screens). Intrinsic valuation is preferred when hunting for absolute mispricing that will correct over time.
2. Identify comparable assets with market prices.
3. Standardize prices into multiples.
4. Compare, controlling for differences ([[comparable-selection-and-controls]]).
5. State the conclusion as *relative*: "cheap/expensive versus this comparable group," never as absolute value.

**Reference data:** none needed.

**Worked example:** The archetype of pricing-in-disguise: a DCF whose terminal value is set as "8x year-5 EBITDA" is a relative valuation wearing DCF clothing — most of its value comes from a multiple, not from cash flows.

**Determinism:** Almost entirely JUDGMENT: choosing pricing vs. valuing, choosing comparables, and interpreting relative verdicts. The standardization arithmetic itself is deterministic once numerator/denominator are chosen.

**Pitfalls:**
- Believing a relative "undervalued" verdict is an absolute one; in a bubble, the whole comparable group is overpriced and the relatively cheap stock still loses money.
- Because pricing grades on a curve, a significant fraction of securities will *always* look under- and over-valued — that is a property of the method, not evidence of opportunity.
- Failing to control for fundamental differences means your conclusions reflect your flawed comparisons, not market mistakes (no two assets are identical).
