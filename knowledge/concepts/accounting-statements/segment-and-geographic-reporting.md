# Segment and geographic reporting

**Core idea:** A consolidated total hides very different economics inside one firm. When a company spans many businesses and regions, the segment disclosure tells you where the revenues, the profits and the capital actually sit. The breakdown sometimes appears on the income statement, but more often it sits in the footnotes. Most firms split revenues by region and by business segment. The segment labels themselves involve judgment, so the boundaries are the firm's choice, not a standard. Segment data is the raw material for country-risk adjustments, for sum-of-the-parts valuation, and for spotting a business that consumes capital without earning a return.

**Formulas:**
- `Segment operating margin = Segment operating income / Segment total net operating revenues`.
- `Segment return on capital ≈ Segment operating income / (Segment identifiable operating assets + Segment investments)`.
- `Consolidated revenue = Σ segment third-party revenues` (intersegment revenues net to zero through eliminations).
- `Σ segment total revenues + Eliminations = Consolidated revenues`.
- `Revenue concentration by region = Segment revenue / Consolidated revenue`. These weights feed a revenue-weighted country risk premium.
- Reconciliation for a segmented oil filer: `Net income (group share) = Σ segment net operating income − Net cost of net debt − Noncontrolling interests`.

**Procedure:**
1. Locate the segment footnote. It carries more detail than the face of the income statement.
2. Record, for each segment: third-party revenues, intersegment revenues, operating income, identifiable assets, investments, capital expenditures and D&A.
3. Check that the segments plus eliminations reconcile to the consolidated totals. If they do not, you have missed a corporate or elimination column.
4. Compute segment operating margins. Rank the segments. Note where the revenue is and where the profit is; they are often different segments.
5. Compute segment return on capital using identifiable operating assets plus investments. Flag any segment with large assets and thin operating income.
6. Build the geographic revenue weights from third-party revenues. Use these weights for revenue-weighted country risk, not the firm's headquarters country.
7. Separate business-type segments from geographic ones. Coca-Cola's Global Ventures and Bottling Investments are business segments sitting inside an otherwise geographic split; mixing them double-counts.
8. Note the corporate column. It typically carries all the interest expense and a negative operating income, so it is not a business.

**Worked example:** Total S.A., 2019 (M$). Refining & Chemicals has the largest revenues from sales at 116,973, but only 3,342 of operating income — a 2.9% margin. Exploration & Production has revenues of just 38,590 yet operating income of 10,542 — a 27.3% margin. The consolidated statement shows neither fact. Reconciliation to the bottom line: segment net operating income 13,436 − net cost of net debt 1,998 − non-controlling interests 171 = net income group share 11,267. For Coca-Cola the same lesson appears on the capital side: Bottling Investments carries 11,170 of identifiable operating assets and 14,093 of investments, against operating income of only 358.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: all segment ratios and reconciliations. Inputs = the segment table. Outputs = margins, returns on segment capital, revenue weights, reconciliation to consolidated totals.
- JUDGMENT: the segment labels themselves are the firm's choice, so cross-firm segment comparison needs care. Assessing what the regional economics mean, and whether a low-return segment should be exited or is strategically necessary, is judgment. It needs the business description and competitive context.

**Pitfalls:**
- Assuming segment definitions are comparable across firms. They are not; labels involve judgment.
- Using total (including intersegment) revenues where third-party revenues are needed for geographic weighting.
- Ignoring the corporate and eliminations columns and then failing to reconcile.
- Treating a high-revenue segment as the important one. Revenue and profit often live in different segments.
- Assuming the segment note is on the income statement. Most often it sits in the footnotes.
