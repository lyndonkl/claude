# Life-cycle patterns in financial statements

**Core idea:** Firms move through six stages, and each stage leaves a signature in all three statements. Revenues follow an S-curve and then decline. Earnings lag revenues: negative early, positive as the firm scales, peaking in maturity, then falling faster than revenues in decline. Assets grow, stabilize and shrink. Debt is a last resort early, grows in maturity, and turns distressed at the end. Equity starts negative from losses, is fed first by capital infusions and later by retained earnings. Cash flows follow the simplest rule of all: young firms burn cash and raise it from others, old firms make cash and give it back. Knowing the stage tells you what a normal statement should look like, so that a loss at a young firm is a feature and a shrinking asset base at an old one is expected.

**Formulas:** No closed-form formula. The classification rule is a pattern match on the sign triple of the cash flow statement, plus revenue growth and profit direction.
- `Sign triple = (sign of CFO, sign of CFI, sign of CFF)`.
- `Revenue growth = (Revenue_t / Revenue_{t−1}) − 1`.
- `Profit direction = sign and trend of operating income over three or more years`.
- `Asset-base age proxy = Accumulated Depreciation / Gross PP&E`. Rising toward and past 50% marks an aging base.
- `Value composition`: early stage, nearly all value sits in growth assets; late stage, all of it sits in assets in place.

**Reference data:** Income statement across the life cycle.

| Stage | Revenue & revenue growth | Profit pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Small, but high growth rate | Losses, as business is built up, but sales are in the future |
| Young Growth | Acquiring substance, with high growth | Losses accelerate, as expenses grow faster than revenues |
| High Growth | Large and still growing | Losses decline & profits emerge, as revenues grow faster than expenses |
| Mature Growth | Large, but growth rate drops off | Profits climb, as economies of scale kick in |
| Mature Stable | Large, but stagnant | Profits level off, and grow with revenues |
| Decline | Revenues start dropping | Profits decline, often faster than revenues |

Balance sheet across the life cycle.

| Stage | Assets | Debt | Shareholder equity |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Small asset base, if any | Only as a last resort | Often negative, reflecting continued losses |
| Young Growth | Asset base grows quickly | Low or non-existent | Turns positive, more from capital infusions than retained earnings |
| High Growth | Continues to grow, at a lower rate | Stays low | Consolidates and starts growth as retained earnings accumulate |
| Mature Growth | Stabilizes & grows with earnings | Starts to grow | Grows quickly as earnings build, but cash return stays low |
| Mature Stable | Stabilizes | Large and often complex | Depends on how much cash the company chooses to return |
| Decline | Declines | Declining or in distress | Declines, as losses & divestitures take their toll |

Cash flows across the life cycle.

| Stage | Operating | Investing | Financing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Negative, starts with losses | Large investing needs | Inflows from new equity issuances |
| Young Growth | Still negative, working capital adds to losses | Continued investing activity | Continued inflows from new equity issuances |
| High Growth | Turns positive as profits appear | Investing growth starts to slow | Not much activity |
| Mature Growth | Increasingly positive, driven by net income growth | Investing stabilizes | Start of debt inflows & first outflows to equity |
| Mature Stable | Positive and stable | Maintenance investing | Regular debt issuance/payment and significant cash return to equity |
| Decline | Declines with profits dropping | Asset sales & divestitures | Debt repayments & equity cash return |

Asset-value composition across the life cycle.

| Stage | Assets in place | Growth assets | Stage description |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Start-up | None | All & unproven | Have an idea for a business that meets an unmet market need |
| 2. Young Growth | Very low | Most, and first proof | Create a business model converting ideas into potential revenues & earnings |
| 3. High Growth | Building substance | Most of the value | Build the business, converting potential into revenues |
| 4. Mature Growth | Becoming larger | Scaling down | Grow the business, shifting from losses to profits |
| 5. Mature Stable | Primary component | Tough to find | Defend the business from new competitors & find new markets |
| 6. Decline | All & declining | What growth assets? | Scale down as the market shrinks |

Stage-transition names used throughout the course: The Lightbulb (Idea) Moment, The Product Test (from idea to business), The Bar Mitzvah, The Scaling-up Test, The Midlife Crisis, The End Game.

**Procedure:**
1. Pull three to five years of all three statements.
2. Compute revenue growth and the direction of operating income. High growth with losses points to stages 1–2. High growth with emerging profits points to stage 3. Slowing growth with rising profits points to stage 4. Flat revenues with flat profits point to stage 5. Falling revenues with faster-falling profits point to stage 6.
3. Read the cash flow sign triple. Negative operating cash plus heavy investing plus equity inflows means start-up or young growth. Positive and growing operating cash plus slowing investing means high or mature growth. Stable operating cash plus maintenance investing plus cash returned to equity plus routine debt rollover means mature stable. Falling operating cash plus divestitures plus debt repayment plus equity cash return means decline.
4. Cross-check on the balance sheet. Negative equity from accumulated losses and cash-dominated assets means young. Large retained earnings, heavy treasury stock, complex debt and heavy accumulated depreciation mean mature or aging.
5. Assign a stage. Do not force a single label if the signals conflict; note which statement points where.
6. Use the stage to set expectations. Losses at a young-growth firm are a life-cycle feature, not automatically a sign of a bad business. Declining profits at an aging firm are expected, and profits often fall faster than revenues.
7. Use the stage to choose valuation inputs: growth-asset-heavy firms need revenue-and-margin forecasts, assets-in-place-heavy firms need current earnings and a decline path.

**Worked example:** Four firms, all from 2019–2020 filings.
- **Peloton (young growth, FY2019, $M).** Revenue 218.6 → 435.0 → 915.0 across FY2017–19. Gross profit positive and rising (73.9 → 189.6 → 383.6), but loss from operations (202.3) in FY2019 as operating expenses of 585.8 outran gross profit. R&D rose 13.0 → 23.4 → 54.8. Balance sheet: total assets 864.5, of which cash 162.1 plus marketable securities 216.0; total stockholders' deficit (538.6) from an accumulated deficit of (629.5). Cash triple: operating (108.6), investing (297.5), financing +417.2. Textbook stage 2.
- **Netflix (growth, 2019, $ thousands).** Revenues 11.69B → 15.79B → 20.16B; operating income 838.7M → 1,605.2M → 2,604.3M; net income 558.9M → 1,211.2M → 1,866.9M. Balance sheet dominated by non-current content assets of 24,504,567 and long-term debt of 14,759,260. Cash triple: operating (2,887,322), investing (387,064), financing +4,505,662. Profitable on paper, cash-hungry, debt-funded — stage 3.
- **Coca-Cola (mature stable, 2019, $M).** Revenues 36,212 → 34,300 → 37,266: large and stagnant. Operating income 10,086, net income 8,985. Balance sheet: goodwill 16,764, equity method investments 19,025, reinvested earnings 65,855, treasury stock (52,244), debt 31,769 in four currencies out to 2098. Cash triple: operating +10,471, investing (3,976), financing (9,004) with dividends 6,845 and buybacks 1,103. Stage 5.
- **Toyota (aging, FY2020, ¥M).** Total net revenues fell 30,225,681 → 29,929,992; operating income flat at 2,467,545 → 2,442,869. PP&E accumulated depreciation 13,855,563 against gross cost 24,457,088 (56.7%). Accrued pension and severance costs 978,626. Retained earnings 23,427,613 dwarf paid-in capital of 489,334. Operating cash 3,590,643, dividends large and steady. Stage 5 shading into 6.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: revenue growth rates; the cash flow sign triple; the accumulated-depreciation ratio; the retained-earnings-to-paid-in-capital ratio; every statement subtotal in the examples above.
- JUDGMENT: assigning the stage. That needs the growth trajectory, the profit pattern, the cash triple, the balance-sheet composition, and knowledge of the industry's own maturity. Two firms with identical sign triples can sit in different stages if one is a capital-light software firm and the other a capital-heavy manufacturer.

**Pitfalls:**
- Reading a loss as a bad business. At start-up and young-growth firms, expenses growing faster than revenues is exactly what the stage predicts.
- Reading a shrinking asset base or falling profits at an old firm as a crisis. Decline is a stage, not an accident.
- Assigning a stage from one statement. A firm can look mature on the income statement and young on the cash flow statement, as Netflix does.
- Forgetting that firms can move backwards, or that a mature firm can re-enter growth through acquisition.
- Comparing life-cycle stage across industries without adjusting for how fast that industry's cycle runs.
