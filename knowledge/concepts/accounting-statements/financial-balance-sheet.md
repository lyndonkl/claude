# The financial balance sheet

**Core idea:** The financial balance sheet describes any business in four boxes, and it is the bridge from accounting statements to valuation. On the asset side, assets in place are the value of investments the firm has already made, updated to reflect their current cash flow potential. Growth assets are the value of investments you expect the firm to make in the future. On the other side, debt holders have first claim on any cash the firm makes, and equity investors get whatever is left. Two things separate this from the accounting balance sheet. It values assets by what they will earn, not by what they cost. And it ranks claims by their place in line rather than by legal label.

**Formulas:**
- Structure: `Value of Assets in Place + Value of Growth Assets = Value of Debt + Value of Equity`.
- `Value of assets in place = present value of cash flows from investments already made`. This can be far below what was invested, far above it, or roughly equal to it.
- `Value of growth assets = present value of excess returns on future investments`, not the raw size of future investment. Excess return = return on the investment minus the cost of funding it.
- Growth asset sign rule: many excess-return investments → high; few investments or zero/low excess returns → near zero; value-destroying investments made anyway → negative.
- Equity as residual: `Equity value = Firm value − Debt value`, with the equity holder receiving cash only after all contractual claims are met.

**Reference data:** The four boxes.

| Box | Definition | What sets its value |
|---|---|---|
| Assets in Place | Value of investments already made over the firm's history | Your estimate of what those existing investments will generate in future |
| Growth Assets | Value of investments you expect the firm to make in future | Expected excess returns on those investments, not their size |
| Debt | Lenders, short and long term | First claim on any cash flow the firm makes |
| Equity | Owners | Whatever is left over after debt is paid |

Value of assets in place versus original investment:

| Earnings power since the investment | Value versus amount invested |
|---|---|
| Deteriorated | Value < amount invested |
| Improved | Value > amount invested |
| Stagnated | Value ≈ amount invested |

The three characteristics that make a claim debt rather than equity:

| # | Characteristic |
|---|---|
| 1 | Contractual commitments to make payments to lenders — interest and principal |
| 2 | Failure to meet those commitments can put the firm out of business, or cost the owners their equity stake |
| 3 | In much of the world, tax law tilts in favour of borrowing; interest is typically tax-deductible |

Equity's two layers:

| Layer | Who gets what |
|---|---|
| Residual claim | The sole owner of a business can claim whatever cash flows are left after everyone else is paid their contractual dues |
| Dividend claim | A stockholder in a public company receives that residual only to the extent managers decide to pay it out as dividends |

**Procedure:**
1. Start from the accounting balance sheet, then rebuild it as four boxes. Nothing carries over at book value except cash, marketable securities and interest-bearing debt.
2. Value assets in place from their current cash flow potential. Ask which way earnings power has moved since the investments were made, and set the value above, below or near the invested amount accordingly.
3. Value growth assets from expected excess returns, not from expected investment. Estimate the return on new investment and the cost of funding it, and value only the spread.
4. If the firm invests in projects that earn below the cost of funding, record growth assets as a negative number. Growth alone adds no value, and growth can destroy value.
5. Classify every financing instrument as debt or equity using the three characteristics. Contractual payments, default consequences and tax deductibility together make it debt. Legal labels do not decide this; leases and other commitments qualify.
6. Compute equity as the residual: firm value less debt value.
7. For a public company, separate what equity investors are owed from what they receive. Managers decide the payout, so a residual claim and a dividend claim are not the same thing (see [[potential-dividends-fcfe]]).
8. Cross-check the composition against the firm's life-cycle stage. Early-stage value should sit almost entirely in growth assets; a declining firm should show all of its value in assets in place.

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola at end-2019 illustrates the gap between the two balance sheets. The accounting sheet shows total assets of 86,381 million dollars, of which goodwill is 16,764 and net PP&E just 10,838, with book equity attributable to shareowners of 18,981. The financial balance sheet reads differently. Assets in place are the present value of the cash flows of a brand and a bottling network. Those assets generate operating income of 10,086 a year. Their value sits far above the 10,838 of book PP&E, because earnings power has improved enormously relative to what was invested. Growth assets are worth little, because a stage-5 firm with stagnant revenue (36,212 → 34,300 → 37,266 over 2017–19) has few excess-return investments left. Debt is 31,769 from the footnote, close to what is truly owed. Equity is the residual, and it bears no relation to the 18,981 of book equity, which is an artefact of 52,244 of treasury stock.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: only the arithmetic of the identity, and the extraction of cash, marketable securities and interest-bearing debt from the accounting statements. Equity as firm value minus debt value is deterministic once both are estimated.
- JUDGMENT: every valuation input. Estimating the future earning power of existing investments is judgment and needs the firm's operating history, competitive position and industry outlook. Forecasting future investment opportunities and their excess returns is judgment and needs the growth pipeline, the return-on-capital record and the cost of capital. Classifying an instrument as debt or equity is judgment when the instrument is hybrid.

**Pitfalls:**
- Carrying accounting book values into the four boxes. Book value measures neither invested capital nor fair value.
- Valuing growth by the size of expected investment. Only the excess return counts, so a firm that reinvests heavily at the cost of capital creates nothing.
- Forgetting that growth assets can be negative. A firm that keeps making bad investments destroys value by growing.
- Treating a public shareholder's residual claim as cash in hand. Managers decide the dividend.
- Classifying debt by legal label. The three characteristics — contractual payments, default consequences, tax deductibility — are the test.
- Assuming assets in place are worth what was invested. They are worth what they will earn.
