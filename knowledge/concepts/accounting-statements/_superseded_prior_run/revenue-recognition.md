# Revenue recognition (ASC 606)

**Core idea:** Accrual accounting books revenue when a product or service is sold, not when cash arrives. For most firms that is simple. It gets hard for firms that sell over many years: how much revenue belongs in the sale year, and how much later? ASC 606 states the core principle: revenue should "depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers in an amount that reflects the consideration to which the entity expects to be entitled in exchange for those goods or services." In plain terms: book revenue as the goods or services actually transfer to the customer, at the amount you expect to collect. For multi-year contracts, performance obligations set the timing. The flip side on the balance sheet: cash collected before the service is delivered sits as deferred revenue, a liability.

**Formulas:** Revenue booked in a period = Expected total payment × Share of performance obligations met in that period. (Expected payment, "consideration" = the amount the firm expects to be owed. Share met = the judged progress on the transfer of goods or services.)

**Procedure:**
1. For a point-in-time sale (retail, most products): book the full revenue at sale.
2. For a multi-year contract: list the performance obligations in the contract.
3. Estimate the expected total payment for the contract.
4. Book revenue each period in step with the obligations met that period. A real estate developer on a multi-year build books revenue as the build moves ahead. A software firm with a multi-year deal books revenue as its obligations are met.
5. Record cash received ahead of delivery as deferred revenue (a liability). Release it into revenue as delivery happens.
6. As an analyst, test the firm's progress claims: pushy progress estimates pull revenue forward.

**Reference data:** ASC 606 core principle text (quoted above). Governing bodies: FASB for GAAP; the parallel IFRS standard follows the same transfer-of-control idea.

**Worked example:** Netflix (2019 balance sheet). Subscribers pay monthly, often ahead of service, so Netflix carried $924.7M of deferred revenue as a liability at year-end 2019. That cash was in the bank but not yet earned. It becomes revenue only as the service month passes. Peloton similarly carried $90.8M of "customer deposits and deferred revenue" at June 30, 2019 — payments for bikes and subscriptions not yet delivered.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: once progress shares and expected payment are set, the revenue split across years is plain math. The deferred-revenue roll-forward (opening + cash billed − revenue booked = closing) is also math. JUDGMENT: deciding when the deal "occurs," judging progress on performance obligations, and sizing the expected payment. That judgment needs the contract terms and proof of delivery progress.

**Pitfalls:** Assuming revenue equals cash collected — it does not under accrual rules. Taking management's progress claims on long contracts at face value; they control revenue timing. Missing deferred revenue as a real duty to deliver. Comparing revenue across firms whose booking timing differs.
