# Expense classification: operating, financing, capital

**Core idea:** Every expense a business incurs falls into one of three classes, and the class decides where it lands in the statements. Operating expenses come from running the business this period: the direct costs of making the product or service, plus other production-era costs such as S,G&A. Financing expenses come from using non-equity funding — most often interest on debt. Capital expenses give benefits over many years, such as plant and equipment. Everything has a place. Operating expenses are netted from revenues on this year's income statement. Financing expenses appear there as financial expenses; the borrowings behind them sit on the balance sheet as debt. Capital expenses become balance-sheet assets. They are then written off as depreciation over the asset's life. The class drives every profit measure, so a wrong class distorts earnings.

**Formulas:**
- Operating Profit = Revenues − Operating Expenses.
- Taxable Income = Operating Profit − Financial Expenses.
- Net Income = Taxable Income − Taxes.
(Revenues = the accountant's count of sales in the period. Operating Expenses = all expenses tied to operations this year, with no benefit spilling into future years. Financial Expenses = expenses tied to non-equity funding. Taxes = taxes on taxable income; these may not equal cash taxes paid.)

**Procedure:** To classify any expense:
1. Ask: is it tied to operations this period, with no benefit spilling into future years? → operating expense. Net it from revenues this year.
2. Ask: does it come from using non-equity funding (debt, leases)? → financing expense. It appears below operating profit; the obligation behind it is balance-sheet debt.
3. Ask: does it create benefits over many years? → capital expense. It goes on the balance sheet as an asset and is depreciated or amortized over its life.
4. Be alert with firms that do not make physical goods. Their capital expenses take less solid forms (R&D, content, customer acquisition), and accounting handles those poorly — often expensing what is really capital.
5. When a firm moves expenses between classes (above all operating → capital), question the shift; it lifts current profits (see [[extraordinary-items-and-pro-forma]]).

**Reference data:** Where each class lands:

| Expense class | Test | Where it shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Operating | Benefits only this period | Income statement, netted from revenues |
| Financing | Cost of non-equity funding | Income statement, financial expenses; borrowing on balance sheet as debt |
| Capital | Benefits over many years | Balance sheet as asset; income statement later as depreciation/amortization |

**Worked example:** Netflix 2019. Content spending of $13.9B creates a multi-year asset, so it is capital in nature. It sits on the balance sheet as content assets ($24.5B non-current at year-end 2019). It flows through the income statement as amortization ($9.2B in 2019) inside cost of revenues. Interest expense of $626M on its $14.8B of long-term debt is a financing expense, below operating income. Marketing of $2.65B benefits mainly the current period, so it is operating. The classification explains why Netflix can report $2.6B of operating income while burning $2.9B of operating cash.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: once each expense is classed, the profit lines follow by subtraction, and the statement placement is mechanical. JUDGMENT: the classing itself — deciding whether a cost's benefits end this period or spill into future years, and whether a commitment is really debt-like. This needs knowledge of the business model and the life of the benefit.

**Pitfalls:** Accepting the accountant's classes at face value for firms without physical products — their true capital expenses (R&D, content) are often expensed as operating. Missing that "taxes" on the income statement may not equal cash taxes paid. Letting firms shift operating expenses into capital to flatter current profits.
