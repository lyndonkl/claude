# Extraordinary items and pro-forma earnings

**Core idea:** Extraordinary items are ones a firm does not face in the normal course of business. Examples: a one-time gain or cost from selling assets or divisions, write-offs from past projects, lawsuit charges, fines, and goodwill impairment from past deals. The test is strict: a truly extraordinary item shows up rarely AND varies in amount. If an item appears every year, it is not extraordinary — even if it flips between profits and losses. Firms have also grown creative with pro-forma statements, which restate earnings with chosen items stripped out. Some do this to fix real flaws in accounting rules. Others do it to make profits look better. Never take pro-forma numbers at face value. Build your own smell test for what deserves an add-back.

**Formulas:** Adjusted (recurring) earnings = Reported earnings ± items that pass the extraordinary test. (Only items that are both rare and variable in amount qualify for removal.)

**Procedure:**
1. List every item the firm labels one-time, extraordinary, or excluded in its pro-forma numbers.
2. Apply the two-part test to each item: (a) does it show up rarely? (b) does its amount vary? Both must hold. An item that appears every year fails, regardless of sign flips.
3. Scrutinize the two highest-risk moves in any pro-forma statement: expenses shifted from operating to capital (sometimes merited, sometimes not), and expenses removed as "one-time or extraordinary."
4. Keep truly rare items out of recurring earnings, but keep recurring "one-time" charges in — a firm with a restructuring charge every year has a recurring cost.
5. Rebuild your own adjusted earnings rather than accepting the firm's version.

**Reference data:**

| Item | Passes extraordinary test? |
|---|---|
| One-time gain/cost from selling a division | Usually yes (rare, variable) |
| Goodwill impairment from an old deal | Case by case; often yes as a charge, but signals past overpayment |
| "Restructuring" charge taken every year | No — recurring |
| Stock-based compensation excluded in pro-forma | No — a real, recurring expense |
| Lawsuit or fine | Yes if genuinely rare for this firm |

**Worked example:** HSBC 2019 ($m). Reported operating expenses of 42,349 included a goodwill impairment of 7,349 — a large one-off charge tied mostly to past deals. Profit before tax was 13,347. An analyst judging HSBC's recurring earning power can treat the 7,349 as extraordinary (rare, variable) and look at profit before tax of about 20,700 before the impairment. The same analyst should refuse to strip the expected-credit-loss charge of 2,756: loan losses recur every year for a bank, so they fail the test.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: recomputing earnings once the keep/strip list is fixed. JUDGMENT: the keep/strip decisions themselves. They need each item's history (how often it appears), its size pattern, and a view on whether an operating-to-capital shift is merited.

**Pitfalls:** Accepting pro-forma earnings at face value. Stripping items that recur every year. Letting firms shift operating expenses to capital to flatter profits. Missing that a "one-time" charge pattern repeated annually is a business cost in disguise. Treating goodwill impairment as pure noise — it also tells you past deals destroyed value.
