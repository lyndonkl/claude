# Balance sheet: structure and dueling views

**Core idea:** The balance sheet lays out what a firm owns against what it owes, at a point in time. Three rival views exist on what it should measure. View 1: a record of capital invested in assets-in-place — Damodaran's preferred view. View 2: the current (fair) value of the business today — the view of a large school of rule-writers. View 3: sale-off value — what a lender would get if the assets were sold today. Modern rules mix views 1 and 2. So today's balance sheets measure neither invested capital nor fair value cleanly. The old way carried fixed and current assets at first cost, net of depreciation. The new way marks assets to fair value. The gap between cost and fair value follows a set pattern: it is larger for older assets than newer ones, and larger for fixed assets than current assets. Bottom line: the most trustworthy items are cash and marketable securities (no accounting nuance) and interest-bearing debt (close to what is truly owed). Treat other book values with care.

**Formulas:** Assets = Liabilities + Equity. Net fixed assets = Gross cost − Accumulated depreciation.

**Procedure:**
1. Sort every asset line into the four groups: fixed assets (long-lived physical), current assets (short-lived), financial assets (stakes in securities and other firms), intangible assets (non-physical).
2. Sort the other side into: current liabilities (short-term), debt (long-term), other liabilities (other long-term), and shareholders' equity.
3. For each asset, ask which value rule applies. Traded securities → market value. Fixed assets → mostly cost net of depreciation. Bought intangibles → deal-based values.
4. Gauge the cost-vs-value gap with the pattern rule: older asset → bigger gap; fixed asset → bigger gap than a current asset.
5. Trust selectively: rely on cash and marketable securities and on interest-bearing debt. Treat book values of operating assets, intangibles, and equity as neither invested capital nor fair value.
6. Read the balance sheet as cumulated history — everything the firm has done since inception — not as a valuation.

**Reference data:** Standard structure:

| Asset category | Description | Other side | Description |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fixed Assets | Long-lived physical assets | Current Liabilities | Short-term obligations |
| Current Assets | Short-lived assets | Debt | Long-term debt |
| Financial Assets | Stakes in securities and other firms | Other Liabilities | Other long-term obligations |
| Intangible Assets | Non-physical assets | Equity | Shareholders' equity |

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola, Dec 31, 2019 ($ millions). Current assets 20,411 (including cash and short-term investments of 7,947 — trustworthy). Fixed assets: PP&E net 10,838. Financial assets: equity method investments 19,025 and marketable securities 3,228. Intangibles: trademarks 9,266 plus goodwill 16,764 — deal-driven accounting numbers, not brand value. Total assets 86,381 = total liabilities 65,283 + total equity 21,098. The book equity of 21,098 sat against a market value of equity many times larger; the balance sheet is not a valuation.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: the sorting map (each line goes to one group), the balance identity, and the net-of-depreciation math. JUDGMENT: choosing which view of the balance sheet fits your purpose, and gauging how far each book value drifts from invested capital or fair value.

**Pitfalls:** Reading book values as fair values, or as clean invested capital — modern sheets are a mix. Trusting intangible and goodwill numbers as economic values. Forgetting the age pattern: older and more fixed assets carry the least reliable book values. Ignoring that the balance sheet is a snapshot, dated to one day.
