# The three-statement framework

**Core idea:** Accounting answers three questions. What do you own? What do you owe? How much money did you make? Each question maps to one statement. The balance sheet is a snapshot of assets, liabilities and book equity at a point in time. The income statement reports accrual earnings over a period. The cash flow statement reports cash in and out over a period, giving cash earnings as opposed to accounting earnings. Damodaran's framing: the accountant is a historian, not a forecaster or a valuer. Accounting's job is to check transactions, record them consistently, and report them in standardized form. It is not accounting's role to predict the future or value the business. The three statements interlock, so no statement can be fully understood alone. Accounting statements are the raw material for almost all financial analysis and valuation. So an analyst must grasp how accountants think, even when disagreeing with them, and must track how accounting rules change over time.

**Formulas:** No canonical formulas at this level; the structural identities live in the child concepts:
- Balance sheet: Assets = Liabilities + Equity.
- Income statement: Net Income = Revenues − Operating Expenses − Financial Expenses − Taxes (see [[income-statement-structure]]).
- Cash flow statement: Net Change in Cash = CF Operations + CF Investing + CF Financing (see [[cash-flow-statement-structure]]).

**Procedure:** When picking up any company's filings:
1. Locate the three statements. The balance sheet is dated "as of" a point in time. The income and cash flow statements cover a period.
2. Map each statement to its question. Balance sheet → what is owned and owed, plus accounting's estimate of equity value. Income statement → accrual profit for the period. Cash flow statement → cash profit and the change in the cash balance.
3. Trace the interconnections before reading any statement alone. Depreciation of fixed assets links the income statement to the balance sheet: the expense reduces fixed-asset book values. Net income flows into balance-sheet equity via retained earnings. It is also the starting line of the cash flow statement. Operating, investing and financing cash flows change balance-sheet accounts, including the cash balance.
4. Read every number as a record of the past, not a forecast or a valuation. Measure profitability both ways: accrual (income statement) and cash (cash flow statement).
5. Remember "what do you owe" includes all contractual commitments, not only borrowings.

**Reference data:** None needed.

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola's 2019 filings, used throughout this concept area. The Dec 31, 2019 balance sheet shows $86,381M of assets against $65,283M of liabilities and $21,098M of equity. The 2019 income statement shows $8,985M of consolidated net income. The 2019 cash flow statement starts from that same $8,985M. After +$10,471M operating, −$3,976M investing and −$9,004M financing cash flows, it reconciles to a year-end cash balance of $6,480M. The same net income figure appears in all three places — the interlock in action.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: the cross-statement tie-outs. Net income on the income statement equals the starting line of the cash flow statement. The change in cash on the cash flow statement equals the change in balance-sheet cash. Retained earnings roll forward as prior balance + net income − dividends. JUDGMENT: interpreting what the statements say about the business, and spotting where accounting conventions distort the picture.

**Pitfalls:** Treating accounting statements as forecasts or as estimates of intrinsic value — they are historical records. Reading one statement in isolation. Assuming book equity measures what equity is worth; it is only accounting's estimate. Ignoring that accounting rules keep changing, so statements from different eras are not directly comparable.
