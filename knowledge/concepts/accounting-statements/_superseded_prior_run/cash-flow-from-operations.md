# Cash flow from operations and the working capital effect

**Core idea:** Cash flow from operations turns accrual net income into the cash the business truly made. The build-up has two moves. First, add back non-cash charges. These are items like depreciation and stock-based pay: they cut income, but no cash leaves. Second, adjust for the change in non-cash working capital. That change is the sum of five current-account moves: receivables, inventory, other current assets, payables, and taxes due. When non-cash working capital rises, cash flow falls. When it falls, cash flow rises. Working capital ties up cash. So at any given net income, a firm with high working-capital needs makes less operating cash than one with low needs.

**Formulas:**
- Cash Flow from Operations = Net Income + Depreciation and Amortization + Other non-cash expenses ± Change in Accounts Receivable ± Change in Inventory ± Change in Other Current Assets ± Change in Accounts Payable ± Change in Taxes Due.
- Non-cash Working Capital = Non-cash current assets − Non-debt current liabilities.
- Sign rule: increase in non-cash working capital → cash outflow; decrease → cash inflow.
(Net income = accrual equity earnings. Non-cash expenses = charges with no cash outlay. Non-debt current liabilities exclude interest-bearing short-term debt.)

**Procedure:**
1. Start with net income (or, in IFRS formats, profit before tax with taxes paid deducted later).
2. Add back depreciation and amortization, plus other non-cash charges: stock-based pay, deferred taxes, write-downs.
3. Compute the change in each working-capital account. Asset up (receivables, inventory) → subtract. Liability up (payables, taxes due) → add.
4. Sum to cash flow from operations.
5. Exclude cash and interest-bearing short-term debt from the working-capital accounts (see [[debt-and-current-liabilities]]).
6. Compare the result with net income. Explain any large or growing gap (see [[accrual-vs-cash-accounting]]).

**Reference data:** The canonical build-up:

| Effect | Item |
|---|---|
| Start with | Net Income |
| Plus | Depreciation and Amortization |
| Plus | Other non-cash expenses |
| ± | Change in Accounts Receivable |
| ± | Change in Inventory |
| ± | Change in Other Current Assets |
| ± | Change in Accounts Payable |
| ± | Change in Taxes Due |
| = | Cash Flow from Operations |

The five ± items together are the change in non-cash working capital.

**Worked example:** Peloton FY2019 ($ millions): net loss (195.6) + D&A 21.7 + stock-based compensation 89.5, less a working-capital drag — inventories used (111.3), partly offset by payables +117.3 — gives operating cash of (108.6). Dr. Reddy's FY2020 (₹ millions) shows the IFRS format: profit before tax 27,758 + depreciation and amortisation 7,892, working-capital changes (receivables (10,927), inventories (1,748), payables +368), less taxes paid (4,769) → net operating cash 18,392.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: the whole build-up. Given net income, non-cash charges, and the account changes, a script computes operating cash flow. Balance-sheet deltas supply the working-capital changes. JUDGMENT: spotting which "other" items are truly non-cash, and reading what the working-capital pattern means for the business.

**Pitfalls:** Counting cash inside working capital, or leaving short-term debt in it. Getting the signs backwards: a rise in receivables is a cash drain, not a gain. Missing stock-based pay as a large non-cash add-back at young firms — it flatters cash flow while diluting shareholders. Forgetting that book taxes and cash taxes differ; the deferred-tax line corrects for it.
