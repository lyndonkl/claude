# Depreciation: economic, accounting, and tax

**Core idea:** Depreciation comes in three forms, and each one measures a different thing. The economic form is the real loss in earning power as an asset ages. It needs nuance. It varies even within one asset type, based on how the asset is used. The accounting form is more of a machine. It is driven mostly by the asset's age. The main choice: write the cost off evenly (straight-line) or faster up front (accelerated). The tax form is whatever the tax office allows when computing taxable income. Depreciation is also the main bridge between statements. The expense on the income statement cuts fixed-asset book values on the balance sheet. And since no cash leaves, it is added back in the cash flow statement.

**Formulas:**
- Straight-line depreciation per year = Asset cost / Useful life in years. (Asset cost = the capitalized amount; useful life = the assumed years of service.)
- Accelerated methods write off more of the cost early and less later; the total written off over the life is the same.
- Net fixed assets = Gross cost − Accumulated depreciation.

**Procedure:**
1. When reading a depreciation number, first ask which form is in play. Book reporting → accounting depreciation. Taxable income → tax depreciation. True value loss → economic depreciation.
2. For accounting depreciation, find the method (straight-line vs accelerated) and assumed lives in the footnotes.
3. Check accumulated depreciation against gross cost on the balance sheet. A high ratio marks an old asset base.
4. In cash flow work, add depreciation and amortization back to net income — no cash left the firm this period.
5. Do not treat book or tax depreciation as the true economic decay of the asset. Judge that separately from how the asset is used.

**Reference data:**

| Form | What it measures | Driver |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Real loss of earning power | Asset use; varies within asset type |
| Accounting | Book write-off of cost | Age; method choice (straight-line or accelerated) |
| Tax | Deduction allowed for taxable income | Tax code rules |

**Worked example:** Toyota, FY2020 (yen in millions). Gross property, plant and equipment at cost: ¥24,457,088. Accumulated depreciation: ¥(13,855,563). Net PP&E: ¥10,601,525. About 57% of the asset base's cost has been written off — the mark of an aging manufacturer. On the cash flow side, Coca-Cola 2019 added back $1,365M of depreciation and amortization on the way from $8,985M net income to $10,471M operating cash. Commodity firms show the largest add-backs: Total S.A. added back $16,401M of depreciation, depletion and impairment in 2019.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: accounting and tax depreciation, given cost, method, and life; net fixed assets from gross cost and accumulated depreciation; the cash-flow add-back. JUDGMENT: economic depreciation — the real decay in earning power — which needs knowledge of how the asset is used and what it can still earn.

**Pitfalls:** Confusing the three forms, especially treating accounting depreciation as economic reality. Forgetting that book taxes use accounting depreciation while cash taxes use tax depreciation, so the two tax numbers diverge (the deferred-tax line captures this). Reading heavily depreciated assets as worthless — book value and earning power can differ widely.
