# Goodwill and intangible assets

**Core idea:** Accountants talk a big game on intangibles, but they are only good at the small-bore ones — licenses, customer lists — where earnings are easy to see and forecast. They remain poor at the big ones: brand name, management quality. Most intangible value on balance sheets sits in one item, goodwill, and goodwill is a plug. It appears only when a company makes an acquisition. It equals the price paid minus the target's "dressed up" book value of assets. It exists as an asset only so the balance sheet balances. Under old rules, goodwill was amortized on autopilot over long periods. Since the late 1990s, both GAAP and IFRS instead require an annual impairment test: re-value the target each year; if value fell, write goodwill down. In practice impairments trail sector market prices by months or years. They carry little news, and their effect on stock prices has been close to nil.

**Formulas:**
- Goodwill = Price paid for the target − Target's restated ("dressed up") book value of assets.
- Net carrying value (definite-lived intangible) = Gross carrying value − Accumulated amortization.

**Procedure:**
1. Split the intangibles note into two groups. Indefinite-lived (trademarks, franchise rights, goodwill): not amortized, impairment-tested yearly. Definite-lived (customer lists, some trademarks): amortized over life.
2. For goodwill, trace which acquisitions created it. Goodwill measures deal premium, not brand value.
3. Apply the impairment logic: each year the target is re-valued. Value up → goodwill unchanged. Value down → goodwill impaired (written down through the income statement).
4. When goodwill impairment appears, read it two ways: as a (usually) extraordinary charge for recurring-earnings work, and as an admission that a past deal destroyed value.
5. Do not read the intangibles block as the firm's true intangible value. A brand-driven firm's real intangible worth is mostly absent from its balance sheet, because internally built intangibles (brand, R&D) are expensed, not capitalized.

**Reference data:** Coca-Cola intangibles, Dec 31, 2019 ($ millions):

| Group | Item | 2019 |
|---|---|---|
| Indefinite-lived | Trademarks | 9,266 |
| Indefinite-lived | Bottlers' franchise rights | 109 |
| Indefinite-lived | Goodwill | 16,764 |
| Indefinite-lived | Other | 110 |
| Indefinite-lived | Total | 26,249 |
| Definite-lived | Total gross | 917 |
| Definite-lived | Accumulated amortization | (400) |
| Definite-lived | Total net | 517 |

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola 2019. The company's worth rests almost wholly on its brand. Yet definite-lived intangibles net to just $517M, and the $9,266M trademark line plus $16,764M of goodwill reflect deal accounting, not brand value. Contrast Dr. Reddy's (FY2020, ₹ millions): goodwill of ₹323M and other intangibles of ₹6,318M, while the firm's real value sits in R&D and drug pipelines that accounting expenses as incurred. In both cases the balance sheet badly understates the true intangible assets. As a goodwill mechanics example: if a buyer pays $10B for a target whose restated asset book value is $6B, goodwill of $4B is booked — the plug that makes the sheet balance.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: goodwill from price paid and restated book value; net carrying values from gross minus accumulated amortization; amortization schedules for definite-lived items. JUDGMENT: the "dressing up" of target book values, the annual re-valuation behind impairment tests, and any attempt to size true brand or management value.

**Pitfalls:** Reading goodwill as an asset with earning power — it signifies little. Treating trademark book values as brand value. Expecting impairments to be timely news; they lag market moves and inform little. Comparing intangible-heavy and intangible-light firms on book values when the difference is mostly accounting treatment of internal investment.
