# Accrual vs cash accounting

**Core idea:** Accrual accounting records a deal when it happens, not when cash moves. Revenue is booked when a product or service is sold, not when the customer pays. Expenses are matched to the period of the sale they support, even if the cash moved earlier or later. Cash accounting instead books revenue when payment arrives and expenses when paid. Any firm that is not small or personal must use accrual rules. The gap matters for analysis: accrual earnings and cash earnings can differ a lot, and the income statement and cash flow statement can fairly tell different stories. But a steady, lasting gap between the two is a red flag for accounting problems. The cash flow statement is the most direct of the three statements. It is built from actual cash in and cash out, so accounting game playing touches it least.

**Formulas:** None at this level. The bridge from accrual earnings to cash flow is: Cash flow = Earnings + Non-cash expenses − Capital expenditures − Change in non-cash working capital (see [[cash-flow-from-operations]] and [[fcfe-potential-dividends]] for the full build-ups).

**Procedure:**
1. Confirm the firm reports on an accrual basis (any listed firm does).
2. For each period, put accrual net income next to cash flow from operations.
3. Explain the gap with the known accrual wedges: non-cash charges (depreciation, amortization, stock-based compensation), working-capital build-ups, and the timing split between capital spending and its later write-off.
4. Judge the gap. A one-period gap is normal. A firm can honestly show profits while its business eats cash, or losses while it throws off cash.
5. Apply the red-flag rule: steady profits with weak operating cash, year after year, points to accounting problems. Trust the cash flows when earnings and cash flows keep disagreeing.

**Reference data:** None.

**Worked example:** Netflix 2019 ($ thousands). Accrual net income: 1,866,916 — a solid profit. Operating cash flow: (2,887,322) — a large cash burn. The wedge: Netflix spent 13,916,683 on new streaming content (cash out now) but expensed only 9,216,247 of content amortization (a non-cash charge on past spending). Debt issuance of 4,469,306 filled the hole. Accrual accounting spreads the content cost over years; cash accounting shows the money leaving today. Neither number is a lie, but they answer different questions.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: computing the earnings-to-cash gap from reported figures (net income − operating cash flow) and listing the reconciling items. JUDGMENT: deciding when a deal "occurs" for revenue timing, and deciding whether a persistent earnings-cash gap signals accounting trouble or a benign growth pattern.

**Pitfalls:** Judging firm health from the earnings bottom line alone. Treating a single-period earnings-cash divergence as fraud — the two can fairly differ. Ignoring a divergence that persists for years, which is the real warning sign. Forgetting that only small or personal businesses may use cash accounting, so any listed firm's revenue line embeds timing judgments.
