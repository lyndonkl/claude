# GAAP vs IFRS: accounting standards and comparability

**Core idea:** Accounting is a rule-driven process. The rules are formal for publicly traded companies. Two goals drive the rules. One is a common standard, so firms can be set against each other. The other is first principles, so earnings, asset value and cash flows measure what they claim to measure. World standards have largely merged into two systems. GAAP governs US reporting; its rules come from FASB. IFRS governs most globally listed firms; its rules come from IASB. Despite talk of drift, standards are growing more alike, not less. Profit measures (gross profit, operating income, net income) are meant to line up across firms. In practice, firms measure and sort expenses in uneven ways. That breaks the match, so an analyst must adjust before comparing.

**Formulas:** None.

**Procedure:** When comparing companies across borders or formats:
1. Identify the standard: US-listed domestic firms → GAAP (FASB rules). Most other listed firms (about 90 countries as of 2020) → IFRS (IASB rules). Notable non-IFRS countries besides the US: China, India, Japan (each with local standards; India's Ind-AS is IFRS-converged).
2. Note layout differences. IFRS balance sheets often list non-current assets first, and equity before liabilities. IFRS/Indian-style income statements often list expenses by nature (materials, employee benefits, depreciation), not by function (COGS, SG&A).
3. Map nature-based expense lines into function buckets (COGS vs other operating) before computing gross margin across firms. Beware coarse catch-all lines such as Dr. Reddy's "Selling and other expenses" (₹33,768M in FY2020). They lump many costs together.
4. Anchor the comparison on the shared profit levels — gross profit, operating income, net income — after fixing uneven expense sorting.
5. Watch dates on rule changes. Example: operating leases count as debt from 2019 under both GAAP and IFRS. Otherwise a rule shift can look like a business change.

**Reference data:** IFRS adoption (as of 2020): required for domestic public companies across most of the world — Canada, Latin America, Europe, Russia, much of Africa, the Middle East, Australia, much of Asia. Non-adopters using their own standards: United States (GAAP), China, India, Japan. Standard-setters: FASB (GAAP), IASB (IFRS).

**Worked example:** Dr. Reddy's Laboratories, FY2020 (Indian Rupees millions, IFRS/Ind-AS format). Expenses are listed by nature: cost of materials consumed ₹25,565M, purchases of stock-in-trade ₹11,172M, changes in inventories ₹(999)M, employee benefits ₹20,302M, depreciation and amortisation ₹7,892M, selling and other expenses ₹33,768M. There is no COGS or SG&A line. Total income ₹125,936M − total expenses ₹98,178M = profit before tax ₹27,758M. Tax splits into current ₹4,839M and deferred ₹(6,458)M, giving profit for the year ₹29,377M. To compare Dr. Reddy's gross margin with a US pharma, the analyst must first rebuild a COGS line from the nature-based items — a judgment call the statement does not make for you.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: identifying the governing standard from listing country. JUDGMENT: remapping nature-based expenses to functional categories, and deciding which adjustments make two firms' profit lines comparable.

**Pitfalls:** Assuming reported gross or operating margins are comparable across firms without checking how expenses were categorized. Treating "different standard" as "different economics" — the end game (gross profit, operating income, net income) is the same everywhere. Missing that coarse expense groupings (nature-based or catch-all SG&A) hide classification choices.
