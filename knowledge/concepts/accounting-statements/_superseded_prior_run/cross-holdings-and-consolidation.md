# Cross holdings: consolidation and minority stakes

**Core idea:** Firms earn income outside their core business. Some comes from cash parked in traded securities. Some comes from stakes in other companies. How a stake is reported depends on its size and intent. A stake over 50% forces full consolidation. The parent counts 100% of the unit's revenues, costs and operating income as its own, even though it owns less than all of it. The part not owned shows up as minority (non-controlling) interest. That is a line on the liability side, and a cut from net income. A stake under 50% is not rolled in. The stake itself sits as an asset. The parent's share of the holding's income appears as one line — equity income — below operating income. Value shown depends on intent. Held for trading → market value. Long-term holding → book value. Traded security holdings are now almost always marked to market.

**Formulas:**
- Net income to parent shareholders = Consolidated net income − Income belonging to non-controlling interests.
- Equity income (minority stake) = Ownership share × Holding's net income (equity method).

**Procedure:**
1. List the firm's stakes in other companies from the footnotes.
2. For each stake, apply the size rule. Over 50% → consolidate: 100% of revenues, costs and operating income go into the parent's statements. The unowned part becomes non-controlling interest. Under 50% → show the stake as an asset; report the income share as an equity-income line.
3. For minority stakes, apply the intent rule. Held for trading → mark to market. Long-term investment → book value.
4. When reading the income statement, treat equity income as non-operating. It sits below operating income. It does not belong in operating margins.
5. When reading net income, strip the non-controlling-interest share. That gives income to the parent's own shareholders.
6. Remember that full consolidation swells the parent's revenues and operating income with amounts it does not fully own. Adjust before valuing.

**Reference data:**

| Stake | Treatment | Statement effect |
|---|---|---|
| >50% (majority) | Full consolidation | 100% of sub's revenues, expenses, operating income; minority interest on liability side; NCI deduction from net income |
| <50%, long-term | Asset at book value; equity method | Equity income line below operating income |
| <50%, held for trading | Asset marked to market | Value changes flow through |
| Traded securities | Marked to market | Interest/investment income line |

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola 2019 ($ millions). Equity method investments of 19,025 sit on the balance sheet — mostly minority stakes in bottlers. The income statement shows "Equity income (loss) — net" of 1,049 below operating income: Coke's share of bottler profits. At the bottom, consolidated net income 8,985 − 65 of non-controlling interests = 8,920 to Coca-Cola shareowners. Toyota shows the same pattern at scale: investments in affiliated companies of ¥4.1 trillion, equity in affiliate earnings of ¥271,152M in FY2020, and ¥66,146M subtracted for non-controlling interests.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: given ownership percent and intent, the reporting treatment follows; the NCI subtraction is arithmetic. JUDGMENT: classifying "held for trading" vs "long-term," and unwinding consolidation when the analyst wants only the parent's true economic share.

**Pitfalls:** Reading rolled-in revenues and operating income as fully owned. Putting equity income inside operating profit. Forgetting that the minority-interest line exists only because a unit was rolled in. Missing that a stake at book value can hide large market value, or large losses.
