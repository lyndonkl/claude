# Book equity: what it is and why to distrust it

**Core idea:** Under old accounting, shareholders' equity told the firm's whole history: equity brought in at founding, plus equity raised over time, plus cumulated retained earnings. Under new accounting, it also carries the jumbled mess of mark-to-market rules. Damodaran's cynical view: either way, book equity has little hope of measuring the intrinsic value of equity, and accounting's quest to make it do so does more damage than good. Five caveats govern how to read it. (1) Par value is a throwback; ignore it. (2) Age matters: young firms have far less book equity than old firms of equal market value, because equity builds from history. (3) Capitalization rules skew it: only capitalized spending becomes assets, so expensed investment (R&D, brand) never reaches book equity. (4) Buybacks and dividends both cut book equity, and buybacks are now so large that their effect dominates. (5) Book equity can go negative — after long losses, big buybacks, or write-offs — with no math or law against it.

**Formulas:** Book equity ≈ Paid-in capital (common stock + capital surplus) + Retained earnings − Treasury stock ± Accumulated other comprehensive income. (Treasury stock = cumulative cost of shares bought back. Retained earnings = cumulated net income minus cumulated dividends.)

**Procedure:**
1. Break the equity block into its parts: par value (ignore), paid-in capital, retained earnings, treasury stock, accumulated other comprehensive income, non-controlling interests.
2. Read the firm's history from the mix. Paid-in heavy → equity came from share sales (young firms). Retained-earnings heavy → equity came from profits (old firms).
3. Check treasury stock. A large negative line means heavy buybacks; it depresses book equity without saying anything about value.
4. If book equity is negative, diagnose the cause: long losses, big buybacks, or write-offs. Negative book equity does not by itself mean the equity is worthless.
5. Never use raw book equity as a value estimate. Compare book equity across firms only after noting age, capitalization policy, and buyback history.

**Reference data:** None beyond the decomposition above.

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola, Dec 31, 2019 ($ millions). Common stock at $0.25 par: 1,760 (ignore the par figure). Capital surplus: 17,154. Reinvested earnings: 65,855 — a century of retained profits. Accumulated other comprehensive loss: (13,544). Treasury stock: (52,244) from decades of buybacks. Net equity to shareowners: 18,981. So a firm with $65.9B of cumulated retained profits shows only $19.0B of book equity, mostly because buybacks stripped $52.2B out. Contrast Peloton at June 30, 2019: accumulated deficit of $(629.5)M and a total stockholders' deficit of $(538.6)M — negative book equity from losses, at a firm about to IPO at a multi-billion valuation. Toyota (FY2020) shows the old-firm pattern: retained earnings of ¥23.4 trillion against paid-in capital under ¥0.9 trillion, less ¥3.1 trillion of treasury stock.

**Determinism:** DETERMINISTIC: the roll-forward arithmetic (retained earnings grow by net income less dividends; treasury stock grows with buyback cost) and the sum of the parts. JUDGMENT: what the mix says about the firm's history, and how far book equity sits from intrinsic value — which needs the firm's age, what it expenses vs capitalizes, and its payout history.

**Pitfalls:** Using par value for anything. Reading low or negative book equity as distress without checking whether buybacks or write-offs caused it. Comparing book equity of a young firm to an old firm's. Forgetting that expensed intangible investment never enters book equity, so R&D-heavy firms look equity-poor. Treating book equity as intrinsic value.
