# Sector and industry differences in financial statements

**Core idea:** The same accounting standards govern every company, yet statements look very different across sectors. Three sectors break the standard template hardest. Commodity companies carry sector-specific exploration and extraction costs, huge depletion charges, and inventory values that swing with commodity prices. Financial service companies break the template completely: for a bank, debt is not a source of capital but raw material, so interest expense is an operating cost netted into revenue, and the debt-versus-equity distinction loses meaning. Pharmaceutical and R&D-driven companies show tiny accounting intangibles because their real asset — the drug pipeline — is expensed rather than capitalized. On the balance sheet the divergences appear as tangible-versus-intangible mix, and for acquisitive firms as goodwill.

**Formulas:**
- Commodity income statement: `Revenues from sales = Sales − Excise taxes`. Excise taxes collected on the firm's behalf are stripped out before revenue.
- Bank revenue lines are nets, not gross: `Net interest income = Interest income − Interest expense`; `Net fee income = Fee income − Fee expense`.
- Bank operating profit: `Operating profit = Net operating income after credit impairments − Total operating expenses`, where `Net operating income = Total operating income − Payments to policyholders − Expected credit losses`.
- Bank leverage: `Equity / Total assets`. A thin ratio is structural, not a warning.
- R&D coverage gap: `Accounting intangibles / Market value of equity`, tiny at pharma and technology firms.

**Reference data:** What to look for by sector.

| Sector | Income statement signature | Balance sheet signature | Cash flow signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commodity (oil, gas, mining) | Excise taxes deducted from sales; exploration costs a separate line; depreciation, depletion and impairment very large | Value in tangible assets: PP&E and reserves; inventories swing with commodity prices; currency translation adjustments in equity | Enormous DD&A add-back; routine divestitures every year |
| Financial services (banks) | Revenue reported as net interest income and net fee income; interest expense is an operating cost; credit-loss provision is a recurring operating expense | Almost entirely financial assets: loans, trading assets, derivatives, investments; deposits are the largest liability; equity a thin slice; negligible PP&E | Operating section dominated by changes in balance-sheet financial items that *are* the business; operating/investing/financing split means little |
| Pharmaceutical / R&D-driven | Under IFRS/Ind-AS, expenses listed by nature; a broad "selling and other expenses" catch-all | Small goodwill and other intangibles relative to firm value; low borrowings, large equity | Much of "investing" is parking cash in financial investments, not physical reinvestment |
| Manufacturer with a captive finance arm | Financing revenues and cost of financing operations reported separately from product sales | Large current and non-current finance receivables | Finance-receivable originations and collections blur the operating/investing split |

**Procedure:**
1. Identify the sector before reading any number.
2. For a commodity firm, run five checks. Strip excise taxes to get true revenues. Locate the exploration and extraction cost treatment. Check whether DD&A includes depletion and impairment. Check how inventory is valued and how sensitive it is to prices. Count divestitures over several years before treating any as one-time.
3. For a financial service firm: do not look for revenues minus COGS. Take net interest income plus net fee income plus trading income as the top line. Keep interest expense inside operations. Treat the credit-loss provision as a recurring operating expense, never as extraordinary. Do not apply a debt-to-capital ratio; deposits and debt are raw material.
4. For a pharma or R&D firm: expect accounting intangibles far below true value. Check whether the filer groups expenses by nature and re-map them before comparing margins. Separate real reinvestment from cash parked in financial investments.
5. For a manufacturer with a captive finance arm: split the finance business out. Its revenues, costs, receivables and cash flows follow bank logic, not manufacturing logic.
6. Whatever the sector, still land on the three anchors — gross profit, operating income, net income — and say what you had to assume to get there.
7. For banks especially, treat the cash flow statement as low-information. The operating/investing/financing split means little when balance-sheet changes are the business.

**Worked example:** HSBC, 2019 ($ millions). The bank reports no COGS and no gross profit. Net interest income of 30,462 is already a net of 54,695 income against (24,233) expense. Total operating income of 71,024 falls to net operating income before credit impairments of 56,098 after payments to policyholders of (14,926), then to 53,342 after expected credit losses of (2,756). Subtract total operating expenses of (42,349), which include the goodwill impairment of (7,349), to get operating profit of 10,993. Add share of profit in associates and joint ventures 2,354 to reach profit before tax of 13,347, and subtract tax of (4,639) for profit of 8,708. Apply the standard template here and you get nonsense. HSBC's "interest expense" is a cost of goods. Its "debt" is inventory. Its equity of 192,668 against assets of 2,715,152 is not a 7% leverage warning; it is the normal structure of a bank.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: every arithmetic step in each sector's statement; the excise-tax deduction; the net interest and net fee calculations; the bank leverage ratio; segment reconciliations.
- JUDGMENT: five decisions. How to define a bank's revenues and its debt. How exposed the firm is to the commodity cycle. How to separate a captive finance arm's flows from the manufacturing business. How to tell real reinvestment apart from cash moved in and out of financial investments. How to map nature-based expense lines onto functional categories. All five need the business description, the segment note and the accounting-policy footnote.

**Pitfalls:**
- Applying non-financial ratios to banks. Debt-to-capital, EBITDA and enterprise value all break down when debt is raw material.
- Treating a bank's loan-loss provision as a one-time charge. It is a recurring operating expense.
- Using a commodity firm's gross sales figure. Excise taxes are collected for the state and do not belong in revenue.
- Treating routine divestitures at a commodity firm as extraordinary. Total's divestments ran 2,060 / 7,239 / 5,264 over 2019/2018/2017.
- Concluding that a pharma firm has few intangibles because the balance sheet shows few. R&D is expensed.
- Reading a captive finance arm's receivable originations as capital investment in the manufacturing business.
- Assuming standards are diverging. The convergence is real; the differences that remain are sector-driven and presentational.
