# Investing cash flows and reinvestment

**Core idea:** The investing section records what the firm spent to build its future, net of what it sold. It covers two different things that must not be mixed. Investment in operating assets — capital expenditures net of divestitures, plus cash acquisitions of other companies — is the engine that drives growth in revenues and operating income. Investment in non-operating assets — financial assets, cross-holdings, other non-operating assets — creates a separate source of value whose payoff appears below the operating income line. One carve-out matters: liquid, close-to-riskless securities count as cash and marketable securities, not as investing. And deals paid for with stock never appear here, because no cash changes hands.

**Formulas:**
- `CF from Investing = − Capital Expenditures + Divestitures of assets − Cash Acquisitions − Investments in financial assets − Investments in non-operating assets + Divestitures of securities & non-operating assets`.
- `Net Operating Reinvestment = Capital Expenditures − Divestitures of operating assets + Cash Acquisitions`. This is the figure that drives operating growth.
- `Net Capital Expenditure = Capital Expenditures − Depreciation and Amortization`. A firm reinvesting only its depreciation is maintaining, not growing.
- `Reinvestment Rate = Net Operating Reinvestment / After-tax Operating Income`.
- Non-operating investment = the remaining lines; its return shows up as interest income, equity income or investment gains, not in operating income.

**Reference data:** The investing-section build-up.

| Cash flow effect | Item | Group |
|---|---|---|
| Minus | Capital Expenditures | Investment in operating assets |
| Plus | Divestitures of assets | Investment in operating assets |
| Minus | Cash Acquisitions | Investment in operating assets |
| Minus | Investments in financial assets | Investment in non-operating assets |
| Minus | Investments in non-operating assets | Investment in non-operating assets |
| Plus | Divestitures of securities & non-operating assets | Investment in non-operating assets |
| Equals | **Cash flow from Investing** | |

Classification rules:

| Item | Classification | Where the payoff shows up |
|---|---|---|
| Capex, net of divestitures (internal) | Operating investment | Revenues and operating income |
| Cash acquisitions of other firms (external) | Operating investment | Revenues and operating income |
| Financial assets, cross-holdings | Non-operating investment | Below operating income: income from cross holdings or securities |
| Liquid, near-riskless securities | Neither — cash & marketable securities | Interest income |
| Stock-funded acquisitions | Absent from the statement | Consolidated results from the deal date |

**Procedure:**
1. List every line in the investing section with its sign.
2. Sort each into operating investment, non-operating investment, or near-cash. Use the payoff test: does the return show up in operating income, or below it?
3. Sum the operating group to get net operating reinvestment. This is the number that feeds growth forecasts.
4. Compare capital expenditures with depreciation. Capex barely above D&A means maintenance-only reinvestment.
5. Look for growth bought rather than built. When cash acquisitions dwarf capex, the firm's growth is acquisition-driven, and its future depends on continuing to buy.
6. Check the operating section for hidden reinvestment. Content additions, capitalized software and originated receivables may be classified as operating by the filer, but they are reinvestment. Move them.
7. Scan the financing and equity sections and the acquisitions footnote for stock-funded deals. They will not appear in this section at all.
8. Note whether the non-operating investment lines are just cash management. Buying and selling short-term investments in near-equal amounts is parking cash, not reinvesting.

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola, 2019 ($ millions). Purchases of property, plant and equipment were (2,054), while acquisitions of businesses, equity method investments and nonmarketable securities were (5,542). Net investing was (3,976), after inflows from divestitures and maturing investments. Growth here is bought, not built: the acquisition spend is 2.7 times the organic capex. Compare Netflix in 2019 ($ thousands), where the investing section shows only (387,064) — a rounding error against additions to streaming content assets of (13,916,683) sitting inside the *operating* section. Read Netflix's investing section alone and you would conclude the firm barely reinvests. Compare Dr. Reddy's FY2020 (₹ millions): net investing (16,892) includes PP&E (4,262) and intangibles (476), but also purchases of investments (122,726) largely offset by sale proceeds of 109,186. Most of that traffic is cash management, not reinvestment.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: the signed addition of the six line types; net operating reinvestment once lines are sorted; net capital expenditure; the reinvestment rate given after-tax operating income.
- JUDGMENT: classifying each asset as operating or non-operating; deciding whether an operating-section item is really reinvestment; separating genuine reinvestment from cash parked in financial investments; identifying stock-funded deals. This needs the accounting-policy footnote, the acquisitions footnote and the investments schedule.

**Pitfalls:**
- Reading the investing section total as reinvestment. It mixes operating investment, cash management and divestitures.
- Missing reinvestment that the filer classified as operating. Netflix's content spend is the standard example.
- Treating a firm's investing outflow as small when the buying is done with stock.
- Counting purchases of near-riskless liquid securities as investing outflows. They belong with cash.
- Ignoring divestitures. At commodity firms they are routine, so gross capex overstates reinvestment badly.
