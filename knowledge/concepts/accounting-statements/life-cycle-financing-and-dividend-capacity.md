# Life-cycle financing choices and dividend capacity

**Core idea:** A firm's funding needs and its ability to meet them internally move in opposite directions over its life. External funding needs start high, though early on they are constrained by the lack of infrastructure to raise money. They fall as the firm matures. Internal financing — cash generated by operations — starts negative and eventually exceeds funding needs. The gap between the two determines two things at once: which external financing sources fit the firm, and how much capacity it has to pay dividends. Capacity to pay is the gap, not the profit. Young firms have none. Capacity first appears in high growth, rises through mature growth, and becomes high in decline, when cash generated exceeds funding needs. That last stage is the harvesting phase.

**Formulas:**
- `Funding gap = External funding needs − Internal financing`. Internal financing = cash generated by operations.
- `Capacity to pay dividends = Internal financing − Funding needs`. Negative or zero means no capacity, regardless of accounting profit.
- Practical proxy from the statements: `Capacity ≈ Cash Flow from Operations − Net Operating Reinvestment` (see [[potential-dividends-fcfe]] for the full build).
- `Payout capacity utilization = (Dividends + Buybacks) / Capacity`. Above 1 means the payout is funded from the cash balance or from borrowing.

**Reference data:** Financing choices across the life cycle.

| Stage | External funding needs | Internal financing | Appropriate external financing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Start-up | High, but constrained by infrastructure | Negative or low | Owner's equity, bank debt |
| 2. Rapid Expansion | High, relative to firm value | Negative or low | Venture capital, common stock |
| 3. High Growth | Moderate, relative to firm value | Low, relative to funding needs | Common stock, warrants, convertibles |
| 4. Mature Growth | Declining, as a percent of firm value | High, relative to funding needs | Debt |
| 5. Decline | Low, as projects dry up | More than funding needs | Retire debt, repurchase stock |

Financing transitions between stages, in order: accessing private equity → initial public offering → seasoned equity issue → bond issues.

Dividend capacity across the life cycle.

| Stage | External funding needs | Internal financing | Capacity to pay dividends |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Start-up | High, but constrained by infrastructure | Negative or low | None |
| 2. Rapid Expansion | High, relative to firm value | Negative or low | None |
| 3. High Growth | Moderate, relative to firm value | Low, relative to funding needs | Very low |
| 4. Mature Growth | Low, as projects dry up | High, relative to funding needs | Increasing |
| 5. Decline | Low, as projects dry up | More than funding needs | High |

**Procedure:**
1. Place the firm on the life cycle first, using [[life-cycle-patterns-in-financial-statements]].
2. Measure internal financing: cash flow from operations, for three or more years.
3. Measure funding needs: net operating reinvestment — capex less divestitures plus cash acquisitions — plus the working capital build.
4. Compute the gap. That gap, not net income, is the dividend capacity.
5. Compare the gap with actual dividends and buybacks. Payout above capacity is being financed from the cash balance or from new debt, and cannot last indefinitely.
6. Check that the observed external financing matches the stage. A start-up funding itself with bond issues, or a declining firm issuing equity, is a mismatch worth explaining.
7. Read the financing section of the cash flow statement as confirmation. Equity inflows at a young firm, debt inflows in mature growth, and debt retirement plus buybacks in decline are the expected signatures.
8. Do not expect a young firm to pay dividends, and do not read a mature firm's high payout as generosity. Both follow from the funding gap.

**Worked example:** Coca-Cola, 2019 ($ millions) — a stage 4–5 firm. Internal financing was operating cash flow of 10,471. Funding needs were PP&E purchases 2,054 plus acquisitions 5,542 = 7,596. Capacity to pay dividends is 10,471 − 7,596 = 2,875. Actual payout was dividends 6,845 plus buybacks 1,103 = 7,948. Utilization is 7,948 / 2,875 = 2.8 times capacity. The shortfall came out of the cash balance, which fell from 9,077 to 6,480, while debt was merely rolled over (issuances 23,009 against payments 24,850). Contrast Peloton in FY2019: operating cash (108.6) against investing of (297.5), so capacity is deeply negative, and the firm paid no dividend and raised 539.1 million of preferred stock instead. Exactly the stage-2 pattern the table predicts.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: internal financing from the cash flow statement; funding needs from the investing section and the working-capital lines; the gap; the utilization ratio.
- JUDGMENT: forecasting future funding needs, which requires a view on the firm's investment opportunities; assessing whether the current financing mix fits the stage; deciding whether a payout above capacity is a temporary choice or a structural problem. This needs the life-cycle placement, the investment pipeline and the firm's stated policy.

**Pitfalls:**
- Equating dividend capacity with net income. Capacity is the gap between internal cash and funding needs, and a profitable growth firm can have none.
- Expecting a young firm to have access to the financing that suits its needs. Early external funding is constrained by lack of infrastructure, not just by appetite.
- Reading a high payout as strength. In decline it is the correct policy, and it can also be an overpayment funded from the cash balance.
- Ignoring buybacks when measuring payout against capacity.
- Assuming the sequence is one-way. A mature firm that finds a new market re-enters the growth financing pattern.
