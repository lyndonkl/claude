# Deal bias: verdict first, trial afterwards

**Core idea:** In most failed acquisitions the price came first and the valuation came second. A CEO wants the deal. Bankers on both sides produce fairness opinions blessing it. Competitors are doing deals, so abstaining feels dangerous. The valuation is then reverse-engineered to justify a number already chosen. Damodaran's diagnosis is that premiums often have nothing to do with synergy, control or strategy, whatever the stated reason — they reflect CEO ego. Overconfident CEOs make more acquisitions and leave a trail across the firms they run. The related failure is the winner's curse: if you define winning a bidding war as the objective, you will win, and you will overpay to do it.

**Formulas:** No formula. Three diagnostic tests:
- Chronology test: compare the date the price was first discussed against the date the valuation was completed. Valuation after price means verdict first.
- Sensitivity test: examine which input was flexed to reach the price. If growth, margin or the discount rate was adjusted until value met price, the model was fitted to a conclusion.
- Accountability test: name the individual whose compensation depends on the promised benefits arriving. No name means no delivery.

**Procedure:**
1. Reconstruct the deal's chronology from board minutes, pitch books and model file dates.
2. Identify who is pushing the deal and what they gain from it — tenure, scale, reputation, bonus.
3. Read the fairness opinion for what it is: a document commissioned by a party that wants the deal, and paid for on completion.
4. Shift the burden of proof. Those advocating a premium must demonstrate the value exists. Skeptics do not have to prove it does not. Buzz words rotate by decade — "synergy" in the 1980s, "strategic considerations" in the 1990s, "real options" in the 2000s — and each can have value, but the advocate must show it.
5. Reject competitive-pressure arguments. If staying competitive in a business requires making bad investments, the right answer may be to exit that business.
6. Set a walk-away price before the auction starts, and drop out when it is exceeded. The best move in a bidding war is to leave it.
7. Tie advisor compensation to how well the deal performs, not to getting it done.
8. Assign delivery accountability to the deal's loudest champion.

**Reference data:**

Bidding-war outcomes, Malmendier, Moretti & Peters (2011) — market-adjusted cumulative abnormal returns around the contest:

| Months after bidding war | Winners' CAR | Losers' CAR |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | ~0% | ~0% |
| +36 to +40 | ~−25% to −30% | ~+25% to +30% |

Losing a bidding war is better for shareholders than winning one. This is the winner's curse measured directly.

**Worked example:** HP's acquisition of Autonomy. HP paid $11.1 billion, a $5.2 billion premium over Autonomy's $5.9 billion market value. Nearly everyone thought HP had overpaid. CEO Leo Apotheker's defence invoked process, not value. He said HP has "a pretty rigorous process... for all our acquisitions, which is a D.C.F.-based model" taking "a very conservative view." He stressed that Autonomy would be "on Day 1, accretive to H.P." He added that the analysis was done "at great length, in great detail," that the price was "very fair," and that it would give "a great return to our shareholders."

Every element of that defence is a rationalization pattern rather than evidence. A DCF can be fitted to any price. Conservatism is unverifiable. Day-1 accretion is guaranteed whenever the target's PE is lower. A year later HP wrote off most of the deal, with $4,451 million attributed to a premium paid for non-existent synergy.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: nothing in the verdict. The supporting statistics are computable — the premium as a percentage of market value, the CAR of winners versus losers, the input change needed to close the gap between value and price.
- JUDGMENT: whether the process was biased. This needs the chronology, the incentive structure of the participants, the advisor fee terms, and the number of rival bidders.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating a fairness opinion as independent evidence.
- Reading "we ran a rigorous DCF" as a substitute for showing the inputs.
- Entering an auction without a pre-set walk-away price.
- Framing abstention as weakness. Losing bidders outperform winners by 50-60 percentage points of CAR over three years.
- Paying advisors on completion, which buys you advice to complete.
- Letting blame diffuse after the fact. In the HP write-off, responsibility was spread across the old CEO, the bankers, the target's managers, the auditors and current management — which is the same as assigning it to nobody.
