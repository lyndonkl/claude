# Cost synergies versus growth synergies: delivery odds

**Core idea:** Valuing synergy and delivering it are different problems. The post-merger evidence is blunt about which kinds arrive. Cost synergies land most of the time; revenue (growth) synergies usually do not. McKinsey found 70% of mergers failed to achieve their expected revenue synergies, while a large majority hit 90% or more of expected cost savings. Three other conditions raise the odds: the synergy must be concrete and planned before the merger closes, someone must be accountable for delivering it, and you must not be one of several bidders. Competition bids the synergy into the price you pay, so you value it and then hand it to the seller.

**Formulas:**
- `Realization rate = Actual synergy delivered / Expected synergy at announcement`.
- Revenue-synergy haircut: `Adjusted revenue synergy = Gross estimate × (1 − customer attrition rate)`, with observed integration attrition typically 2%–5%.
- `Synergy retained by acquirer = Value of synergy − Premium paid`. As bidders increase, the premium rises toward the full synergy value and this goes to zero.

**Procedure:**
1. Split the synergy schedule into cost savings and revenue/growth items. Value them separately; never present one blended number.
2. For revenue synergies, apply the outside view. Check the implied growth and market share against overall market growth and competitor reactions. If the plan needs share gains no competitor will concede, cut it.
3. Haircut revenue synergies for customer losses during integration — 2% to 5% is the typical range, and ignoring it is the most common error.
4. For cost synergies, build bottom-up. Do a location-by-location or function-by-function review of overlaps. Do not use a percentage-of-cost-base rule.
5. Subtract one-time costs to achieve the savings. Firms routinely underestimate these.
6. Sanity-check every estimate against precedent transactions, and reject benchmarks drawn from non-comparable situations.
7. Test concreteness: can you name the plant, the contract, the headcount, and the month? If not, the synergy is fuzzy and should be discounted heavily or dropped.
8. Assign an owner. Synergy is far more likely to appear when a named person is held responsible for delivering it — preferably the person pushing hardest for the deal.
9. Count the bidders. If you are one of several, assume the synergy value gets competed into the price and reduce what you are willing to pay.

**Reference data:**

McKinsey (2002) Postmerger Management Practice — client survey and case studies. Percent of mergers achieving a given share of expected synergies:

| Share of expected synergies achieved | Revenue synergies (% of mergers, N=77) | Cost savings (% of mergers, N=92) |
|---|---|---|
| <30% | 23 | 3 |
| 30–50% | 5 | 5 |
| 51–60% | 13 | 1 |
| 61–70% | 6 | 4 |
| 71–80% | 14 | 12 |
| 81–90% | 8 | 13 |
| 91–100% | 13 | 25 |
| >100% | 17 | 36 |

Headline: 70% of mergers failed to achieve expected revenue synergies.

Common estimation errors:

| Revenue synergies | Cost synergies |
|---|---|
| Ignoring/underestimating customer losses from integration (typically 2%–5%) | Underestimating one-time costs to achieve |
| Growth and share targets out of line with market growth and competitive dynamics (no outside view) | Using benchmarks from noncomparable situations |
| | Not sanity-checking management estimates against precedent transactions |
| | No bottom-up analysis (e.g., location-by-location overlap review) |

Four findings on synergy odds:
1. Cost synergies are far more likely to be delivered than growth synergies.
2. Concrete synergies planned at merger time beat fuzzy ones.
3. Synergy shows up much more often when someone is held responsible for it.
4. A sole bidder keeps more of the synergy than one of several bidders.

**Worked example:** A deal claims $400M of annual synergies — $250M cost, $150M revenue. Apply the evidence. The cost piece is bottom-up and location-verified, so use the 91–100% band: expect roughly $240M, less one-time costs to achieve. The revenue piece assumes a share gain in a flat market with no attrition allowance. Apply a 3.5% customer-attrition haircut and the sub-70% realization band that covers 47 of the 77 mergers in the McKinsey sample. A defensible figure is well under half, say $60M. The credible synergy is about $300M, not $400M — and the $100M gap is exactly the kind of overstatement that becomes an overpayment. Compare with HP/Autonomy, where the post-mortem attributed $4,451M of the price to synergy that never existed.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: the realization rate given actual and expected figures; the attrition haircut given a rate; the retained-synergy subtraction given a premium.
- JUDGMENT: which realization band applies to this deal; the attrition rate inside the 2%–5% range; whether the cost plan is genuinely bottom-up; how many credible rival bidders exist. This judgment needs the integration plan, the overlap analysis, precedent transactions, and the auction structure.

**Pitfalls:**
- Presenting a single blended synergy number, which hides the fact that the fragile half is the revenue half.
- Assuming zero customer loss during integration.
- Benchmarking cost savings off a merger in a different industry or structure.
- Forgetting one-time costs, which are real cash and reduce the net benefit.
- Leaving accountability diffuse. Without an owner, the synergy is a forecast nobody will be asked about again.
- Bidding against rivals while assuming you keep the synergy. Competition transfers it to the target's shareholders.
