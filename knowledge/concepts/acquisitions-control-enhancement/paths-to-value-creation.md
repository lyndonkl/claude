# The four paths to value creation

**Core idea:** In a DCF framework there are exactly four ways to make a firm more valuable. Raise the cash flows from existing assets. Raise the expected growth rate of those cash flows. Extend the length of the high-growth period. Lower the cost of capital. Any action that touches none of these four cannot create value, however good it sounds. The framework is a filter: run every proposed initiative, restructuring plan or "value enhancement" program through it and demand to know which lever it moves and by how much. It also separates value from price. Cosmetic changes can move the stock without touching any of the four.

**Formulas:**
- `FCFF = EBIT(1−t) + Depreciation − Capital expenditures − Change in working capital`, with `EBIT = Revenues × Operating margin`.
- `Expected growth rate = Reinvestment rate × Return on capital`.
- `Return on capital = Operating margin × Capital turnover ratio`.
- `Cost of capital = Cost of equity × E/(D+E) + Pre-tax cost of debt × (1−t) × D/(D+E)`, where `E` = market value of equity and `D` = market value of debt.
- Firm value combines them: `Value = Σ FCFF_t / (1 + WACC)^t + Terminal value`, so the four levers are the only entry points.

**Procedure:**

**Lever 1 — increase cash flows from assets in place.** Work down the FCFF calculation and act on each line.
1. Raise the operating margin through more efficient operations and cost cutting.
2. Divest assets with negative EBIT — they consume value every year they are held.
3. Reduce the effective tax rate by moving income to lower-tax locales, through transfer pricing, and through risk management.
4. Reduce capital expenditure by living off past over-investment.
5. Reduce working capital with better inventory management and tighter credit policies.

**Lever 2 — increase value from expected growth.**
6. Reinvest more (more projects, or acquisitions), which raises the reinvestment rate.
7. Or earn a higher return on capital, through higher operating margins or higher capital turnover.
8. Choose a pricing strategy consistent with the ROC decomposition: a price-leader strategy runs high margin and low turnover; a volume-leader strategy runs low margin and high turnover. Both can reach the same ROC.
9. Apply game theory. Ask how competitors will react to your move, and how you will react to theirs. A margin increase that triggers a price war creates nothing.

**Lever 3 — extend the high-growth period.**
10. Identify a genuine competitive advantage. The four sources: brand name, legal protection (patents and licences), switching costs, and cost advantages.
11. Either build on an existing advantage or find a new one. Without one, excess returns get competed away and the growth period cannot be extended by assumption.

**Lever 4 — reduce the cost of capital.**
12. Reduce operating leverage through outsourcing and flexible wage contracts and cost structures.
13. Make the product or service less discretionary to customers, by changing product characteristics or through more effective advertising. This lowers business risk and the beta.
14. Move the financing mix toward the optimal debt ratio.
15. Match debt to assets, using swaps, derivatives and hybrids, to reduce default risk at any given debt level.

**Final filter.** If a proposed action does not change existing-asset cash flows, expected growth, the growth period or the cost of capital, it does not create value. It may still move the price.

**Reference data:**

The four levers and their sub-actions:

| Lever | Actions |
|---|---|
| 1. Cash flows from existing assets | Raise margins; divest negative-EBIT assets; cut the tax rate; cut cap ex; cut working capital |
| 2. Expected growth rate | Raise the reinvestment rate; raise the return on capital (margin × turnover) |
| 3. Length of high-growth period | Build brand, legal protection, switching costs, or cost advantages |
| 4. Cost of capital | Cut operating leverage; make the product less discretionary; move to the optimal debt ratio; match debt to assets |

Price versus value — evidence that price moves without value:

| Event | Market reaction |
|---|---|
| Firms adding ".com" to their names during the bubble | Cumulative abnormal returns of roughly +100% to +200% around the announcement |
| Firms dropping ".com" after the bubble burst | Price bounce again (~+20%) |

Neither change touched cash flows, growth or risk. "The market gives, and takes away."

**Worked example:** Take SAP's restructuring as an application of levers 2 and 4 together. Lever 2: raise the reinvestment rate from 57.42% to 70%, which lifts expected growth from `0.5742 × 0.1993 = 11.44%` to `0.70 × 0.1993 = 13.99%`. Lever 4: move from a near-zero debt ratio to the optimal 30%, which cuts WACC from 8.68% to 8.24% (cost of equity rises to 10.57%, but the cheaper after-tax debt at 2.80% more than offsets it). Value per share rises from €106.12 to €126.51.

Contrast with a firm renaming itself. Same cash flows, same growth, same risk — no lever moved, so no value created, whatever the stock does that week.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: FCFF from revenues, margin, tax rate, depreciation, cap ex and ΔWC; growth from reinvestment rate and ROC; ROC from margin and turnover; WACC from the component costs and weights. A script computes the value effect of any *stated* input change.
- JUDGMENT: which actions are achievable, by how much, and at what cost. Whether a competitive advantage is durable enough to extend the growth period. How competitors respond. Whether a tax-rate reduction is sustainable or a one-off.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating an action as value-creating because it raises earnings. Earnings gains bought with more capital at a sub-WACC return destroy value.
- Extending the high-growth period without naming the competitive advantage that justifies it. This is the most common way to inflate a DCF.
- Cutting cap ex to raise FCFF while assuming growth continues. Growth must be paid for.
- Ignoring competitor reactions to a margin or pricing move.
- Confusing price enhancement with value enhancement. Renaming, stock splits and cosmetic financial engineering move price, not value.
- Assuming more debt always lowers the cost of capital. Past the optimal ratio, the rating collapses and WACC rises.
