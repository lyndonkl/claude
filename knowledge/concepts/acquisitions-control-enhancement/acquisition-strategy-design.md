# Designing an acquisition strategy with better odds

**Core idea:** The base rates are against acquirers, but they are not uniform. Deal design shifts them measurably. Five choices separate the acquirers who create value from those who destroy it. Be the sole bidder rather than one of several. Buy private firms and subsidiaries rather than public companies. Pay with cash rather than stock. Buy small targets rather than large ones. Count on cost synergies rather than growth synergies. Layered on top are five disciplines about behaviour: stay inside a proven strategy, plan the benefits before closing, drop out of bidding wars, assign accountability, and pay advisors on outcomes.

**Formulas:** No formula. The strategy is a scoring profile:

| Better odds | Worse odds |
|---|---|
| Sole bidder | Bidding war |
| Private target or subsidiary | Public target |
| Pay with cash | Pay with stock |
| Small target (relative to acquirer) | Large target |
| Cost synergies | Growth synergies |

Target size is measured as `Target value / Acquirer value`, with the evidence bucketed at <5%, 5–9.99%, 10–19.99% and >20%.

**Procedure:**
1. Define the acquisition strategy narrowly and write it down: which sectors, which size range, which target type, which synergy source.
2. Screen for private companies and corporate subsidiaries first. There is less competition and more negotiating room than in public deals.
3. Keep target size small relative to your own. Deals above 20% of acquirer size have the worst outcomes, especially in stock.
4. Pay cash where you can. Stock payment signals that you think your own shares are overvalued, and the evidence on large stock deals is the worst in the sample.
5. Build the value case on cost synergies. Treat growth synergies as upside, not as the justification.
6. Before entering an auction, set a walk-away price. If a contest develops, drop out.
7. Put realistic delivery plans for synergy and control in place *before* the merger completes. Realistic means reachable magnitudes and time frames that reflect how long two organizations take to work as one.
8. Name the person accountable for delivering the benefits — preferably whoever pushed hardest for the deal.
9. Tie advisor compensation to how the deal performs, not to closing it.
10. Resist scope creep. If a strategy works, size and hubris will push you to "expand" it. That expansion is where serial acquirers break.

Note the direction reverses with size. Returns to private and subsidiary deals *rise* with target size; returns to public deals *fall*.

Bidding wars — Malmendier, Moretti & Peters (2011): winners drift to roughly −25% to −30% CAR over 40 months; losers drift to roughly +25% to +30%.

**Worked example:** An acquirer with a $10B market cap is choosing between two deals. Deal A is a public company worth $2.5B (25% of its size), contested by two rivals, to be paid in stock, justified by revenue synergies. Deal B is a corporate carve-out worth $600M (6% of its size), sole-bidder negotiation, cash, justified by plant consolidation.

Score them. Deal A hits the worse side of all five choices: bidding war, public target, stock, >20% size, growth synergies. The relevant evidence cell is stock payment for targets above 20% of acquirer size — about −4.4% announcement CAR, with winners of bidding wars drifting to −25% or worse over three years. Deal B hits the better side of all five. The relevant cell is a subsidiary target at 5–9.99% of acquirer size, about +2.9%. Deal B is not guaranteed to work, but the structural odds differ by roughly seven percentage points on announcement and far more over three years.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: target size as a percentage of acquirer value; classification of payment method and target status; looking up the matching evidence cell; computing the acquirer's own historical realization rates.
- JUDGMENT: whether a proven strategy is being stretched; whether the synergy plan is realistic in magnitude and timing; whether a private target's price advantage survives due-diligence risk; when to abandon an auction. This needs the integration plan, the bidder count, and the acquirer's own deal history.

**Pitfalls:**
- Applying the size preference without the status preference. Large deals are dangerous for *public* targets; for private and subsidiary targets, larger deals actually performed better.
- Reading "pay with cash" as a financing rule. It is a signalling and discipline rule; cash deals do not let you overpay with inflated paper.
- Building the value case on growth synergies because they are bigger on the page. They are also the ones that do not arrive.
- Letting a successful small-deal strategy expand into a transformational large deal. Scale changes the odds against you.
- Planning synergy delivery after closing. By then the negotiating leverage and the attention are gone.
