# The Five Corporatism Archetypes

**Core idea:** Damodaran classifies corporate systems into five end games. Each one describes who really controls the firm, what the firm maximizes, and how every stakeholder fares as a result. The archetypes are a diagnostic vocabulary. Name the archetype a company sits in, and you have named its governance risk, its likely agency costs, and the objective function an analyst should use for it. Four of the five are pathologies. Only the last one — constrained corporatism — is the equilibrium Damodaran endorses.

**Formulas:** None. The archetypes are defined by who holds control and what gets maximized.

**Procedure:** Classifying a real company:
1. Identify who actually controls the firm. Read the ownership register and the voting structure (see [[ownership-and-control-structure-analysis]]). Candidates: dispersed shareholders, a founder/family, the government, or the managers themselves.
2. Identify what the firm actually maximizes. Read the proxy statement's compensation metrics and the CEO's stated priorities.
3. Check the constraint set. Are there real covenants? Real antitrust enforcement? A real labor market? Real environmental enforcement?
4. Match to the archetype table below.
5. Convert the archetype into an analytical adjustment. Cutthroat and crony: expect a wealth transfer from minority shareholders, employees, customers, and society; expect regulatory/backlash risk. Managerial: expect value-destroying empire building and excess cash. Confused: expect margin and efficiency drag and no accountability. Constrained: take reported governance at face value.
6. Re-run periodically. Firms move between archetypes as CEO tenure lengthens and ownership changes.

**Reference data:** The five archetypes.

| Archetype | Who controls | Objective | Lenders | Competition | Employees | Society | Customers | End game |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Cutthroat corporatism** (the strawman) | Founder, family, or investor group with a controlling stake, via voting rights and a compliant board | Maximize founder wealth; other shareholders along for the ride | Controlled by or beholden to the founder; few or no constraints | Market power used to drive out competitors | Bargaining power used to minimize wages/benefits — paid less to do more | Laws protecting society ignored or subverted; large side costs | Market dominance used to raise prices — customers pay more | Winners dominate or monopolize, exploiting customers, employees and society while enriching founders |
| **Utopian corporatism** | Shareholders, with equal voting rights | Maximize stock price | Fully protected, explicitly by covenant or implicitly by reputation | Play to win with better products or lower prices; sector winnowed to the best | Unions or a strong labor market even the game; fair wages | All costs traced and charged to the firm; society bears nothing | Treated well so they come back; they get a good deal | Stock price maximization also maximizes stockholder wealth, all stakeholders get their due, society and the economy gain |
| **Managerial corporatism** | Managers. Shareholders small, dispersed and powerless; board is a rubber stamp; annual meetings scripted and ineffective | Managerial interests, delivering just enough to other stakeholders to neuter them | Banks and bondholders collect interest and principal, leave decisions to managers | Larger manager-dominated firms; competitive enough to be profitable, not aggressive | Bought off with wage contracts and benefits just good enough | Societal action only when it also improves managerial standing | Served only where their interests converge with managers' | Survivors are the firms that keep managers happy; other stakeholders fare well or badly by coincidence |
| **Crony corporatism** | Founder, family, or government official with a controlling stake and a compliant board; rule writers co-opted or corrupted | Maximize founder wealth, with government officials benefiting | Often government banks; few or no constraints | Reduced or eliminated; the government tilts the field | Government sides with the company; paid less to do more | Rules rewritten or unenforced; competition and monopoly law not enforced; large side costs, few benefits | Pay higher prices | The most politically connected firms dominate or monopolize, exploiting customers, employees and society |
| **Confused corporatism** (stakeholder wealth maximization) | Shareholders own but share control; the board promotes stakeholder interests over shareholder interests | Maximize "stakeholder wealth" | Paid only if payment does not endanger other stakeholders; rights enforced after balancing | Holds back on its own advantages; gains share but avoids dominance | Unions or a strong labor market even the game; living wage regardless of profitability or competitive effect | Avoids social costs and actively creates benefits — protect society at any cost | Holds back on pricing power; maximizes satisfaction even without repeat business or profit | Trying to serve all stakeholders, none are served; managers face no accountability; firms get less competitive and efficient |
| **Constrained corporatism** (endorsed) | Shareholders with equal voting rights; the board checks the CEO; shareholders vote at annual meetings | Maximize shareholder wealth, subject to constraints — external or self-imposed | Covenants restrict corporate actions; the firm trades that freedom for cheaper debt | Sector winnowed to the best; play to win with better products or lower prices | Unions or a strong labor market even the game; fair wages | Minimize societal costs, add to societal benefits; act as a good corporate citizen | Treated well because the firm wants repeat customers; good value for money | Winners maximize shareholder wealth *while* being good corporate citizens, protecting employees, and delivering value to customers |

**Worked example:** Vale (Brazil, circa 2010s). Common (voting) shares 3,172 million, preferred (non-voting) shares 1,933 million. Valespar holds 54% of the common and therefore controls the vote, while owning only about 1% of the preferred. The Brazilian government holds golden (veto) shares and 6% of common. Ten of eleven board members were nominated by Valepar, and the board chair was Valepar's own CEO. Control sits with a minority economic stake plus a state veto. Archetype: crony corporatism. Analytical consequence: the analyst should not assume Vale's cash flows accrue to minority holders, and should expect politically motivated pricing and investment.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: computing voting control from share classes and holdings (share counts, class voting rights, holder stakes -> % of votes controlled); counting board seats nominated by the controlling holder.
- JUDGMENT: the archetype label itself. Every cell in the table requires reading evidence about behavior, not just structure. Two firms with identical ownership structures can sit in different archetypes.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating cutthroat corporatism as what shareholder wealth maximization actually means. The source presents it explicitly as a *strawman* caricature.
- Reading the stakeholder model as automatically better than the shareholder model. Confused corporatism is a pathology, not an ideal.
- Confusing utopian with constrained corporatism. Utopian assumes the constraints hold by themselves. Constrained assumes they must be imposed and paid for.
- Assuming legal form determines the archetype. A US-listed firm can sit in crony corporatism through a VIE or dual-class structure.
