# Alternative Objective Functions (Solution II)

**Core idea:** The second fix for a broken objective is to replace it. Two versions exist. Version IIa swaps the stock price for another metric — earnings, revenues, firm size, market share, or EVA. These are all *intermediate* objectives. They work where they correlate with the long-term health and value of the company, and they produce disasters where they do not. Version IIb swaps the constituency, maximizing the collective wealth of all stakeholders instead of shareholders. It sounds fair and has powerful backers, including the 2019 Business Roundtable. Damodaran's verdict is harsh: a firm that tries to serve every stakeholder serves none, and it destroys accountability along the way.

**Formulas:** No formula. The test that governs every intermediate metric:

```
An intermediate metric M is an acceptable objective
  IF AND ONLY IF M correlates with long-term firm value.

Where corr(M, long-term value) is high  -> M works well
Where corr(M, long-term value) is low   -> the firm can end up
                                            with a disaster
```

**Procedure:** Evaluating a proposed alternative objective at a real company:
1. Read the compensation plan and the investor-day deck. Find the metric the firm actually maximizes. Common answers: EPS, revenue growth, market share, EBITDA, EVA, "total shareholder return," or a stakeholder scorecard.
2. If the metric is intermediate, test the correlation. Ask what a manager would do to maximize *that number alone*, and check whether those actions raise or lower long-term value.
3. Look for the classic decoupling cases. Market share bought by cutting price below cost. Revenue growth bought by loosening credit terms. EPS growth bought by buying back stock with expensive debt. Earnings growth bought by underinvesting.
4. If the firm has adopted a stakeholder objective, find out how conflicts are resolved. If there is no tiebreaker rule, there is no accountability. That is the diagnostic.
5. Check the two cautionary comparators below. A firm with a laundry list of objectives and no ranking is heading toward the government-enterprise outcome.
6. Report which archetype the firm's objective places it in (see [[corporatism-archetypes]]) and adjust your expectations for margins, efficiency and accountability.

**Reference data:**

*IIa — Intermediate objective functions.* Candidates the source names: maximizing earnings; maximizing revenues; maximizing firm size; maximizing market share; maximizing EVA (Economic Value Added). The rule for all of them is the correlation test above.

*IIb — The three drivers behind stakeholder wealth maximization:*
1. **A fairness argument.** Shareholder wealth maximization seems, at least at first sight, to put every other stakeholder in the back seat. That seems unfair.
2. **An easy fix?** The logical response seems to be stakeholder wealth maximization — maximize the collective wealth of all stakeholders. That is its promise.
3. **A protective response.** Corporations have been losing the battle for public opinion. Many CEOs, and even some institutional investors, have bought into the idea, partly as self-protection.

*The Business Roundtable statement (2019).* Each company serves its own corporate purpose, but the signatories share "a fundamental commitment to all of our stakeholders." They commit to:
- **Delivering value to customers** — meeting or exceeding customer expectations.
- **Investing in employees** — fair compensation, benefits, training and education, diversity and inclusion, dignity and respect.
- **Dealing fairly and ethically with suppliers** — serving as good partners to companies large and small.
- **Supporting communities** — respecting people and protecting the environment through sustainable practices.
- **Generating long-term value for shareholders**, who provide the capital for investment, growth and innovation, with transparency and effective engagement.

*Two cautionary comparators for multi-objective management:*

| Case | What happened |
|---|---|
| **Government-owned companies** | Managers got a laundry list of objectives, much like the stakeholder list, and were told to deliver on all of them. The result was some of the most inefficient companies on earth. Every stakeholder group felt ill-served. |
| **US research universities** | They lack a central focus. Whose interests dominate shifts depending on who you talk to and when. The result is economically inefficient operations that can run deficits no matter how much tuition is collected. Every stakeholder group feels aggrieved. |

*The end game — Confused Corporatism.* Shareholders own the company but share control with other stakeholders. The board promotes stakeholder interests over shareholder interests. Lenders get paid only if payment does not endanger other stakeholders. The firm holds back on its own competitive advantages and on its pricing power. It maximizes customer satisfaction even when that does not translate into repeat business or profits. It protects society's interests at any cost. Employees earn a living wage regardless of profitability or competitive effect. Result: in trying to serve all stakeholders, none are served. Managers face no accountability, and the company becomes less competitive and less efficient. Full detail in [[corporatism-archetypes]].

**Worked example:** Market share as an objective. A retailer targets market share and wins it by pricing below cost, funded by debt. Market share rises from 12% to 18% over three years. Revenue grows 40%. Every intermediate metric the firm reports improves. Meanwhile operating margin goes from +4% to -2%, the debt ratio doubles, and firm value falls. The correlation test fails: market share and long-term value moved in opposite directions. The metric was maximized and the company was damaged. That is the precise failure mode the source warns about.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: identifying which metric compensation is tied to (comp plan -> metric list and weights); computing the metric itself; measuring the historical correlation between the metric and firm value for this company or its sector.
- JUDGMENT: whether the correlation will hold going forward; whether a stakeholder commitment is genuine or a public-relations position; how much efficiency drag to expect from a multi-objective firm; whether the fairness argument should change your analysis at all.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating an intermediate metric as the objective rather than as a proxy for it. The source's whole point is that proxies break.
- Assuming that because a metric worked historically it will keep working. The correlation is contingent, not structural.
- Reading the Business Roundtable statement as settling the debate. The source presents it as the real-world embodiment of stakeholder maximization, then argues that the embodiment fails.
- Missing the accountability cost. A manager answerable to everyone is answerable to no one.
- Confusing "maximize shareholder wealth subject to constraints" with stakeholder maximization. They are different archetypes with different end games. The first is constrained corporatism, which the source endorses.
