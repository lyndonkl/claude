# Self-Correction and the Counter-Forces (Solution III)

**Core idea:** Damodaran's answer to the four broken links is not to abandon stock price maximization. It is to keep the objective and fix the linkages. The argument rests on an empirical claim: the system self-corrects. Excesses on any linkage, left unregulated, trigger counter-actions that reduce or eliminate those excesses. Managers who abused stockholders produced a far more active market for corporate control. Stockholders who abused bondholders taught bondholders to protect themselves at issue. Firms that misled markets made markets more skeptical and more punitive. Firms that created social costs produced more regulation, plus investor and customer backlashes. An analyst's job is to check whether each counter-force is actually present at this company, because where it is absent the excess persists.

**Formulas:** No arithmetic. The four counter-forces, paired with the four failures:

```
Failure I  (stockholders vs managers)  -> COUNTER: activist investors
                                           and hostile takeovers put
                                           managers of poorly run firms
                                           on notice
Failure II (stockholders vs bondholders)-> COUNTER: covenants and new
                                           bond types
Failure III (firms vs markets)          -> COUNTER: firms punished for
                                           misleading markets; investors
                                           and analysts more skeptical
Failure IV (firms vs society)           -> COUNTER: "corporate good
                                           citizen" constraints via more
                                           laws and investor/customer
                                           backlash
```

Solution III's prescription, stated positively: maximize stock prices but minimize side costs. Make managers and employees into stockholders. Protect lenders from expropriation. Provide honest and prompt information to financial markets. Minimize social costs.

**Procedure:** Checking whether the counter-forces are live at a real company:
1. **Stockholder counter-force.** Look for activist positions (13D filings), for a private-equity or activist holder on the register, and for say-on-pay or director votes with high dissent. Check whether the firm is takeable — see the target profile below. Then check whether the entrenchment package blocks a bid (see [[managerial-entrenchment-and-takeover-defenses]]).
2. **Bondholder counter-force.** Read the covenants. Check for puttable bonds and ratings-sensitive notes. See [[stockholder-bondholder-conflict]].
3. **Market counter-force.** Check analyst coverage, options market depth, short interest and information access. A thinly covered stock with no options market has a weak counter-force.
4. **Societal counter-force.** Check the regulatory pipeline for the industry, whether the customer base is socially conscious, and whether the stock is excluded by ESG mandates. See [[firms-and-society-social-costs]].
5. Score each counter-force present, weak, or absent.
6. Where a failure is live *and* the counter-force is absent, expect the excess to persist and price it. Where the counter-force is present, expect discipline within a few years.

**Reference data:**

*The hostile-takeover target profile.* The typical target firm in a hostile takeover has three traits:

| Trait | Threshold |
|---|---|
| Return on equity | Almost **5 percentage points lower** than its peer group |
| Stock performance | Significantly underperformed the peer group over the **previous 2 years** |
| Managerial ownership | Managers hold **little or no stock** in the firm |

Two conclusions follow. The best defense against a hostile takeover is to run the firm well and earn good returns for stockholders. And banning hostile takeovers mostly protects poorly run firms, not well-managed ones.

*The stockholder backlash — three forms:*
1. **Activist institutional investors** now monitor the companies they invest in and demand changes in how business is done. Private equity firms like KKR and Blackstone have joined them.
2. **Activist individuals** like Carl Icahn take large positions in companies they feel need to change — Blockbuster, Time Warner, Motorola, Apple — and push for change.
3. **Vocal stockholders**, armed with more information and new powers, express displeasure at annual meetings. They vote against management compensation contracts, or against the board of directors.

*The bondholders' defense:* more restrictive covenants on investment, financing and dividend policy, aimed at preventing future "Nabiscos"; puttable bonds; ratings sensitive notes; hybrid bonds with a conversion option or warrant. Detail in [[stockholder-bondholder-conflict]].

*The financial market response:* analysts still issue more buy than sell recommendations, but the payoff to uncovering negative news is large enough that such news is eagerly sought and quickly revealed. Better investor access to information makes it harder for firms to control the timing of disclosure. Option trading makes it easier to trade on bad news, which reveals the news to everyone else. When firms mislead markets, the punishment is quick and savage. Detail in [[firms-and-financial-markets]].

*The societal response:* governments — especially democracies — pass laws against firms that flout societal norms. Firms serving a socially conscious clientele lose business and value when they fall short, even when the behavior is legal. Investors may simply refuse to hold the stock. Detail in [[firms-and-society-social-costs]].

**Worked example: applying the takeover-target profile.** A mid-cap industrial has ROE of 7.5% against a peer median of 12.5% — a gap of 5.0 percentage points, matching the threshold. Its stock returned -18% over two years against +14% for peers. Insider ownership is 0.3% of shares. All three traits hit. The firm is a textbook hostile-takeover target, so the stockholder counter-force is live. Now check the blocker: if the firm has a classified board plus a poison pill adopted without a shareholder vote, the counter-force is neutralized and the underperformance can persist. If it has neither, expect a bid or an activist within 12-24 months.

Disney is the historical case of the counter-force working. Eisner's entrenchment held from 1996 to 2004. Then three counter-forces landed at once: activist directors resigning in protest (Roy Disney and Stanley Gold, 2003), a hostile bid (Comcast, 2004), and a vocal shareholder vote (43% withheld from Eisner's re-election, 2004). Eisner lost the chairmanship. See [[disney-governance-case-study]].

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: the three-part takeover-target screen (firm ROE, peer ROE, 2-year relative return, insider ownership % -> target yes/no); counting activist 13D filings; dissent percentages on director and say-on-pay votes; analyst coverage counts; covenant counts.
- JUDGMENT: whether a counter-force is strong enough to actually discipline this management; how long discipline will take; whether an activist's agenda creates value or merely extracts it; whether regulation will arrive and in what form.

**Pitfalls:**
- Assuming self-correction is automatic or fast. Disney took eight years. The counter-force has to be present and unblocked.
- Forgetting that entrenchment defeats the takeover counter-force. A poison pill plus a staggered board can make a firm untakeable regardless of how badly it performs.
- Reading "banning hostile takeovers" as investor protection. It mostly protects poorly run firms.
- Assuming an activist's presence means governance is fixed. It means pressure exists, not that it will succeed.
- Treating regulation as the primary counter-force. The source is skeptical of legislation — see [[governance-legislation-and-payoff]].
