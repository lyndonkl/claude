# The Disney Governance Case Study (1984-2019)

**Core idea:** Disney is the running case for the whole governance module, and it is worth carrying as a template because it shows the full cycle. A successful CEO consolidates power. The board becomes captive. Bad decisions get rubber-stamped. Performance deteriorates. Shareholders eventually revolt and the board is reformed. Then, as the *next* successful CEO's tenure lengthens, the board drifts back toward captivity and the cycle restarts. The lesson an analyst should take is that governance is not a state you verify once. It is a cycle you locate a company within, and CEO tenure is the clock.

**Formulas:** No arithmetic. The cycle, as a repeating sequence:

```
1. CEO succeeds early -> accumulates authority and credibility
2. CEO shapes the board -> board becomes captive, rubber-stamps
3. Major decisions go unchallenged -> value-destroying investments
4. Performance deteriorates -> stock underperforms
5. Shareholders revolt -> activists resign/attack, hostile bid arrives,
                          withhold votes spike
6. Board reforms -> independence, separated chair, executive sessions
7. New CEO succeeds -> tenure lengthens -> RETURN TO STEP 1
```

**Procedure:** Using Disney as a template on another company:
1. Measure CEO tenure. Long tenure plus early success is the risk condition.
2. Check whether the CEO holds the chairmanship, and whether that combination was ever separated and then re-combined. Re-combination is the clearest drift signal.
3. Check the board's insider count and independence against the CalPERS tests (see [[board-independence-assessment]]).
4. Find the largest capital-allocation decision of the last five years and ask whether the board challenged it. Absence of any public friction over many years is a finding.
5. Track relative stock performance over the CEO's tenure.
6. Check for revolt signals: activist 13Ds, director resignations in protest, hostile bids, withhold-vote percentages, ISS and CalSTRS/CalPERS red flags.
7. Check succession. A named successor who leaves, or a CEO who repeatedly extends, indicates entrenchment.
8. Locate the company on the seven-step cycle and forecast the next step.

**Reference data: the Disney timeline.**

| Period | Event | Governance reading |
|---|---|---|
| Late 1980s-early 1990s | Michael Eisner's early success lets him consolidate power | Step 1 |
| Through the 1990s | The boards Eisner creates are increasingly captive | Step 2 |
| **1996** | Eisner spearheads the push to buy ABC. The board rubber-stamps it, as it does other major decisions | Step 3 |
| **1997** | The board (16 members) is packed with insiders and CEO connections. Disney is the **only S&P 500 company to fail all three CalPERS tests**. Business Week ranks it the **#1 worst board in America**, overall score 10.3 | Step 2/3 confirmed |
| Late 1990s | Problems emerge with ABC and other operations | Step 4 |
| **1998-2002** | The stock price **halves**. Stockholders grow restive | Step 4 |
| **2003** | **Roy Disney and Stanley Gold resign from the board**, protesting Eisner's autocratic style. The board still holds three insiders and Eisner still chairs | Step 5 begins |
| **Early 2004** | **Comcast makes a hostile bid for Disney** | Step 5 |
| **Later 2004** | **43% of Disney shareholders withhold votes** for Eisner's board re-election. The board votes **unanimously to elect George Mitchell as Chair**, replacing Eisner, who vows to stay on as CEO | Step 5 |
| **2004 (after)** | Seven governance reforms adopted (listed below) | Step 6 |
| **2008** | Board down to 12 members, one insider (Iger). **John Pepper is non-executive Chairman**, separate from the CEO | Step 6 complete |
| **2011** | Iger announces he will step down as CEO in 2015, so a successor can be groomed. **In the same year the board votes to reinstate Iger as chair**, reversing the post-Eisner separation of CEO and Chair | Step 7 — drift begins |
| **2011** | Activist investors (**CalSTRS**) start making noise. **ISS** raises red flags about compensation and board monitoring at Disney | Drift detected |
| **2015** | The board convinces Iger to stay on as CEO for an extra year, "for the good of the company" | Drift |
| **2016** | **Thomas Staggs**, considered Iger's heir apparent, leaves Disney. Other potential CEOs also leave | Succession vacuum |
| **2017** | Disney acquires Fox. Iger's term is extended to 2019, and perhaps beyond, because his stewardship is deemed essential for the merger to work | Entrenchment restored |

*The seven post-2004 reforms Disney adopted:*
1. At least two executive sessions of the board each year, with no CEO or other management present.
2. A non-management presiding director. Senator George Mitchell was appointed to lead the executive sessions and help set the board's work agenda.
3. A new, more rigorous definition of director independence.
4. A requirement that a substantial majority of the board meet the new independence standards.
5. Reduced committee size, with committee and chairmanship assignments rotated among independent directors.
6. New provisions for management succession planning, plus evaluations of both management and board performance.
7. Enhanced continuing education and training for board members.

*Disney's ownership across the same period (detail in [[ownership-and-control-structure-analysis]]):*
- **2003:** fully splintered. Largest holder Barclays Global at 4.10%. Top 17 sum to 29.3%. No one could discipline management.
- **2009:** Steve Jobs is the largest holder at 7.46% ($3.34bn), far above any institution, following the Pixar acquisition. A real blockholder exists — and sits on the board.

*The Disney social-responsibility exercise:* open an inner-city store expected to lose about $1 million a year, creating needed jobs and possibly revitalizing the area. Would you? Would you tell stockholders and let them vote? See [[firms-and-society-social-costs]].

**Worked example:** The case *is* the worked example. The single most instructive number is the 2004 withhold vote. 43% of shareholders withholding votes from a sitting CEO-chairman is an extreme reading — under normal conditions withhold votes run in the low single digits. That number, arriving alongside a hostile bid and two protest resignations, is what finally moved a board that had rubber-stamped for eight years. An analyst screening for governance revolt should treat withhold votes above roughly 20-30% as a serious signal, and the arrival of two or three revolt signals at once as the trigger for actual change.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: board size, insider count, CalPERS test results per year (director roster -> pass/fail); withhold-vote percentage; CEO tenure in years; whether chair and CEO are the same person; relative stock performance over a period; the timeline dates themselves.
- JUDGMENT: whether a nominal outsider is truly independent; whether the ABC and Fox acquisitions destroyed value; where on the seven-step cycle a company currently sits; whether an extension of a CEO's term reflects entrenchment or genuine necessity; how long drift takes to produce damage.

**Pitfalls:**
- Treating governance reform as permanent. Disney separated the chair and CEO roles in 2004-2005, then recombined them in 2011. The reform lasted about six years.
- Assuming a reformed board stays reformed under a *successful* CEO. Iger's success is precisely what enabled the drift.
- Reading a succession announcement as succession planning. Iger announced a 2015 exit in 2011 and was still CEO in 2019.
- Missing the departure of heirs apparent. Staggs' 2016 exit was a stronger governance signal than any board-composition statistic.
- Waiting for a single revolt signal. Change at Disney needed three at once: protest resignations, a hostile bid, and a mass withhold vote.
- Assuming a large blockholder solves the problem. Steve Jobs held 7.46% and sat on the board through the start of the drift.
