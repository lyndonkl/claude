# Assessing Board Independence

**Core idea:** The board is the stockholders' only standing check on management, so an analyst must grade it. Grading is concrete work. Count the insiders. Check whether the CEO is also chairman. Check who staffs the audit and compensation committees. Then look for tangible evidence that the board has ever stopped or slowed something the CEO wanted. US boards improved a great deal between the 1970s and the late 1990s — smaller, fewer insiders, paid in stock, staffed by nominating committees. But the link between board composition and firm value is weak in the data. The recurring theme in problem companies is not a formal composition failure. It is an ineffective board that fails to ask tough questions of an imperial CEO.

**Formulas:** The three **CalPERS tests** of an independent board (California Public Employees' Retirement System, 1997):

```
Test 1: Are a majority of the directors OUTSIDE directors?
Test 2: Is the chairman of the board INDEPENDENT of the company
        (i.e. not the CEO)?
Test 3: Are the COMPENSATION and AUDIT committees composed
        ENTIRELY of outsiders?

Pass all three -> independent board.
Fail all three -> the Disney 1997 case; Disney was the ONLY S&P 500
                  company to fail all three tests.
```

Supporting counts:
- `Inside directors = employees of the firm + ex-managers of the firm`
- `Outside director % = (Total directors - Inside directors) / Total directors`

**Procedure:** Grading a real board:
1. Pull the proxy statement's director table: name, occupation, tenure, committee memberships, shareholding.
2. Classify each director as inside (current employee or ex-manager) or outside. Compute the outside percentage. **Test 1 passes if > 50%.**
3. Check the chairman. **Test 2 passes if the chair is not the CEO and is not a company employee.**
4. Read the audit and compensation committee rosters. **Test 3 passes only if every member is an outsider — one insider fails it.**
5. Go past the formal labels. For each "outside" director, hunt for ties to the CEO or the firm: business relationships, consulting or legal fees, charitable donations to the director's institution, personal relationships, and board interlocks (CEOs sitting on each other's boards).
6. Check director equity. Compare stake value to annual director fee, and check whether shares were bought or granted.
7. Check board size against the modern benchmark of 9-11. Smaller boards are less unwieldy and, in the evidence, more effective.
8. Check whether a nominating committee (not the CEO) selects directors.
9. Consult an external governance score as a cross-check. Yahoo! Finance publishes a corporate governance score ranking firms against the market and their sector. Institutional Shareholder Services (ISS) flags compensation and monitoring problems. CalSTRS and other activists publish target lists.
10. Search the news for tangible evidence of independence: an action the CEO wanted that the board stopped or slowed. Absence of such evidence over many years is itself a finding.
11. Grade the board and carry the grade into your view of agency cost.

*Board reform package — the seven changes Disney adopted after the 2004 revolt.* Use these as a checklist of what a reformed board looks like:
1. At least two executive sessions of the board each year, with no CEO or other management present.
2. A non-management presiding director to lead the executive sessions and help set the board's agenda.
3. A new, more rigorous definition of director independence.
4. A requirement that a substantial majority of the board meet the new independence standard.
5. Smaller committees, with committee and chairmanship assignments rotated among independent directors.
6. Management succession planning, plus evaluations of both management and board performance.
7. Enhanced continuing education and training for board members.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: inside-director count and outside percentage; CalPERS Tests 1-3 given a labeled director list and committee rosters (director list, roles, committee membership -> three booleans); board size vs. benchmark; director stake value vs. fee; Business Week and governance-score lookups.
- JUDGMENT: reclassifying a nominal outsider as non-independent because of undisclosed or soft ties (Bowers, O'Donovan, Russell, Stern at Disney); deciding whether the board actually acts independently; weighing external governance scores against your own read.

**Pitfalls:**
- Stopping at the CalPERS tests. They are necessary, not sufficient. A board can pass all three and still be a rubber stamp.
- Expecting board composition to predict firm value. The source says the link between board composition and firm value is weak. Smaller boards do tend to be more effective, and that is about all the data supports.
- Ignoring the qualitative theme. In problem companies the common thread is an ineffective board that will not ask tough questions of an imperial CEO.
- Grading the board once. Boards drift back toward captivity as a successful CEO's tenure lengthens — see [[disney-governance-case-study]].
- Treating a governance score from a vendor as the answer rather than as a cross-check.
