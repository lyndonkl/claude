# Firms and Society: Social Costs and Benefits

**Core idea:** The fourth link in the classical chain assumes every cost and benefit a firm creates can be traced back to the firm. In practice some cannot. A social cost or benefit accrues to society as a whole, not to the decision maker. Pollution, congestion and health damage are social costs. Jobs in a high-unemployment area, inner-city development, and access to goods where none existed are social benefits. The hard part is that these resist quantification for three structural reasons, which means an analyst cannot simply add them to a cash flow forecast. Society answers back through regulation, customer backlash, and investors who refuse to hold the stock — and those responses *are* quantifiable, because they hit cash flows and cost of capital.

**Formulas:** No formula. The definition and the taxonomy:

```
Social cost/benefit = a cost or benefit that accrues to SOCIETY AS A WHOLE,
                      not to the firm making the decision.
                      (In economic terms: an externality.)

Social costs    : environmental costs (pollution, health costs)
                  quality-of-life costs (traffic, housing, safety)
Social benefits : creating jobs in high-unemployment areas
                  supporting inner-city development
                  creating access to goods where none exists
```

Why they resist quantification — three reasons:
1. **Cannot know the unknown.** They may be unknown at the time of the decision. A firm may believe its product helps society and discover large costs only later. Asbestos was seen as a wonderful product — light and easy to work with. Its health consequences surfaced only after decades.
2. **Eyes of the beholder.** They are person-specific. Different decision makers weigh the same social cost very differently.
3. **Decision paralysis.** Carried to extremes, weighing every social cost paralyzes decision making.

**Procedure:** Handling social costs in a real analysis:
1. List the firm's material externalities, cost and benefit alike. Use the industry, the production process, and the product's end use as prompts.
2. Split each one into two buckets. Bucket A: already priced or regulated, so it hits the firm's own cash flows (carbon prices, emissions permits, tort liability, fines, remediation obligations). Bucket B: not yet priced.
3. Model Bucket A directly as operating costs, capital spending, or contingent liabilities.
4. For Bucket B, do not invent a social cost number. Instead model the *societal response* as risk. Three channels are available:
   - Governments — especially democracies — pass laws and regulations against firms that consistently flout societal norms and create large social costs. Model this as a probability-weighted future cost or a higher discount rate.
   - Firms catering to a socially conscious clientele lose business and value when they fail to meet societal norms, even when the behavior is legal. Model this as revenue risk.
   - Investors may simply refuse to invest in the stock of a firm they view as socially irresponsible. Model this as a smaller investor base and a higher cost of equity.
5. Disclose your assumptions. Because social costs are person-specific, another analyst weighing them differently should be able to see and change your inputs.
6. Do not let the exercise paralyze the decision. Size the material items and move on.

**Worked example: the Disney inner-city store.** You work for Disney. You can open an inner-city store that is expected to lose about **$1 million a year**. The store would create much-needed jobs and might revitalize the area. Three questions follow.
- Would you open the store?
- If yes, would you tell stockholders and let them vote on it?
- If no, how would you answer a stockholder who asks why you ignored your social responsibilities?

There is no numeric solution. The point is the disclosure and consent problem, which bites either way. Doing social good with $1 million a year of *other people's* money requires those people's consent. Refusing to do it invites the charge of ignoring social responsibility. The clean resolution is transparency: state the cost, state the benefit, and let the owners of the money decide.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: the annual accounting loss of a specific project ($1 million/year for the Disney store); the cost of already-priced externalities (permit prices, fines, remediation accruals -> cash flow impact).
- JUDGMENT: nearly everything else. Identifying externalities that have not yet been recognized (the asbestos problem is that they are literally unknown at decision time); weighing them (person-specific by construction); estimating the probability and size of a future regulatory response; deciding whether a social benefit justifies a project that loses money.

**Pitfalls:**
- Assuming today's knowledge is complete. Asbestos was regarded as a wonderful product for decades before the health costs surfaced.
- Presenting a social-cost number as objective. The source is explicit that these are in the eyes of the beholder.
- Letting social-cost analysis paralyze decision making. Carried to extremes, everything a firm does has a social cost.
- Spending shareholders' money on social good without telling them. That is the core of the Disney store exercise.
- Ignoring the societal counter-response as unquantifiable. Regulation, customer backlash and investor exclusion all hit cash flows and cost of capital, and those *are* modelable.
