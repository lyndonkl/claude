# Governance Legislation and the Payoff to Good Governance

**Core idea:** Two questions close the governance discussion. Can you legislate good governance? And does better governance pay? The source answers no to the first and yes to the second. Every scandal produces a legislative response — Enron and WorldCom produced Sarbanes-Oxley — but laws are blunt instruments. Compliance costs often exceed the benefits, unintended consequences always follow, and the burden falls harder on good companies than on bad ones. Meanwhile the empirical evidence on governance itself is strong. Firms with the strongest investor protections earn higher returns, carry higher market values, and behave better operationally. Note the distinction that matters most for an analyst: *investor protection provisions* predict value. *Board composition* barely does.

**Formulas:**

```
Governance index  = count of investor-protection provisions in force
                    (24 distinct provisions, scored per firm)

Higher index score = FEWER investor protections in the study's convention.

Empirical results (1,500 firms):
  Long strongest-protection / short weakest-protection portfolio
    -> annual excess return of +8.5%
  Each 1-point increase in the index toward FEWER protections
    -> market value LOWER by 8.9% (measured in 1999)
```

**Procedure:** Assessing the governance payoff for a real company:
1. Build or obtain a governance index. Count the investor-protection provisions in force at the firm: anti-takeover charter provisions, dual-class stock, staggered board, supermajority requirements, restrictions on shareholder action, and so on. Vendor scores (ISS, Yahoo! Finance's corporate governance score) are a shortcut.
2. Compare the firm's score to its sector and to the market.
3. Apply the value relationship as a rough magnitude, not a precise adjustment: each additional point of weakened protection is associated with roughly 8.9% lower market value in the 1999 cross-section.
4. Cross-check with the operating signature. Firms scoring high on investor protection also had higher profits, higher sales growth, and made fewer acquisitions. Check whether this firm shows that signature.
5. Do not lean on board composition. The link between board composition and firm value is weak. Smaller boards do tend to be more effective, and that is the one composition finding the data supports.
6. Apply the qualitative test instead. Anecdotally, problem companies share a common theme: an ineffective board that fails to ask tough questions of an imperial CEO. Look for that pattern directly.
7. Discount the value of any recent compliance-driven governance change. A firm that improved its governance only because a law forced it to has not necessarily changed its behavior.

**Reference data:**

*The governance-index study — the most comprehensive evidence cited:*

| Item | Value |
|---|---|
| Firms in the sample | 1,500 |
| Distinct governance provisions scored | 24 |
| Annual excess return, long strongest protections / short weakest | **+8.5%** |
| Market value effect per 1-point move toward fewer protections | **-8.9%** (1999) |
| Operating signature of high-protection firms | Higher profits, higher sales growth, fewer acquisitions |

*Board composition and value:* the link is weak. The one robust finding is that smaller boards tend to be more effective.

*Why you cannot legislate good corporate governance — three reasons:*
1. **Costs exceed benefits.** The costs of meeting legal requirements often exceed the benefits.
2. **Unintended consequences.** Laws always have them.
3. **Blunderbuss effect.** Laws tend to be blunt instruments that penalize good companies more than they punish bad ones.

The pattern: every corporate scandal creates impetus for a legislative response. The scandals at Enron and WorldCom laid the groundwork for Sarbanes-Oxley.

**Worked example:** Two firms in the same sector. Firm A scores in the strongest-protection decile of the governance index. Firm B scores 3 points worse. The cross-sectional relationship implies Firm B trades roughly 3 x 8.9% ≈ 27% below Firm A on comparable fundamentals, purely on governance. Check the operating signature to confirm: if Firm B also shows lower profitability, slower sales growth, and a heavier acquisition record, the governance read is corroborated and the discount is likely real. If Firm B shows the *opposite* operating signature, treat the index score as noise for this firm and look for a specific reason the provisions are in place.

**Determinism:**
- DETERMINISTIC: counting governance provisions to build the index (charter and bylaw provisions -> index score); applying the -8.9% per point relationship; the excess-return and index figures as table lookups; counting acquisitions; computing profitability and sales growth versus peers.
- JUDGMENT: whether the 1999 cross-sectional relationship still holds and applies to this firm; whether a provision is genuinely value-destroying or serves a legitimate purpose; whether an ineffective board is present, which is the qualitative test the source ultimately relies on; whether a compliance-driven change reflects real behavioral change.

**Pitfalls:**
- Expecting legislation to fix governance. The source is explicit that you cannot legislate good corporate governance.
- Using board composition to predict value. The link is weak. Use investor-protection provisions instead.
- Extrapolating the -8.9% coefficient as a precise valuation adjustment. It is a 1999 cross-sectional average, not a firm-specific discount.
- Confusing correlation with causation. High-protection firms had higher profits and growth, but the study does not establish the direction.
- Assuming a good vendor governance score means a good board. Scores measure provisions, not whether directors ask hard questions.
