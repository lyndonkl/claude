## p.2 — Interest Rates: The Basics
**Teaches:** An interest rate is what a borrower pays to borrow money and what a lender earns for lending it. The many interest rates encountered in life (fixed deposit rates, mortgage rates, credit card rates) fall into four broad groups. (1) Market-determined rates are set by demand and supply. (2) Market-influenced rates are based upon a market-determined value. (3) Entity-set rates are set by an entity such as a bank or credit card company. (4) Negotiated rates rest on negotiating power and need. What makes the market-determined rates change over time, and run higher in some periods than others, has been a source of endless debate.
**Rules:** Classification of interest rates: market-determined (demand/supply), market-influenced (based on a market rate), entity-set (bank/credit card company sets it), negotiated (bargaining power and need).

## p.3 — US Treasury Rates 1928–2019 (chart)
**Teaches:** A line chart of the 3-month T.Bill rate and the 10-year T.Bond rate from 1928 to 2019. Both rates were low (roughly 0–4%) from the 1930s through the early 1950s. They climbed steadily through the 1960s–70s and peaked around 14% in 1981–82. They then declined over the following decades to roughly 1.5–2% by 2019, with near-zero short rates after 2008. The 10-year rate generally sits above the 3-month rate, and the short rate is far more volatile. The point: market interest rates move in long cycles and today's low rates are not historically unprecedented in direction, only in level.

## p.5 — The Fisher Equation
**Teaches:** While many forces move interest rates, the Fisher equation lies at the core: the nominal interest rate equals the real interest rate plus expected inflation. The real interest rate reflects investor preferences for current consumption, but it also tends to be a proxy for real growth — higher real growth goes with higher real interest rates. On an expected basis: the nominal rate will generally be higher as expected inflation rises, and in a world with deflation and very low or negative real growth, the nominal interest rate can be negative.
**Formulas:** Nominal Interest Rate = Real Interest Rate + Expected Inflation. (Nominal Interest Rate = the stated market rate; Real Interest Rate = compensation for deferring consumption, proxied by real growth; Expected Inflation = anticipated inflation rate over the period.)
**Rules:** Higher expected inflation → higher nominal rates. Deflation plus low/negative real growth → nominal rates can go negative.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given any two of nominal rate, real rate, expected inflation, compute the third); estimating expected inflation itself is JUDGMENT.

## p.6 — An Intrinsic Riskfree Rate
**Teaches:** Using the Fisher equation, one can construct an "intrinsic" riskfree rate from fundamentals: intrinsic riskfree rate = inflation rate + real GDP growth (with real GDP growth proxying the real interest rate). A smoothed version uses the 10-year average of inflation and GDP growth. A chart from 1954–2019 shows that the actual 10-year T.Bond rate has tracked this intrinsic riskfree rate closely over decades. This implies that low rates after 2008 came mostly from low inflation and low real growth, not solely from Fed action.
**Formulas:** Intrinsic riskfree rate = Inflation Rate + Real GDP growth. Smoothed intrinsic rate = 10-year average inflation + 10-year average real GDP growth.

## p.7 — The Role of Central Banks
**Teaches:** Over the last century central banks have taken a bigger role in the interest rate market, and some investors believe they set rates. In reality, central banks set only a few rates directly; their influence on the rest of the rate market comes from the perception of central banking power. A central bank perceived as powerful can affect market rates through actions on rates that, by themselves, have little consequence.

## p.8 — The Fed Effect? (chart)
**Teaches:** Defines the "Fed Effect" as the gap between the actual 10-year T.Bond rate and the intrinsic riskfree rate. This gap measures how far the market rate strays from what fundamentals (inflation + real growth) imply. Part of that gap can be attributed to central bank action. A chart from 1954–2019 plots the Fed Funds rate, the 10-year T.Bond rate, and the Fed Effect bars. The effect is generally small, mostly within +/-5%. It is negative in many years before 1980, briefly strongly positive in the early 1980s (~+8.5%), and modestly negative after 2008. This suggests the Fed's actual impact on long rates is smaller than commonly believed.
**Formulas:** Fed Effect = 10-year T.Bond Rate - Intrinsic Riskfree Rate (where Intrinsic Riskfree Rate = inflation rate + real GDP growth).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (T.Bond rate, inflation, GDP growth → Fed Effect); interpreting what the Fed caused is JUDGMENT.

## p.9 — Quantitative Easing
**Teaches:** After the 2008 crisis, central banks became much more activist in trying to influence interest rates, markets, and stock prices. Quantitative Easing primarily captures central banks buying government bonds to keep rates low. Central banks have even provided backstops in corporate bond and lending markets, giving distressed firms lifelines to borrow more during market crises. Critics argue this activism rewards risk takers by protecting them from their mistakes and increases the chances of future inflation.

## p.10 — The Yield Curve
**Teaches:** The yield curve is a graph of market interest rates on bonds issued by a given entity (government or corporate) plotted against bond maturities — short-term borrowing rates versus long-term borrowing rates. The slope of the curve can be: upward sloping (long-term rates higher than short-term rates), flat (rates similar across maturities), or downward sloping / inverted (long-term rates lower than short-term rates).
**Rules:** Slope classification: long rates > short rates → upward sloping; roughly equal → flat; long rates < short rates → downward sloping (inverted).

## p.12 — Yield Curve: A Rationale for Upward Sloping Curves
**Teaches:** Embedded in every treasury rate are expectations of expected inflation and expected real interest rates: Interest Rate = Expected Inflation Rate + Expected Real Interest Rate. Over much of the last century the US treasury yield curve has been upward sloping. The standard economic rationalization is simple. Suppose inflation expectations are similar for the short and long term. Investors then demand a "maturity premium" (a higher real interest rate) for buying longer-term bonds. That premium causes the upward tilt in the yield curve.
**Formulas:** Interest Rate = Expected Inflation Rate + Expected Real Interest Rate (each treasury rate decomposes into these two expectation components).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (decomposition requires estimating expectations).
**Tags:** yield-curve, maturity-premium, expected-inflation, real-rate

## p.14 — Inverted Yield Curves: A Fed Effect?
**Teaches:** The rises in short-term rates that produce each inverted yield curve episode are accompanied by increases in the Fed Funds rate. Suppose the Fed's policy action (raising the Fed Funds rate) accomplishes its objective of slowing growth. The yield curve slope metric then becomes a stand-in for the Fed effect on the economy. A more positive slope goes with easier monetary policy.
**Rules:** More positive yield curve slope ↔ easier monetary policy; inversions coincide with Fed Funds rate hikes.

## p.16 — Yield Curves and Economic Growth: A Fuller Analysis
**Teaches:** Every inversion in the last few decades has been followed by a recession, which frightens investors — but is inversion a foolproof predictor? Yield curve slope metrics and economic growth are continuous variables, not discrete ones. A fuller test of predictive power therefore measures the link between the slope of the yield curve and the level of economic growth. It uses the full slope, not just whether the curve is inverted, and the full growth level, not just its sign.

## p.18 — Looking past the picture: spread–growth correlations
**Teaches:** Correlations between yield curve slope measures and subsequent real GDP growth. The sign of the correlation measures how GDP growth links to the slope. A positive correlation supports the base hypothesis: flatter or more negatively sloped curves go with lower or negative economic growth. The strength of the relationship shows in how close the correlation is to one. Over 1962–2018, correlations are weak (all below 0.29 in absolute value), and shorter-end slopes correlate positively with future growth while longer-end slopes correlate negatively. Over 2008–2018 the 1 yr vs 3 mth slope correlates strongly negatively (-0.49 to -0.53) with future growth — opposite the base hypothesis. Conclusion: the yield curve is a much weaker and less reliable growth predictor than the inversion-recession folklore suggests.
