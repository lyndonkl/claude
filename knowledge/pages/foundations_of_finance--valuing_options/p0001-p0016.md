## p.2 — The ingredients that make an "option"
**Teaches:** An option provides the holder the right (not obligation) to buy or sell a specified quantity of an underlying asset at a fixed price — called the strike price or exercise price — at or before the option's expiration date. For an option to exist there must be a clearly defined underlying asset whose value changes over time in unpredictable ways. For a real option specifically, the payoffs on the asset must be contingent on a specified event occurring within a finite period.
**Rules:** An "option" requires: (1) a clearly defined underlying asset with unpredictable value changes; (2) payoffs contingent on a specified event; (3) a finite period (expiration date).

## p.3 — A call option defined
**Teaches:** A call option gives you the right to buy an underlying asset at a fixed price (the strike or exercise price). If that right can be exercised at any time over the option's life it is an American option; if it can be exercised only at the end of the period (at expiration) it is a European option.
**Rules:** American option = exercisable any time during the option's life; European option = exercisable only at expiration.

## p.4 — Payoff diagram on a call
**Teaches:** Diagram showing the net payoff of a call option as a function of the price of the underlying asset at expiration. Below the strike price the net payoff is flat and negative: the buyer loses the premium paid. Above the strike, the payoff rises one-for-one with the underlying asset price. The net payoff turns positive once the asset price exceeds the strike plus the premium. So a call has limited downside (the premium) and unlimited upside.
**Formulas:** Gross payoff on call at expiration = max(S − K, 0), where S = price of underlying asset at expiration and K = strike price; net payoff = max(S − K, 0) − premium paid.

## p.5 — A put option defined
**Teaches:** A put option gives you the right to sell an underlying asset at a fixed price (the strike or exercise price). As with calls, the right may extend over the whole life of the option (American option) or apply only at the end of the period (European option).
**Rules:** American put = exercisable any time; European put = exercisable only at expiration.

## p.6 — Payoff diagram on a put option
**Teaches:** Diagram showing the net payoff of a put option against the price of the underlying asset at expiration. The payoff is highest when the asset price is lowest, declines one-for-one as the asset price rises toward the strike price, and is flat and negative (a loss equal to the premium paid) at all asset prices above the strike. A put therefore gains when the underlying falls and has downside limited to the premium.
**Formulas:** Gross payoff on put at expiration = max(K − S, 0), where K = strike price and S = price of underlying asset at expiration; net payoff = max(K − S, 0) − premium paid.

## p.7 — Determinants of option value
**Teaches:** Option value is driven by three groups of variables. First, variables relating to the underlying asset. (1) Value of the underlying asset: as it rises, calls (the right to buy at a fixed price) gain value and puts (the right to sell) lose value. (2) Variance in that value: higher variance makes BOTH calls and puts more valuable. All options have limited downside and depend on price volatility for upside. (3) Expected dividends: dividends reduce the price-appreciation component of the asset. That reduces call value and increases put value. Second, variables relating to the option. (4) Strike price: a lower strike makes the right to buy more valuable and the right to sell less valuable. (5) Life of the option: both calls and puts benefit from a longer life. Third, (6) the level of interest rates. As rates increase, the right to buy at a fixed future price gains value and the right to sell loses value.
**Rules:** Direction of effect on option value. Underlying asset value up → call up, put down. Variance up → call up, put up. Expected dividends up → call down, put up. Strike price up → call down, put up. Option life up → call up, put up. Interest rates up → call up, put down.

## p.8 — The essence of option pricing: replicating portfolio and arbitrage
**Teaches:** Option pricing models rest on the replicating-portfolio idea: you can create a combination of the underlying asset and a risk-free investment (lending or borrowing) that has exactly the same cash flows as the option being valued. Three things make this possible. The underlying asset must be traded, which gives observable prices and volatility as inputs and makes replication feasible. An active marketplace must exist for the option itself. And you must be able to borrow and lend money at the risk-free rate. Arbitrage then closes the argument: if the replicating portfolio has the same cash flows as the option, the two must be valued (priced) the same.
**Rules:** Preconditions for replication-based option pricing: (1) underlying asset is traded; (2) active market for the option; (3) borrowing and lending at the risk-free rate.

**Tags:** replicating-portfolio, arbitrage, option-pricing, no-arbitrage

## p.9 — Creating a replicating portfolio
**Teaches:** The objective of a replicating portfolio is to combine risk-free borrowing/lending with a position in the underlying asset to produce the same cash flows as the option being valued. A call is replicated by borrowing plus buying D (delta) units of the underlying stock; a put is replicated by selling short D units of the underlying asset plus lending. The number of shares bought or sold is called the option delta. By arbitrage, the option's value must equal the value of its replicating portfolio.
**Formulas:** Call = Borrowing + Buying D of the underlying stock; Put = Selling short D on underlying asset + Lending; D = option delta = number of shares of the underlying bought (call) or sold short (put) in the replicating portfolio.

## p.10 — The binomial option pricing model (worked two-period example)
**Teaches:** A two-period binomial tree illustrates option valuation by backward induction with replicating portfolios. At each node you solve two equations, one per branch, for D (shares of stock) and B (borrowing). The equations require D·(stock price) − (1+r)·B to equal the call payoff in each state. The call value at the node is then D·S − B. Working backward from the terminal payoffs gives the current call value.
**Formulas:** At each node solve two equations for D and B: S_up · D − (1+r) · B = C_up, and S_down · D − (1+r) · B = C_down. Then Call value at node = D · S − B. Symbols: S = current stock price at the node. S_up, S_down = next-period stock prices. C_up, C_down = call values in those states. r = riskless rate per period (1+r = 1.11 here). D = option delta. B = amount borrowed.
**Example:** Option details: strike K = $40, t = 2 periods, r = 11%. Stock starts at 50; after one period it moves to 70 or 35; terminal prices are 100, 50, or 25 with terminal call payoffs (max(S−40,0)) of 60, 10, and 0 respectively. Upper node (S=70): solve 100D − 1.11B = 60 and 50D − 1.11B = 10 → D = 1, B = 36.04; Call = 1·70 − 36.04 = 33.96. Lower node (S=35): solve 50D − 1.11B = 10 and 25D − 1.11B = 0 → D = 0.4, B = 9.01; Call = 0.4·35 − 9.01 = 4.99. Initial node (S=50): solve 70D − 1.11B = 33.96 and 35D − 1.11B = 4.99 → D = 0.8278, B = 21.61; Call = 0.8278·50 − 21.61 = 19.42. Current call value = $19.42.

## p.11 — The limiting distributions of the binomial
**Teaches:** As the binomial time interval shrinks (t → 0), the limiting distribution of prices takes one of two forms. If price changes become smaller as the interval shortens, the limit is the normal distribution and the price process is continuous. If price changes remain large as t → 0, the limit is the Poisson distribution — a distribution that allows for price jumps. The Black-Scholes model applies when the limiting distribution is normal: it explicitly assumes the price process is continuous with no jumps in asset prices.
**Rules:** If price changes shrink as t → 0 → normal distribution, continuous price process → Black-Scholes applies. If price changes stay large as t → 0 → Poisson distribution (price jumps) → Black-Scholes does not apply.

## p.13 — The Black-Scholes model (formula)
**Teaches:** The Black-Scholes value of a call and the replicating portfolio embedded in it. To replicate the call you would buy N(d1) shares of stock — N(d1) is the option delta — and borrow K·e^(−rt)·N(d2).
**Formulas:** Value of call = S·N(d1) − K·e^(−rt)·N(d2), where d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + sigma^2/2)·t] / (sigma·sqrt(t)) and d2 = d1 − sigma·sqrt(t). Symbols: S = current value of underlying asset; K = strike price; t = time to expiration; r = riskless rate for the option's life; sigma = standard deviation (sigma^2 = variance) of ln(value) of the underlying asset; N(·) = cumulative standard normal distribution function. Replicating portfolio: buy N(d1) shares (option delta) and borrow K·e^(−rt)·N(d2).

## p.15 — Adjusting Black-Scholes for dividends; put-call parity
**Teaches:** Define the dividend yield y = dividends / current value of the asset. If y is expected to remain unchanged during the option's life, the Black-Scholes model can be modified for dividends by discounting the asset price at the dividend yield. The value of a put can then be derived from put-call parity, an arbitrage condition.
**Formulas:** Dividend-adjusted call: Call value = S·e^(−yt)·N(d1) − K·e^(−rt)·N(d2), where d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − y + sigma^2/2)·t] / (sigma·sqrt(t)) and d2 = d1 − sigma·sqrt(t). Put via put-call parity: Put value = K·e^(−rt)·(1 − N(d2)) − S·e^(−yt)·(1 − N(d1)). Symbols: S = current value of underlying asset; K = strike price; t = time to expiration; r = riskless rate; y = continuous dividend yield = dividends / current value of asset; sigma = standard deviation of ln(value) of underlying; N(·) = cumulative standard normal.
**Rules:** The dividend adjustment applies only if the dividend yield is expected to stay constant over the option's life.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, r, y, sigma → call value and put value).
**Tags:** black-scholes, dividend-adjustment, put-call-parity, put-option

## p.16 — Choice of option pricing models: binomial vs Black-Scholes
**Teaches:** Some practitioners argue for the binomial model over Black-Scholes, on the grounds that early exercise is the rule rather than the exception with real options, and that underlying asset values are generally discontinuous (they jump). In practice, however, deriving the end nodes of a binomial tree is difficult. You can use the variance of the asset to build a synthetic binomial tree. But the value you then get will be very similar to the Black-Scholes value.
**Rules:** Prefer the binomial model when early exercise matters (common for real options) or the underlying value is discontinuous. But a synthetic binomial tree built from the asset's variance yields values very close to Black-Scholes. So Black-Scholes is a practical default.
