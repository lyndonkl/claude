## p.2 — Inflation across currencies

**Teaches:** In business and investing you often have to work in more than one currency. The same project or company can show very different numbers in US dollars than in, say, Indonesian Rupiah. Those gaps do NOT come from country risk or from business differences — those do not change when you switch currencies. They come purely from different expected inflation rates built into each currency. Higher inflation in a currency raises both the interest rates you observe in it and the expected nominal growth rates you should use in it.

## p.4 — Interest rates and inflation: differential-inflation technique

**Teaches:** A simple technique for estimating the risk-free/interest rate in any currency is to start from the US dollar risk-free rate and adjust for differential inflation between that currency and the US dollar, either additively (approximate) or compounded (exact). This is also a good way to sanity-check quoted interest rates in a currency that you do not trust (e.g., where government bond markets are illiquid or manipulated).
**Formulas:**
- Approximate (simple): Interest rate in currency = US dollar interest rate + Differential inflation, where Differential inflation = expected inflation in the currency − expected inflation in US dollars.
- Exact (compounded): Interest rate in currency = (1 + US $ rate) × (1 + inflation rate in currency) / (1 + inflation rate in US $) − 1. Here "US $ rate" is the US dollar risk-free interest rate. The inflation rates are expected inflation in the local currency and in US dollars.
**Example:** Egyptian pound, December 2015. Inputs: risk-free rate in US dollars on 12/31/15 = 2.27%; expected US inflation = 1.50%; expected Egyptian inflation = 9.70%. Computation: Risk-free rate in EGP = (1.0227) × (1.097/1.015) − 1 = 10.53%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (US $ rate + the two expected inflation rates → local-currency interest rate); choosing the expected inflation estimates is JUDGMENT.

## p.5 — Currency consistency in valuation (diagram)

**Teaches:** A diagram showing that in any DCF valuation the currency choice must affect the numerator and denominator consistently. The currency choice feeds expected inflation into BOTH sides. On the cash-flow side, the base-year cash flow grows with expected inflation plus real growth in the economy and company. On the discount-rate side, the rate equals expected inflation plus a real interest rate, plus a risk premium driven by company characteristics. The same expected inflation enters cash flows and discount rate, so value is (in principle) invariant to currency choice. Mismatching currencies between numerator and denominator is the error to avoid.
**Formulas:** Value of asset = E(CF_1)/(1+r) + E(CF_2)/(1+r)^2 + E(CF_3)/(1+r)^3 + … + E(CF_n)/(1+r)^n. Here E(CF_t) = expected cash flow in year t, in the chosen currency. r = discount rate in the same currency, built as expected inflation + real interest rate + risk premium.
**Rules:** The currency of the cash flows and the currency of the discount rate must match; inflation assumptions in growth and in the discount rate must be the same currency's inflation.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC once cash flows and r are given (standard PV sum); the framework itself is conceptual.
**Tags:** currency-consistency, dcf, discount-rate, valuation, inflation

## p.6 — Exchange rates: why and how to forecast

**Teaches:** If you commit to currency consistency, you often need forecasts of future exchange rates — for example, to convert local-currency cash flows into another currency. For some currencies, market-based forecasts exist in forward/futures markets. For others you must forecast on your own. Two simple tools help there. Interest rate parity forecasts exchange rates from the interest-rate gap between the two currencies. Purchasing power parity turns inflation gaps into expected exchange-rate changes.

## p.7 — Three forecasting approaches

**Teaches:** Three ways to forecast exchange rates. (1) Expert/Personal forecasts: use your own view of whether a currency is too strong or weak right now, plus fundamentals. (2) Market forecasts: use market-set forward or futures exchange rates for the currency. (3) Parity conditions: use two parity rules. Interest rate parity uses the interest-rate gap between currencies to forecast exchange rates. Purchasing power parity uses inflation gaps to forecast changes in exchange rates.
**Rules:** Classification of exchange-rate forecasting methods: expert/personal, market-based (forwards/futures), parity-based (IRP and PPP).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (taxonomy; parity methods themselves are deterministic given inputs).

## p.8 — 1. Expert/personal forecasts: why to avoid them

**Teaches:** Exchange-rate forecasts, like most macroeconomic forecasts, are done poorly — even the best forecasters barely beat random forecasts. Suppose you believe you can forecast exchange rates, or can hire someone who does. Building those views into a project analysis or valuation still muddies the waters. The final judgment on whether to invest ends up driven more by your currency views than by what you think of the project itself. Put simply, if you are that good at forecasting exchange rates, there are far easier ways to make money than running a business or investing in companies.
**Rules:** Do not embed personal exchange-rate views in project/valuation analysis; keep the investment judgment about the investment, not the currency.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** exchange-rate-forecasting, forecasting-skepticism, valuation-hygiene

## p.9 — 2. Market forecasts: forwards and futures

**Teaches:** Forward and futures markets on exchange rates have developed over the last few decades. For widely held currencies like the US dollar and the Euro, you can easily and cheaply buy forward contracts that lock in exchange rates for future periods (3, 5 or even 10 years out), giving market-based forecasts. In emerging-market currencies it is trickier. Forward markets may not extend past the near term (one or two years). There may be no market-set rates at all, forcing you to buy customized forward contracts with a bank setting the rates.
**Rules:** Use market forward rates when liquid quotes exist for your horizon; for emerging-market currencies expect short or missing forward curves and bank-set customized pricing.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (availability assessment); reading a forward rate itself is deterministic lookup.
**Tags:** forward-rates, futures, market-forecasts, emerging-markets

## p.11 — 3a. Interest rate parity

**Teaches:** Interest rate parity is an arbitrage condition. If it breaks, traders can lock in riskless profit. Take two currencies with a current exchange rate and an interest rate in each. The interest-rate gap then pins down the expected (forward) future exchange rate. The rates used must match the forecast horizon: one-year rates for one-year forward rates, compounded two-year rates for two-year forward rates, and so on.
**Formulas:** XRF_$,Euro = XR_$,Euro × (1 + Interest Rate_US$) / (1 + Interest Rate_Euro). Here XR_$,Euro = current exchange rate in $ per Euro. Interest Rate_US$ and Interest Rate_Euro = interest rates in US dollars and Euros for the matching horizon. XRF_$,Euro = expected future (forward) exchange rate in $ per Euro. For t-year horizons, use interest rates compounded over t years: XRF = XR × (1+r_US$)^t / (1+r_Euro)^t.
**Example:** Current rate $1.1662/Euro, US interest rate 2%, Euro interest rate 1%: XRF_$,Euro = 1.1662 × (1.02)/(1.01) = $1.1777/Euro one year forward.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (spot rate + two interest rates for the horizon → forward exchange rate).
**Tags:** interest-rate-parity, forward-rates, arbitrage, exchange-rate-forecasting

## p.12 — 3b. Purchasing power parity

**Teaches:** Purchasing power parity (PPP) rests on a simple principle. High-inflation currencies should depreciate over time against low-inflation currencies, because they lose purchasing power on a relative basis. Start with the current exchange rate and the expected inflation rate in each currency. PPP then gives the expected future exchange rate for any horizon by compounding the inflation gap.
**Formulas:** XRF_$,Rs = XR_$,Rs × (1 + Inflation Rate_$) / (1 + Inflation Rate_Rs). Here XR_$,Rs = current exchange rate in $ per Rupee. Inflation Rate_$ and Inflation Rate_Rs = expected inflation in US dollars and in Rupees. XRF_$,Rs = expected exchange rate one year forward. For t years: XRF_t = XR × (1 + Inflation_$)^t / (1 + Inflation_Rs)^t.
**Example:** Current rate $0.013/Rupee, expected inflation 10% in India and 1% in the US. Next year: XRF = 0.013 × (1.01)/(1.10) = $0.0119. Two years from now: XRF = 0.013 × (1.01^2)/(1.10^2) = $0.0110. Put simply, the rupee is expected to depreciate roughly 9% against the dollar next year.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (spot rate + two expected inflation rates + horizon → expected future exchange rate); estimating the expected inflation rates is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** purchasing-power-parity, inflation-differential, exchange-rate-forecasting, currency-depreciation
