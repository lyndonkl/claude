## p.2 — Balance Sheet: A Life Cycle Perspective

**Teaches:** A company's balance sheet changes in predictable ways as the firm ages. The slide draws revenues and earnings over time and splits the life into six stages: Start-up, Young Growth, High Growth, Mature Growth, Mature Stable, and Decline. The transition points carry nicknames: "The Lightbulb (Idea) Moment," "The Product Test," "The Bar Mitzvah," "The Scaling up Test," "The Midlife Crisis," and "The End Game." Assets grow from a small base, stabilize, and then shrink. Debt starts as a last resort, stays low through growth, then becomes large and complex, and finally distressed. Equity starts negative from losses, is fed first by capital infusions and later by retained earnings, and finally declines with losses and divestitures.

**Data:**

| Lifecycle stage | Start-up | Young Growth | High Growth | Mature Growth | Mature Stable | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Assets | Small asset base, if any | Asset base grows quickly | Asset base continues to grow, albeit at lower rate | Asset base stabilizes & grows with earnings | Asset base stabilizes | Asset base declines |
| Debt | Only as a last resort | Low or non-existent | Stays low | Starts to grow | Large and often complex | Decline or in distress |
| Shareholder Equity | Often negative, reflecting continued losses | Turns positive, more because of capital infusions than retained earnings | Consolidates and starts growth as retained earnings start accumulating | Grows quickly, as earnings build, but cash return stays low | Depends on how much cash the company chooses to return | Declines, as losses & divestitures take their toll |

## p.7 — Coca Cola's Accounting Intangibles: Small-bore?

**Teaches:** Accounting splits intangibles into two groups. Indefinite-lived intangibles (trademarks, bottlers' franchise rights, goodwill) are not amortized; they are tested for impairment. Definite-lived intangibles (customer relationships, some franchise rights and trademarks) are amortized over time. The title's "small-bore?" hints that these accounting intangibles capture only a sliver of Coca Cola's true brand value: the definite-lived total is just $517M net for a company whose worth rests almost entirely on its brand.

**Data:** Indefinite-lived intangible assets (USD millions), December 31:

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Customer relationships | 344 | (177) | 167 | 290 | (151) | 139 |

| Other | 55 | (30) | 25 | 88 | (61) | 27 |

**Rules:** Indefinite-lived intangibles are carried without amortization (impairment-tested only). Definite-lived intangibles are carried at gross value less accumulated amortization.

**Formulas:** Net carrying value = Gross carrying value − Accumulated amortization, where gross carrying value is the original recorded cost of the intangible and accumulated amortization is the total amortization charged to date.

## p.10 — Sector and Industry Differences

**Teaches:** Balance sheets differ across sectors and industries even when the same accounting standards govern every company — the same point made earlier for income statements. The divergences appear on both sides of the balance sheet. On the asset side, they show up in how much value comes from tangible versus intangible assets. For acquisitive companies, they also show up as uniquely accounting items like goodwill.

## p.15 — Bottom Line

**Teaches:** The balance sheet is the financial statement that most reflects a company's history. Built correctly, it is the cumulated result of everything the company has done since inception. But accountants disagree about what that history should show. Old-time (historical cost) accounting says it should reflect what the company invested in its existing assets. Fair-value accounting says it should reflect their current value. The result: modern balance sheets are a mess, measuring neither invested capital nor fair value. The most useful items left are (a) cash and marketable securities, which are not subject to accounting nuance, and (b) debt, which measures closely what is owed, at least on interest-bearing liabilities.

**Rules:** When using a balance sheet, trust cash & marketable securities and interest-bearing debt figures. Treat other book values as neither invested capital nor fair value.
