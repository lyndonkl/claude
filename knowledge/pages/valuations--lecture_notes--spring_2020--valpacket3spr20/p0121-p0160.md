## p.123 — The Three (Value) Reasons for Acquisitions

**Teaches:** There are only three value-based justifications for buying a company. (1) Undervaluation: the market misprices the target and you can buy it for less than fair value. (2) Control: the target is badly managed and you intend to change how it is run; if you are right and can make the changes, firm value rises under your management. (3) Synergy: combined with a business or resource you already own, the merged entity can do things the separate entities could not. Synergy comes in three flavors: offensive (higher growth and increased pricing power), defensive (cost cutting, consolidation, preempting competitors), and tax (directly from tax clauses or indirectly through debt).

**Rules:** Any acquisition rationale should map to undervaluation, control, or synergy; anything else is not a value reason.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification framework).
**Tags:** acquisitions, synergy, control, undervaluation

## p.124 — Four numbers to watch (the Acid Test)

**Teaches:** Every acquisition analysis needs four numbers. (1) Acquisition Price: what you pay — negotiated for private targets, a premium over market price for public ones. (2) Status Quo Value: value of the target run by existing management. (3) Restructured Value: value of the target with changed investing, financing and dividend policies. (4) Synergy Value: value of the combined company with synergy benefits, minus the sum of the acquirer's standalone value and the restructured value of the target.

**Rules:** The Acid Test — an acquisition passes on a given motive only if:
- Undervaluation: Price for target < Status Quo Value
- Control: Price for target < Restructured Value
- Synergy: Price for target < Restructured Value + Value of Synergy

**Formulas:** Synergy value = Value of combined company (with synergy) − [Value of acquirer standalone + Restructured value of target].

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC comparisons once the four values are estimated; estimating the values is JUDGMENT → MIXED.
**Tags:** acquisitions, acid-test, synergy, control, valuation

## p.132 — Price Enhancement versus Value Enhancement

**Teaches:** Distinguishes actions that raise a stock's PRICE from actions that raise intrinsic VALUE. Evidence: firms that added ".com" to their names during the dot-com bubble earned cumulative abnormal returns of roughly 100-200% around the announcement with no change in fundamentals ("the market gives"). Later, when dot-com fell out of favor, firms DROPPING the dot-com name got a ~20% bounce ("and takes away"). Cosmetic changes can move price without creating any value — and the market can reverse them.

## p.133 — The Paths to Value Creation

**Teaches:** Within the DCF framework there are exactly four ways to increase firm value. (1) Increase cash flows from existing assets: raise after-tax earnings from assets in place, or reduce reinvestment needs (net cap ex or working capital). (2) Increase the expected growth rate of those cash flows: raise the reinvestment rate, or improve the return on capital on new investments. (3) Lengthen the high-growth period. (4) Reduce the cost of capital: reduce operating risk, change the financial (debt/equity) mix, or change the financing composition (matching debt to assets). Any proposed "value creation" action must work through at least one of these levers.

**Rules:** If an action changes none of the four levers (cash flows, growth, growth-period length, cost of capital), it cannot create value.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework).
**Tags:** value-creation, dcf, value-drivers, framework

## p.134 — Value Creation 1: Increase Cash Flows from Assets in Place

**Teaches:** Maps management actions onto each line of the FCFF computation. Revenues × Operating Margin = EBIT: raise the margin via more efficient operations and cost cutting, and divest assets that have negative EBIT. Subtracting Tax Rate × EBIT gives EBIT(1−t): reduce the tax rate by moving income to lower-tax locales, transfer pricing, and risk management. Adding Depreciation and subtracting Capital Expenditures and Change in Working Capital gives FCFF: live off past over-investment to cut cap ex, and use better inventory management and tighter credit policies to reduce working capital.

**Formulas:** FCFF = EBIT(1−t) + Depreciation − Capital Expenditures − Change in Working Capital, where EBIT = Revenues × Operating Margin and t = tax rate.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (FCFF from inputs); choosing the actions is JUDGMENT → MIXED.
**Tags:** fcff, cash-flow, value-creation, operations

## p.135 — Value Creation 2: Increase Value from Expected Growth

**Teaches:** Expected growth = Reinvestment Rate × Return on Capital, so growth can be raised by reinvesting more (in projects or via acquisitions) or by earning a higher return on capital (increase operating margins, increase capital turnover ratio). Pricing strategy matters: Return on Capital = Operating Margin × Capital Turnover Ratio, so a firm can pursue a price-leader strategy (high margin, low turnover) or a volume-leader strategy (low margin, high turnover). Game theory intrudes: ask how competitors will react to your moves and how you will react to theirs.

**Formulas:** Expected Growth Rate = Reinvestment Rate × Return on Capital; Return on Capital = Operating Margin × Capital Turnover Ratio.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (growth from reinvestment rate and ROC); strategy choice is JUDGMENT → MIXED.

## p.139 — Value Creation 4: Reduce Cost of Capital

**Teaches:** The fourth lever is lowering the cost of capital. Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × (E/(D+E)) + Pre-tax Cost of Debt (after tax) × (D/(D+E)). **Formulas:** Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × (E/(D+E)) + Pre-tax Cost of Debt × (D/(D+E)), where E = market value of equity, D = market value of debt (slide displays the weighted-average identity).

## p.145 — The Expected Value of Control (framework)

**Teaches:** The value of control embedded in any stock is the product of two components: (Probability that you can change the management of the firm) × (Change in firm value from changing management). The probability depends on takeover restrictions, voting rules and rights, access to funds, and the size of the company. The change in value is (Value of the firm run optimally) − (Value of the firm run status quo).

**Formulas:** Expected value of control = Probability of management change × (Optimal firm value − Status quo firm value).

**Compute:** MIXED — the multiplication is DETERMINISTIC; both inputs (probability, optimal value) are JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** value-of-control, corporate-governance, framework

## p.146 — Why the probability of management changing shifts over time

**Teaches:** The probability of management change is not static. Three drivers: (1) corporate governance rules change as new laws are passed — rules giving stockholders more power raise the likelihood of change; (2) activist investing ebbs and flows with market movements (activists are more visible in down markets) and often responds to scandals; (3) events such as hostile acquisitions make investors reassess the likelihood of change by reminding them of the power they possess.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** management-change, corporate-governance, activist-investing

## p.147 — Estimating the Probability of Change

**Teaches:** The probability of management change can be estimated from historical data on companies where change occurred, using statistical techniques such as probits or logits. Empirically, forced management turnover is related to: (1) stock price and earnings performance — turnover is more likely in firms performing poorly versus peers and expectations; (2) board structure — forced CEO changes are more likely when the board is small, composed of outsiders, and the CEO is not also chairman; (3) ownership structure — more common with high institutional and low insider holdings, and in firms more dependent on equity markets for new capital; (4) industry structure — CEOs are more likely to be replaced in competitive industries.

**Rules:** Higher probability of management change when: poor relative performance; small outsider-dominated board; CEO ≠ chairman; high institutional / low insider ownership; dependence on equity markets; competitive industry.

**Compute:** MIXED — probit/logit estimation is DETERMINISTIC given data; specifying the model and interpreting it is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** management-change, probability-estimation, corporate-governance, empirical

## p.148 — Manifestations of the Value of Control

**Teaches:** Four places where the value of control shows up: (1) hostile acquisitions — the control premium should reflect the value change from replacing management; (2) market prices of publicly traded firms — every market price should embed an expected value of control: Market value = Status quo value + (Optimal value − Status quo value) × Probability of management changing; (3) voting versus non-voting shares — the voting premium should increase with the expected value of control; (4) minority discounts in private companies — the discount for buying less than a controlling stake should increase with the expected value of control.

**Formulas:** Market value = Status quo value + (Optimal value − Status quo value) × Probability of management changing.

**Compute:** MIXED — formula is DETERMINISTIC given inputs; inputs are JUDGMENT.

## p.151 — Value of stock in a publicly traded firm (voting vs non-voting)

**Teaches:** For a badly managed firm, the market prices in the chance of better future management. With one share class: Value per share = [Status Quo Value + Probability of control change × (Optimal − Status Quo Value)] / Number of shares outstanding. With voting and non-voting shares, the value of control accrues disproportionately to voting shares. In the extreme case where non-voting shares are completely unprotected: Value per non-voting share = Status Quo Value / (# voting shares + # non-voting shares); Value per voting share = Value per non-voting share + [Probability of control change × (Optimal − Status Quo Value)] / # voting shares.

**Formulas:** (1) Value per share = [Status Quo Value + P × (Optimal − Status Quo)] / N, where P = probability of control change, N = shares outstanding. (2) Value per non-voting share = Status Quo Value / (V + NV), V = # voting shares, NV = # non-voting shares. (3) Value per voting share = Value per non-voting share + P × (Optimal − Status Quo) / V.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given status quo value, optimal value, probability, and share counts.

## p.154 — Alternative Approaches to Value Enhancement

**Teaches:** Instead of full DCF, firms can maximize a proxy variable correlated with firm value. Candidates: an accounting variable (earnings, return on investment); a marketing variable (market share); a cash-flow variable (cash flow return on investment, CFROI); or a risk-adjusted cash-flow variable (Economic Value Added, EVA). Advantage: these are often simpler and easier to use than DCF value. Disadvantage: simplicity comes at a cost — these variables are not perfectly correlated with DCF value, so maximizing them can diverge from maximizing value.

## p.155 — Economic Value Added (EVA) and CFROI defined

**Teaches:** EVA measures the surplus value created on an investment. Define return on capital (ROC) as the "true" cash-flow return on capital earned on an investment. Define cost of capital as the weighted average of the costs of the financing instruments used. Then EVA = (ROC − Cost of Capital) × Capital Invested. CFROI is a measure of the cash-flow return made on capital, computed as an IRR based on a base value of capital invested and the cash flows on that capital.

**Formulas:** EVA = (Return on Capital − Cost of Capital) × (Capital Invested in Project). CFROI = IRR of the cash flows on a base value of capital invested.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, cost of capital, capital invested → EVA; cash flows → CFROI as IRR).
**Tags:** eva, cfroi, excess-returns, definitions

## p.156 — The bottom line: all consistent DCF approaches agree

**Teaches:** The value of a firm does not change just because you use a different metric for value. All discounted-cash-flow-based approaches (including EVA-based valuation) should yield the same value for a business if they make consistent assumptions. Any difference in value across approaches must be attributable to differences in assumptions — explicit or implicit — behind the valuation, not to the metric itself.

**Rules:** If EVA valuation ≠ DCF valuation, look for an inconsistent assumption; the framework itself cannot create or destroy value.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (principle).
**Tags:** eva, dcf, equivalence, valuation-principles
