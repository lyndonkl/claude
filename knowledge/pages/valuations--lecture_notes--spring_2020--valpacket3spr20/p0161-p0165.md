## p.161 — Implications: growth alone does not create value; MVA reflects excess returns

**Teaches:** Growth by itself does not create value. Value comes only from growth paired with investment in excess-return projects — projects that earn more than the cost of capital. In the running example, the 5% annual growth after year 5 adds no value. Those later investments earn exactly the cost of capital. "Market value added" (MVA) is the excess of market value over capital invested. It is a function of the excess value created. In the example, market value of $170.85 million exceeds book value of $100 million because return on capital is 5% above the cost of capital.

**Formulas:** MVA = Market value of firm − Capital invested, where MVA = market value added, Market value of firm = value of the firm from a DCF/EVA valuation, Capital invested = book capital invested in the firm.

**Rules:** Growth creates value only if the return on capital on new investments exceeds the cost of capital; growth at exactly the cost of capital is value-neutral (MVA contribution = 0).

**Example:** Continuing example firm: market value $170.85 million vs. capital invested $100 million, so MVA = $70.85 million, arising because ROC exceeds cost of capital by 5%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given market value and capital invested, MVA = market value − capital invested.

## p.162 — Year-by-year EVA changes: uses and costs

**Teaches:** Firms are often judged on year-to-year changes in EVA (economic value added) rather than on the present value of EVA over time. This comparison has two strengths. It is simple and needs no forecasts of future earnings. It can also be split by any unit — person, division, etc. — as long as one is willing to assign capital and allocate earnings across those same units. But while simpler than DCF valuation, it has a cost. A firm that pushes EVA up each year can end up worth less.

**Rules:** Year-to-year EVA change is a valid metric only when raising current EVA does not sacrifice future EVA. A rising annual EVA does not guarantee a rising firm value.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — deciding when to judge a firm by EVA changes requires weighing tradeoffs (though the EVA change itself is a simple difference of two EVA figures).

**Tags:** eva, performance-measurement, value-based-management, divisional-evaluation

## p.163 — Gaming the system: three ways to deliver high current EVA while destroying value

**Teaches:** Managers can deliver high current EVA while destroying value in three ways. (1) The Growth trade-off game: give up valuable future growth opportunities to deliver higher EVA this year. (2) The Risk game: deliver a higher dollar EVA but in riskier businesses. Business value is the present value of EVA over time, so the risk effect (a higher discount rate) may dominate the higher EVA. (3) The Capital Invested game: make investments that do not show up as capital invested. Operating income then rises while measured capital invested falls, which mechanically inflates EVA.

**Rules:** Watch for three EVA-gaming red flags: future growth traded away for current EVA; higher EVA earned in riskier businesses (a rising cost of capital); and investments kept off the capital-invested base (off-balance-sheet or expensed items).

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — spotting these games requires weighing growth prospects, risk shifts, and how capital is measured.

**Tags:** eva, gaming-metrics, value-destruction, risk, capital-invested

## p.164 — High EVA may not translate into higher stock prices

**Teaches:** The link between EVA and market value changes is more complicated than the link between EVA and firm value. A firm's market value reflects the expected EVA of assets in place plus the expected EVA from future projects. Market prices embed expectations. If actual EVA comes in below expected EVA, market value can fall even though the delivered EVA is higher than before.

**Rules:** The stock price reacts to actual EVA versus expected EVA, not to the EVA level or its year-over-year rise. Higher EVA that still falls short of what was expected can lower market value.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — one must estimate the market's expected EVA and compare actual results against it.

**Tags:** eva, market-expectations, stock-prices, expectations-game

## p.165 — When year-to-year EVA focus has the least side effects

**Teaches:** Focusing on year-to-year EVA changes causes the least harm under three conditions. (1) Most or all of the firm's assets are already in place, so little or none of firm value comes from future growth. This minimizes the risk that gains in current EVA come at the expense of future EVA. (2) Leverage is stable, and the firm's investment decisions cannot easily alter the cost of capital. This minimizes the risk that higher EVA comes with a higher cost of capital. (3) The firm is in a sector where investors expect little or no surplus returns; firms there are expected to just earn their cost of capital. This minimizes the risk that the EVA increase falls short of what the market expected, which would drop the price.

**Rules:** Use year-to-year EVA change as the primary performance metric only when: (a) assets in place dominate value (minimal growth value), (b) leverage/cost of capital is stable, and (c) the sector is expected to earn no surplus returns.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — sorting a firm into or out of these three cases takes qualitative review.

**Tags:** eva, performance-measurement, mature-firms, cost-of-capital, market-expectations
