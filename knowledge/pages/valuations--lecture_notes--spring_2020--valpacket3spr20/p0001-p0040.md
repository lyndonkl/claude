## p.3 — Underlying theme: searching for an elusive premium
**Teaches:** Traditional discounted cash flow (DCF) models underestimate the value of investments that contain embedded options. Four such options exist. (1) Delay: defer making the investment. (2) Flexibility: adjust or alter production schedules as prices change. (3) Expansion: expand into new markets or products at later stages, based on observing favorable early outcomes. (4) Abandonment: stop production or abandon the investment if early outcomes are unfavorable. Put another way, real-option advocates believe you should pay a premium on top of DCF value estimates.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual framing).
**Tags:** real-options, dcf, option-premium

## p.5 — The same investment becomes a good one when staged
**Teaches:** The same style of gamble becomes good when the investment can be staged and abandoned mid-way (i.e., when it contains options). Tree: first stage has probability 3/4 of an interim outcome of +20 and 1/4 of -20 (stop). From the +20 node, a second stage has probability 2/3 of +80 and 1/3 of -100. Because the firm can observe the first-stage outcome before committing to the second stage, the sequential (optioned) investment has positive expected value even though the all-at-once version (p.4) was negative. This is the core intuition for why optionality adds value.
**Example:** Stage 1: 3/4 → +20, 1/4 → -20. Stage 2 (only after +20): 2/3 → +80, 1/3 → -100. Staging converts the bad one-shot bet on p.4 into a good investment.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given tree probabilities and payoffs → staged expected value).

## p.7 — When is there an option embedded in an action?
**Teaches:** An option provides the holder the right (not the obligation) to buy or sell a specified quantity of an underlying asset at a fixed price (the strike or exercise price) at or before the expiration date. For a real option to exist: (1) there must be a clearly defined underlying asset whose value changes over time in unpredictable ways, and (2) the payoffs must be contingent on a specified event occurring within a finite period.
**Rules:** An embedded option exists only if you can identify the underlying asset AND the payoff is contingent on a specified event within a finite period.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of whether an option exists).
**Tags:** real-options, option-definition, contingent-claims

## p.10 — When does the option have significant economic value?
**Teaches:** For an option to have significant economic value there must be a restriction on competition in the event of the contingency: in a perfectly competitive product market no contingency, no matter how positive, generates positive net present value. At the limit, real options are most valuable when you have exclusivity — you and only you can exploit the contingency — and become less valuable as barriers to competition become less steep.
**Rules:** Exclusivity → full option value; perfectly competitive market → zero option value; partial barriers → scaled-down option value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** real-options, exclusivity, competitive-advantage

## p.11 — Determinants of option value
**Teaches:** Six variables drive option value. Underlying-asset variables: (1) Value of the underlying asset — as it rises, calls become more valuable and puts less valuable; (2) Variance in that value — higher variance makes both calls and puts more valuable because options have limited downside and depend on volatility for upside; (3) Expected dividends on the asset — they reduce price appreciation, reducing call value and increasing put value. Option variables: (4) Strike price — the right to buy (sell) at a fixed price is more (less) valuable at a lower strike; (5) Life of the option — both calls and puts benefit from longer life. Plus (6) Level of interest rates — as rates rise, the right to buy (sell) at a fixed future price becomes more (less) valuable.
**Rules:** Call value: increases with S, variance, life, interest rates; decreases with K and dividends. Put value: increases with K, variance, life, dividends; decreases with S and interest rates.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (directional comparative statics; the actual pricing is deterministic once a model is chosen).
**Tags:** option-value-determinants, options-basics, comparative-statics

## p.12 — When can you use option pricing models to value real options?
**Teaches:** Option pricing models are built on the notion of a replicating portfolio, so they are best suited to valuing real options where: (1) the underlying asset is traded (giving observable prices and volatility and permitting replication), (2) an active marketplace exists for the option itself, and (3) the cost of exercising the option is known with some certainty. When option pricing models are applied to real (non-traded) assets, the value estimates will be far more imprecise, and value can deviate much more dramatically from market price because arbitrage is difficult.
**Rules:** Use option pricing models most confidently when: underlying is traded; option itself trades; exercise cost is known. Otherwise expect imprecision and no arbitrage enforcement.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** real-options, replicating-portfolio, option-pricing, arbitrage

## p.13 — Creating a replicating portfolio
**Teaches:** A replicating portfolio combines riskfree borrowing/lending with the underlying asset to create the same cash flows as the option being valued. A call = Borrowing + Buying D (delta) units of the underlying stock; a put = Selling short D units of the underlying asset + Lending. The number of shares bought or sold is the option delta. By arbitrage, the option's value must equal the value of the replicating portfolio.
**Formulas:** Call = Borrowing + D × (underlying stock); Put = -D × (underlying stock, sold short) + Lending; D = option delta = number of shares in the replicating portfolio.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC once payoffs and prices are given (solve for D and borrowing B).
**Tags:** replicating-portfolio, option-delta, arbitrage

## p.14 — The binomial option pricing model (worked two-period example)
**Teaches:** Worked binomial tree valuing a call by backward induction with replicating portfolios at each node. Option details: strike K = $40, t = 2 periods, r = 11% per period. Stock tree: 50 → (70 or 35); 70 → (100 or 50); 35 → (50 or 25). Terminal call payoffs (K=40): at 100 → 60; at 50 → 10; at 25 → 0. At each node solve for delta D and borrowing B such that D×(up stock) - (1+r)×B = up option value and D×(down stock) - (1+r)×B = down option value; then Call = D×S - B.
**Formulas:** At each node: D×S_up - (1+r)B = C_up and D×S_down - (1+r)B = C_down; node call value = D×S - B, where D = option delta, B = borrowing, S = current stock price, r = riskfree rate per period (1+r = 1.11 here), C_up/C_down = option values in the up/down states.
**Example:** Node S=70: 100D - 1.11B = 60 and 50D - 1.11B = 10 → D = 1, B = 36.04, Call = 1×70 - 36.04 = 33.96. Node S=35: 50D - 1.11B = 10 and 25D - 1.11B = 0 → D = 0.4, B = 9.01, Call = 0.4×35 - 9.01 = 4.99. Root node S=50: 70D - 1.11B = 33.96 and 35D - 1.11B = 4.99 → D = 0.8278, B = 21.61, Call = 0.8278×50 - 21.61 = 19.42.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (tree prices, K, r → deltas, borrowing, option value at every node).
**Tags:** binomial-model, option-pricing, replicating-portfolio, worked-example

## p.15 — The limiting distributions
**Teaches:** As the binomial time interval shortens (t → 0), the limiting distribution takes one of two forms: if price changes become smaller as t → 0, the limit is the normal distribution and the price process is continuous; if price changes remain large as t → 0, the limit is the Poisson distribution, which allows for price jumps. The Black-Scholes model applies when the limiting distribution is normal and explicitly assumes a continuous price process with no jumps in asset prices.
**Rules:** Continuous small price changes → normal limit → Black-Scholes applies; persistent large changes (jumps) → Poisson limit → Black-Scholes not appropriate.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (model selection).
**Tags:** black-scholes, binomial-model, price-process, distributions

## p.16 — Black-Scholes: setting and inputs

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given the five inputs (formula on p.17); estimating the inputs (especially variance) is JUDGMENT.

## p.17 — The Black-Scholes model (formula)
**Teaches:** The Black-Scholes call value formula, and the fact that the replicating portfolio is embedded in it: to replicate the call you buy N(d1) shares of stock (N(d1) is the option delta) and borrow K·e^(-rt)·N(d2).
**Formulas:** Value of call = S·N(d1) - K·e^(-rt)·N(d2), where d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + sigma^2/2)·t] / (sigma·sqrt(t)) and d2 = d1 - sigma·sqrt(t). Symbols: S = current value of underlying asset; K = strike price; t = time to expiration; r = riskless rate for the option's life; sigma = standard deviation of ln(value) of the underlying; N(·) = cumulative standard normal distribution. Replication: buy N(d1) shares (option delta) and borrow K·e^(-rt)·N(d2).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, r, sigma → d1, d2, call value).
**Tags:** black-scholes, option-pricing, formula, option-delta

## p.19 — Adjusting Black-Scholes for dividends
**Teaches:** If the dividend yield y (= dividends / current value of the asset) of the underlying is expected to remain unchanged over the option's life, Black-Scholes can be modified to account for dividends by discounting S at the dividend yield; the put value follows by the same logic.
**Formulas:** Dividend-adjusted call: C = S·e^(-yt)·N(d1) - K·e^(-rt)·N(d2), where d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r - y + sigma^2/2)·t] / (sigma·sqrt(t)) and d2 = d1 - sigma·sqrt(t). Put: P = K·e^(-rt)·(1 - N(d2)) - S·e^(-yt)·(1 - N(d1)). Symbols: y = dividend yield = dividends / current value of asset; S = current underlying value; K = strike; t = time to expiration; r = riskless rate; sigma = std dev of ln(value); N(·) = cumulative standard normal.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, r, y, sigma → C and P).
**Tags:** black-scholes, dividend-adjustment, put-option, formula

## p.20 — Choice of option pricing models
**Teaches:** Most practitioners valuing real options argue for the binomial model over Black-Scholes because (1) early exercise is the rule rather than the exception with real options, and (2) underlying asset values are generally discontinuous (jumpy). A binomial tree with outcomes at each node looks much like a capital-budgeting decision tree, so the key question becomes when and why the two approaches yield different value estimates.
**Rules:** Prefer binomial over Black-Scholes for real options when early exercise matters or asset values jump.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (model choice).
**Tags:** binomial-model, black-scholes, model-choice, real-options

## p.21 — The decision tree alternative
**Teaches:** Traditional decision tree analysis discounts cash flows in every branch at one cost of capital and uses probabilities to compute an expected value; these values will generally differ from option pricing model values. If you modify decision tree analysis to either (a) use different discount rates at each node reflecting where you are in the tree (the Copeland solution), or (b) discount cash flows in each branch at the riskfree rate, compute the probability-weighted expected value, and then adjust that expected value for the market risk in the investment, decision trees can yield the same values as option pricing models.
**Rules:** Single-discount-rate decision trees ≠ option values; node-specific discount rates (Copeland) or riskfree discounting plus a market-risk adjustment can reconcile the two.
**Compute:** MIXED — the tree arithmetic is deterministic; choosing node-specific rates or the risk adjustment is judgment.
**Tags:** decision-tree, option-pricing, discount-rate, copeland

## p.23 — Key tests for real options
**Teaches:** Checklist for real-option claims. (1) Is there an option embedded in this asset/decision? — can you identify the underlying asset, and can you specify the contingency under which you get the payoff? (2) Is there exclusivity? — if yes, there is option value; if no, there is none; if in between, you have to scale the value. (3) Can you use an option pricing model to value it? — is the underlying asset traded, can the option be bought and sold, and is the cost of exercising known and clear?
**Rules:** Option test (identifiable underlying + specified contingency); Exclusivity test (yes → value, no → none, partial → scale value); Pricing test (traded underlying, tradable option, known exercise cost).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** real-options, checklist, exclusivity, option-test

## p.32 — Valuing a firm with patents
**Teaches:** A firm with a substantial number of patents can be valued as a sum of three parts. Value of firm = Value of commercial products (using DCF) + Value of existing patents (using option pricing) + (Value of new patents to be obtained in the future - Cost of obtaining these patents). The last term measures the firm's efficiency at converting R&D into commercial products; if the firm merely earns its cost of capital on research, that term is zero. Using this approach you must be careful not to double count by also allowing a high growth rate in cash flows in the DCF portion.
**Formulas:** Value of firm = DCF value of commercial products + option value of existing patents + (value of future new patents - cost of obtaining them).
**Rules:** If R&D earns exactly the cost of capital, the future-patents term = 0. Do not double count: exclude patent-driven high growth from the DCF piece when patents are valued separately as options.
**Compute:** MIXED — the sum is deterministic; each component (DCF, option values, R&D efficiency) requires estimation.
**Tags:** firm-valuation, patent-valuation, dcf, double-counting

## p.33 — Value of Biogen's existing products
**Teaches:** Biogen had two commercial products (a Hepatitis B drug and Intron) licensed to other pharmaceutical firms, expected to generate $50 million per year in after-tax license cash flows for the next 12 years. Because these cash flows were contractually guaranteed, they were discounted at the pre-tax cost of debt of the guarantors (7%).
**Formulas:** PV of license fees = CF × (1 - (1+r)^(-n)) / r (annuity PV), with CF = $50 million, r = 7% (guarantors' pre-tax cost of debt), n = 12 years.
**Example:** PV of license fees = $50 million × (1 - (1.07)^(-12)) / 0.07 = $397.13 million.
**Rules:** Contractually guaranteed cash flows should be discounted at the guarantors' pre-tax cost of debt, not the firm's cost of capital.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (CF, r, n → PV = $397.13M).
**Tags:** biogen, annuity, license-fees, cost-of-debt, worked-example

## p.34 — Value of Biogen's future R&D (assumptions)

## p.37 — The real options test: patents and technology
**Teaches:** Applying the three key tests to patents. Option test: underlying asset = the product the patent would generate; contingency: payoff = PV of cash flows from development - cost of development if PV > cost, else 0. Exclusivity test: patents restrict competitors from developing similar products, but do not stop competitors from developing other products to treat the same disease (so exclusivity is real but imperfect). Pricing test: patents (as underlying) are not traded. You must therefore estimate present values and volatilities yourself, and you cannot construct replicating positions or arbitrage. The option (patent) itself is bought and sold, though less frequently than oil reserves or mines. The exercise cost (cost of converting the patent to commercial production) can be estimated fairly precisely by experienced drug firms. Conclusion: you can estimate the real option's value, but the quality of the estimate is a direct function of the quality of your capital budgeting. The approach works best for a publicly traded firm that derives most of its value from one or a few patents. There you can use the firm's market value and its variance in the option pricing model.
**Rules:** Patent option payoff: PV of development CFs > cost → PV - cost; otherwise 0. Best-case application: publicly traded, value concentrated in one or a few patents.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (test application and input quality assessment).
**Tags:** patent-valuation, real-options-test, exclusivity, pricing-test

## p.38 — Example 2: valuing natural resource options
**Teaches:** In a natural resource investment the underlying asset is the resource, whose value depends on two variables: the quantity of the resource available and its price. There is a cost to developing the resource; the profit to the owner is the difference between the value extracted and the development cost — so an undeveloped reserve is a call option on the resource.
**Formulas:** Payoff on natural resource investment = V - X if V > X; = 0 if V ≤ X, where V = estimated value of the resource and X = cost of development.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (V, X → payoff); estimating V is judgment.
**Tags:** natural-resources, real-options, call-option, oil-reserves
