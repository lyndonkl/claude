## p.81 — Eurotunnel option-pricing inputs: firm-value variance

**Teaches:** To value equity in a distressed firm (Eurotunnel) as a call option on firm value, you need the variance of firm value, which is not directly observable. It can be constructed from the observed volatilities of the firm's traded stock and traded bonds, weighted by the debt/equity proportions in the capital structure, plus their correlation. Eurotunnel's stock (London Exchange) had an annualized std deviation of ln(prices) of 41%. Its traded bonds had an annualized std deviation of ln(price) of 17%. The correlation between stock and bond price changes was 0.5, and the debt proportion during 1992-1996 was 85%. The riskless rate is the government bond rate matched to the option's life: a 15-year bond yielding 6% was used (duration approximately 11 years, matching the option life).
**Formulas:** Annualized variance in firm value = w_E^2 * sigma_E^2 + w_D^2 * sigma_D^2 + 2 * w_E * w_D * rho_ED * sigma_E * sigma_D. Symbols: w_E = equity weight in capital structure (0.15). w_D = debt weight (0.85). sigma_E = std deviation of ln(stock price) (0.41). sigma_D = std deviation of ln(bond price) (0.17). rho_ED = correlation between stock and bond price changes (0.5). Computation: (0.15)^2 (0.41)^2 + (0.85)^2 (0.17)^2 + 2(0.15)(0.85)(0.5)(0.41)(0.17) = 0.0335.
**Rules:** Match the riskless rate to a bond whose duration approximates the life of the option (here, ~11-year duration bond for an ~11-year option).
**Example:** Eurotunnel: sigma_stock = 41%, sigma_bond = 17%, correlation = 0.5, debt weight = 85%, equity weight = 15% → firm-value variance = 0.0335. Riskless rate = 6% (15-year bond).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (weights, volatilities, correlation → firm-value variance via the portfolio-variance formula).
**Tags:** real-options, distressed-equity, firm-value-variance, eurotunnel, option-inputs

## p.82 — Valuing Eurotunnel equity and debt with Black-Scholes

**Teaches:** Equity in a heavily levered firm is valued as a call option on the firm's assets with strike equal to the face value of debt. With Eurotunnel's inputs, the Black-Scholes value of the equity (call) is only £122 million even though the face value of debt (£8,865 million) far exceeds firm value (£2,312 million) — equity retains option value because of time and volatility. The implied interest rate on the debt can then be backed out from the market value of debt (firm value minus equity value).
**Formulas:** Inputs. S = value of the underlying asset = firm value = £2,312 million. K = exercise price = face value of outstanding debt = £8,865 million. t = life of the option = weighted average duration of debt = 10.93 years. sigma^2 = variance in firm value = 0.0335. r = riskless rate (T-bond rate matching option life) = 6%. Black-Scholes call: Value of call = S*N(d1) - K*e^(-r*t)*N(d2). Computed: d1 = -0.8337, N(d1) = 0.2023; d2 = -1.4392, N(d2) = 0.0751. Value of call (equity) = 2312(0.2023) - 8865*e^(-0.06*10.93)*(0.0751) = £122 million. Appropriate interest rate on debt = (Face value of debt / Market value of debt)^(1/t) - 1 = (8865/2190)^(1/10.93) - 1 = 13.65%, where market value of debt = firm value - equity value = 2312 - 122 = 2190.
**Example:** Eurotunnel: S = 2,312, K = 8,865, t = 10.93, sigma^2 = 0.0335, r = 6% → equity = £122m; implied debt yield = 13.65%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, sigma^2, r → equity value via Black-Scholes; face value and market value of debt → implied interest rate).
**Tags:** black-scholes, equity-as-option, distressed-debt, eurotunnel, cost-of-debt

## p.83 — Real options: closing thoughts

**Teaches:** Real options are everywhere, but most have no significant economic value because there is no exclusivity in exercising them. When options do have significant value, the same inputs used in a binomial model can be used in traditional approaches (decision trees) to yield equivalent value. The real payoff from real-options thinking is (1) recognizing that building flexibility and escape hatches into large decisions has value, and (2) insights into how and why companies behave the way they do in investment analysis and capital structure choices.
**Rules:** An option must be exclusive (competitors cannot exercise it too) to have significant economic value.

**Tags:** real-options, exclusivity, decision-trees, flexibility

## p.86 — Long-term post-merger evidence is negative too

**Teaches:** Managers claim markets can't see the long-term benefits of mergers, but if they were right mergers should create long-term benefits for acquiring firms — and the evidence does not support this. McKinsey & Co. examined acquisition programs with two tests: (a) did the return on capital invested in acquisitions exceed the cost of capital, and (b) did the acquisitions help the parent outperform the competition. Half of all programs failed one test; a quarter failed both. KPMG's study of global acquisitions concluded most mergers (>80%) fail — the merged companies do worse than their peer group. Many acquisitions are reversed quickly: about 20% of acquisitions made 1982-1986 were divested by 1988, and in ten-year-plus tracking studies the divestiture rate rises to almost 50%.
**Data:** McKinsey: 50% of acquisition programs failed at least one test (ROC > cost of capital; outperform competition); 25% failed both. KPMG: >80% of mergers fail vs peer group. Divestitures: ~20% of 1982-86 acquisitions divested by 1988; ~50% divested over ten-plus years.

## p.89 — The seven sins in acquisitions

**Teaches:** The seven structural mistakes ("sins") in acquisitions. (1) Risk Transference: attributing the acquiring company's risk characteristics to the target firm. (2) Debt subsidies: subsidizing target-firm stockholders for the strengths (borrowing capacity, cheap debt) of the acquiring firm. (3) Auto-pilot Control: the "20% control premium" and other myths applied mechanically. (4) Elusive Synergy: misidentifying and mis-valuing synergy. (5) It's all relative: relying on transaction multiples and exit multiples. (6) Verdict first, trial afterwards: setting the price first and doing the valuation afterward to justify it. (7) It's not my fault: holding no one responsible for delivering results.
**Rules:** Each sin is a diagnostic category for auditing an acquisition process.

**Teaches:** Poses the risk-transference test: if you as the acquiring firm are in a much safer business with a cost of equity of 10%, what is the value of the target firm to you? ## p.93 — Lesson 1: Don't transfer your risk characteristics to the target

**Teaches:** The cost of equity used for an investment should reflect the risk of the investment, not the risk characteristics of the investor who raised the funds. Risky businesses cannot become safe just because the buyer of those businesses is in a safe business. So the safe acquirer should still value the risky target at the target's own (higher) cost of equity.
**Rules:** Always discount target cash flows at a rate reflecting the target's business risk, never the acquirer's.

## p.95 — Lesson 2: Render unto the target what is the target's — not a penny more

**Teaches:** An acquiring firm may be able to borrow much more than the target could on its own, and at a much lower rate. If you build those acquirer financing characteristics into the valuation of the target, you are transferring wealth from your firm's stockholders to the target firm's stockholders. When valuing a target firm, use a cost of capital that reflects the debt capacity and cost of debt that would apply to the target firm itself.
**Rules:** Value the target with the target's own debt capacity and cost of debt, not the acquirer's.

## p.96 — Test 3: Control premiums question

**Teaches:** Poses the control-premium test in three parts: (1) You are told it is "conventional" to pay a 20% control premium (backed by Mergerstat) — how much would you pay for the target? ## p.97 — Lesson 3: Beware of rules of thumb

**Teaches:** Valuation is cluttered with rules of thumb: after carefully valuing a target with your best estimates, you will be told it is common practice to add arbitrary premiums for brand name, quality of management, control, etc. These premiums are often backed by data, studies, and services, but what they don't reveal is the enormous sampling bias in the studies and the standard errors in the estimates. If your valuation is done right, those premiums are already incorporated in your estimated value — paying a premium on top is double counting.
**Rules:** Never add arbitrary percentage premiums (brand, management, control) on top of a properly done intrinsic valuation — that is double counting.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** acquisitions, control-premium, double-counting, rules-of-thumb

## p.99 — The value of synergy: taxonomy

**Teaches:** Synergy is created when two firms are combined and can be either operating or financial. Operating synergy accrues to the combined firm in two forms. Strategic Advantages: higher returns on new investments (higher ROC, higher growth rate); more new investments (higher reinvestment, higher growth rate); more sustainable excess returns (longer growth period). Economies of Scale: cost savings in current operations (higher margin, higher base-year EBIT). Financial synergy comes in three forms. Tax Benefits: lower taxes on earnings due to higher depreciation or operating loss carryforwards. Added Debt Capacity: higher debt ratio and lower cost of capital. Diversification (questioned): may reduce cost of equity only for a private or closely held firm. Each synergy type maps to a specific valuation input, which is what makes synergy valuable.
**Rules:** Diversification as a synergy lowers cost of equity only for private/closely held firms, not for publicly traded firms with diversified investors. Every claimed synergy must map to a specific input: growth rate, reinvestment, ROC, growth period, margin, taxes, or cost of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** synergy, operating-synergy, financial-synergy, valuation-inputs

## p.100 — Valuing synergy: three-step procedure

**Teaches:** Synergy is valued in three steps. (1) Value the firms in the merger independently, discounting each firm's expected cash flows at its own weighted average cost of capital. (2) Add the two standalone values to get the value of the combined firm with no synergy. (3) Build the effects of synergy into expected growth rates and cash flows, then revalue the combined firm with synergy. The value of synergy is the difference.
**Formulas:** Value of Synergy = Value of the combined firm with synergy - Value of the combined firm without synergy, where value of combined firm without synergy = sum of the standalone DCF values of the two firms (each at its own WACC).
**Compute:** MIXED (the DCF arithmetic in each step is deterministic given inputs; estimating how synergy changes growth and cash flow is judgment).
**Tags:** synergy, dcf, merger-valuation, wacc

## p.102 — Synergy Example 3: Tax benefits (Best Buy / Zenith)

**Teaches:** Poses a tax-synergy exercise: Best Buy considers acquiring Zenith, which has net operating losses (NOLs) of $2 billion; Best Buy's tax rate is 36%. (1) Estimate the tax benefit of the acquisition — if the NOLs can be used immediately against ample taxable income, the benefit is up to NOL × tax rate = $2,000m × 0.36 = $720 million. (2) If Best Buy had only $500 million in taxable income per year, the NOLs can be used only gradually, so the tax savings must be spread over multiple years and discounted, reducing their present value. (3) If Zenith's market value is $800 million, should you pay the tax benefit as a premium on top of market value? Not automatically — the market price may already partly reflect the NOLs, and paying the full benefit away hands the synergy to the target's shareholders.
**Formulas:** Maximum immediate tax benefit = Net operating losses x Acquirer's tax rate = $2,000 million x 36% = $720 million (only if usable immediately; otherwise present value of tax savings used over time).
**Example:** Zenith NOL = $2 billion; Best Buy tax rate 36% → up to $720m tax benefit. With only $500m/yr taxable income, the benefit is the PV of $180m/yr of tax savings ($500m x 36%) until the NOLs run out (~4 years of shelter). That PV is less than $720m.
**Compute:** MIXED (NOL x tax rate and PV of staggered savings are deterministic given usage schedule and discount rate; whether to pay it as a premium is judgment).

## p.104 — Test 5: Comparables and exit multiples question

**Teaches:** Poses the relative-pricing test: (1) An analysis of other acquisitions shows acquirers have been willing to pay 5x EBIT; your target has EBIT of $20 million — would you pay $100 million? (2) What if the terminal value in your DCF is estimated using an exit multiple of 5x EBIT — that smuggles the same transaction pricing into a "DCF." (3) Your banker adds that the deal is accretive: your PE is 20, the target's PE is 10, so EPS jumps after the acquisition. All three arguments are traps: transaction multiples embed other acquirers' overpayment, exit multiples embed today's pricing into the future, and accretion is mechanical.

## p.105 — Biased samples = poor results

**Teaches:** Biased samples yield biased results. Basing what you pay on what other acquirers have paid is a recipe for disaster: acquirers on average pay too much, so matching their prices replicates their mistakes. Even sector pricing metrics may rest on firms that are not truly comparable. And using exit multiples assumes that what the market pays for comparable companies today is what it will continue to pay in the future.
**Rules:** Do not set acquisition prices from precedent-transaction multiples (a sample biased by overpayment); do not use exit multiples that hard-wire today's market pricing into terminal value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** transaction-multiples, sample-bias, exit-multiples, relative-valuation

## p.106 — Lesson 5: Don't be a lemming

**Teaches:** Two arguments are used all too often to justify acquisitions. One: "everyone else in your sector is doing acquisitions, you must too to survive." Two: "the target's value is based on what others have paid, which may be much higher than your own estimate of value." With the right set of comparable firms you can justify almost any price. EPS accretion is a meaningless measure: buying any company with a PE lower than yours will mechanically lead to EPS accretion, regardless of whether the deal creates value.
**Rules:** EPS accretion is not evidence of value creation — acquiring any lower-PE firm is mathematically accretive. Reject herd-based and comparable-shopping justifications.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** eps-accretion, herd-behavior, relative-valuation, acquisitions

## p.108 — Lesson 6: Don't let egos or bankers beat common sense

**Teaches:** If you define your objective in a bidding war as winning at any cost, you will win — but beware the winner's curse. Premiums paid on acquisitions often have nothing to do with synergy, control, or strategy, though those are given as reasons. They may just reflect CEO ego. There is evidence that overconfident CEOs are more likely to make acquisitions and leave a trail across the firms they run. Pre-emptive or defensive acquisitions — overpaying because everyone else is, or from fear of being left behind — are dangerous. If the only way to stay competitive in a business is by making bad investments, it may be best to get out of the business.
**Rules:** In a bidding war framed as win-at-any-cost, winning is losing (winner's curse). If staying competitive requires bad investments, consider exiting the business.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** winners-curse, ceo-overconfidence, bidding-wars, defensive-acquisitions

**Teaches:** Evidence from Malmendier, Moretti & Peters (2011) on market-adjusted cumulative abnormal returns in the 40 months before and after bidding wars. Winners and losers track each other before the contest. After the bidding war ends, losers' CARs drift up (to roughly +30% by month +36), while winners' CARs drift steadily down (to roughly -25 to -30%). Winning a bidding war destroys value for the winner's shareholders; losing one is followed by better stock performance.

## p.116 — Growth vs cost synergies: McKinsey delivery evidence

**Teaches:** McKinsey (2002) post-merger survey evidence: revenue (growth) synergies are usually missed — 70% of mergers failed to achieve expected revenue synergies — while cost-synergy estimation is better, with most mergers achieving 90%+ of expected cost savings. Typical revenue-synergy estimation errors: ignoring or underestimating customer losses from integration (typically 2% to 5%) and assuming growth/share targets out of line with overall market growth and competitive dynamics (no "outside view" calibration). Typical cost-synergy errors: underestimating one-time costs, using benchmarks from noncomparable situations, not sanity-checking management estimates against precedent transactions, and failing to ground estimates in bottom-up analysis (e.g., location-by-location review of overlaps).
**Data:** Mergers achieving stated % of expected REVENUE synergies (percent of mergers, N=77): <30%: 23; 30-50%: 5; 51-60%: 13; 61-70%: 6; 71-80%: 14; 81-90%: 8; 91-100%: 13; >100%: 17. Mergers achieving stated % of expected COST savings (percent, N=92): <30%: 3; 30-50%: 5; 51-60%: 1; 61-70%: 4; 71-80%: 12; 81-90%: 13; 91-100%: 25; >100%: 36. Source: McKinsey (2002) Postmerger Management Practice client survey and case studies.
**Rules:** Trust cost-synergy estimates more than revenue-synergy estimates; haircut revenue synergies for customer attrition of 2-5% and calibrate to market growth.

## p.117 — Synergy: odds of success

**Teaches:** Studies of synergy reach four conclusions. (1) You are far more likely to deliver cost synergies than growth synergies. (2) Synergies that are concrete and planned for at the time of the merger are more likely to be delivered than fuzzy synergies. (3) Synergy is much more likely to show up when someone is held responsible for delivering it. (4) You are more likely to keep a share of the synergy gains as a single bidder than as one of multiple bidders, because competition bids the synergy value into the price paid.
**Rules:** Favor cost over growth synergies; require concrete pre-merger delivery plans; assign accountability for synergy delivery; avoid multi-bidder contests to retain synergy gains.

## p.118 — Lesson 7: Stay disciplined

**Teaches:** For acquisitions to create value, stay disciplined. Five points. (1) If you have a successful acquisition strategy, stay focused on it; don't let size or hubris drive you to "expand" the strategy. (2) Realistic plans for delivering synergy and control must be in place before the merger completes. "Realistic" means benefit magnitudes that are reachable (not pipe dreams), and time frames that reflect how long it takes two organizations to work as one. (3) The best thing to do in a bidding war is to drop out. (4) Someone should be held to account for delivering the benefits — preferably the person pushing hardest for the merger. (5) Compensation for investment bankers and others involved should be tied to how well the deal works, not to getting the deal done.
**Rules:** Five discipline rules: keep to a proven strategy; pre-commit realistic synergy/control plans; drop out of bidding wars; assign accountability to the deal's champion; tie advisor compensation to deal outcomes rather than deal completion.
