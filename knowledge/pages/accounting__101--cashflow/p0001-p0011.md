## p.2 — The End Game with Cash Flows
**Teaches:** On the surface, a statement of cash flows explains how much the cash balance of a business changed during a period, and why. The statement also embeds three other things. First, it shows the company's cash earnings for the period. These contrast with the accrual earnings shown in income statements. Second, it shows how much cash the company reinvested, and where, to sustain and grow its business. Third, it shows how much cash the company raised from, or returned to, its debt and equity investors. The statement keeps the signs on cash flows: negative flows appear as minuses and positive flows as pluses. It views cash flows through the eyes of the equity investors in the company.

## p.3 — Revisiting the Cash Flow Statement (three-section structure)
**Teaches:** The statement of cash flows has three sections that sum to the change in the cash balance. (1) Cash Flows From Operations: net cash flow from operations, after taxes and interest expenses. (2) Cash Flows From Investing: divestiture and acquisition of real assets (capital expenditures), disposal and purchase of financial assets, and cash spent to acquire other firms. (3) Cash Flows from Financing: net cash flow from issuing and buying back equity, and from issuing and repaying debt, after dividend payments. The total is the net cash flow after investing and financing.
**Formulas:** Net Change in Cash Balance = Cash Flows From Operations + Cash Flows From Investing + Cash Flows from Financing. (Each term is the net cash flow of that section, with its sign preserved.)

## p.4 — Cash Flows from Operations (build-up from net income)
**Teaches:** Cash flow from operations starts with net income (equity income). Non-cash items are added back. Then changes in non-cash working capital are added or subtracted to get to cash to equity from operations. The five "plus or minus" change items together make up the change in non-cash working capital.
**Formulas:** Cash Flow from Operations = Net Income + Depreciation and Amortization + Other non-cash Expenses +/- Change in Accounts Receivables +/- Change in Inventory +/- Change in Other Current Assets +/- Change in Accounts Payable +/- Change in Taxes Due. (Net Income = accrual equity earnings; D&A and other non-cash expenses are added back because they reduce income without using cash; the five change items = change in non-cash working capital.)
**Data:**
| Cash Flow Effect | Item | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| Start with | Net Income | Equity Income |
| Plus | Depreciation and Amortization | Add back non-cash items |
| Plus | Other non-cash Expenses | Add back non-cash items |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Accounts Receivables | Get to cash to equity from operations |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Inventory | Get to cash to equity from operations |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Other Current Assets | Get to cash to equity from operations |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Accounts Payable | Get to cash to equity from operations |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Taxes Due | Get to cash to equity from operations |
| Equals | Cash flow from Operations | |

## p.5 — The Working Capital Effect
**Teaches:** Cash flow from operations embeds the change in working capital items, excluding cash. When non-cash working capital rises, cash flows fall. When it falls, cash flows rise. Non-cash working capital ties up cash and capital. So a firm with higher working-capital needs has lower cash flows from operations, at any given net income, than a firm with lower needs.
**Formulas:** Non-cash Working Capital = Non-cash current assets - Non-debt current liabilities.
**Rules:** Increase in non-cash working capital → decrease in cash flow; decrease in non-cash working capital → increase in cash flow.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (current assets ex-cash and current liabilities ex-debt → non-cash working capital; its change → sign of cash-flow effect).
**Tags:** non-cash-working-capital, cash-flow-from-operations, current-assets, current-liabilities

## p.6 — Cash Flows from Investing (build-up)
**Teaches:** Cash flow from investing combines two groups. The first is investment in operating assets: capital expenditures net of divestitures, plus cash acquisitions of other firms. The second is investment in non-operating assets: financial assets and other non-operating assets, net of divestitures of securities and non-operating assets. Outflows are minuses; inflows (divestitures) are pluses.
**Formulas:** Cash Flow from Investing = - Capital Expenditures + Divestitures of assets - Cash Acquisitions - Investments in financial assets - Investments in non-operating assets + Divestitures of securities & non-operating assets.
**Data:**
| Cash Flow Effect | Item | Why? |
|---|---|---|
| Minus | Capital Expenditures | Investment in operating assets |
| Plus | Divestitures of assets | Investment in operating assets |
| Minus | Cash Acquisitions | Investment in operating assets |
| Minus | Investments in financial assets | Investment in non-operating assets |
| Minus | Investments in non-operating assets | Investment in non-operating assets |
| Plus | Divestitures of securities & non-operating assets | Investment in non-operating assets |
| Equals | Cash flow from Investing | |

## p.7 — Operating or Non-operating Assets
**Teaches:** The investing section covers both operating and non-operating assets. The one carve-out is liquid, close-to-riskless securities. Those count as cash & marketable securities, not investing. Investment in operating assets can be internal (cap ex, net of divestitures) or external (buying other companies). It is the engine that drives growth in the operating line items, such as revenues and operating income. Deals paid for with stock will not show up here, since no cash changes hands. Investment in non-operating assets creates a separate source of value. Its payoff shows up below the operating income line, as income from cross holdings or securities, not in the operating line items.
**Rules:** Liquid, near-riskless securities → cash & marketable securities, not investing. Cap ex and cash acquisitions → operating investment; drives operating line items. Financial assets / cross holdings → non-operating investment; payoff shows up below operating income. Stock-funded deals do not appear in the cash flow statement.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classifying assets as operating vs non-operating requires reasoning).
**Tags:** operating-assets, non-operating-assets, acquisitions, cross-holdings, marketable-securities

## p.8 — Cash Flows from Financing (build-up)
**Teaches:** Cash flow from financing nets the cash the firm raises from and returns to those who fund it. Debt raised minus debt repaid gives net cash from/to debt. New equity issued, minus dividends paid, minus stock bought back, gives net cash from/to equity investors.
**Formulas:** Cash Flow from Financing = Debt Raised - Debt Repaid + New Equity Issuances - Dividends Paid - Stock Buybacks.

| Plus | New Equity Issuances | Net Cash from/to Equity Investors |
| Minus | Dividends Paid | Net Cash from/to Equity Investors |
| Minus | Stock Buybacks | Net Cash from/to Equity Investors |
| Equals | Cash flow from Financing | |

## p.9 — Debt Cash Flows
**Teaches:** Interest expenses show up in the operating cash flow section. They reduce net income and show up in deferred taxes. Debt repayments, in contrast, belong in the financing section. If debt repayments are funded with new debt issuances, the net effect on cash flows can be neutral or even positive. If total debt rises during a period, that is a cash inflow. If it falls, that is a cash outflow. Companies that plan to bring their debt down (or up) over time should expect these consequences.
**Rules:** Interest expense → operating section. Debt repaid or raised → financing section. Net debt up → cash inflow. Net debt down → cash outflow.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (interpretation of where debt-related flows sit and their net effect).
**Tags:** debt-cash-flows, interest-expense, financing-section, debt-repayment

## p.10 — Dividends and Buybacks
**Teaches:** Until the 1980s, dividends were the only cash flow that equity investors in publicly traded companies received. Paying dividends reduces the company's cash balance and puts cash in the pockets of every shareholder. Starting in the 1980s, US companies have returned growing amounts to shareholders as buybacks. To the company, buying back stock is exactly the same as paying dividends: cash leaves the company. For shareholders, the effect differs. Those who sell their shares back get cash from the company. Those who do not sell get no cash, but get a larger share of the equity left in the company. Both dividends and buybacks reduce shareholder equity on the balance sheet.
**Rules:** At the company level, dividends and buybacks are the same cash outflow, and both reduce shareholder equity. At the holder level, dividends pay all holders; buybacks pay only sellers, and non-sellers gain a larger ownership stake.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual comparison, no calculation).
**Tags:** dividends, buybacks, cash-return, shareholder-equity

## p.11 — Potential Dividends (Free Cash Flow to Equity)
**Teaches:** Free cash flow to equity (FCFE) is the "potential dividend": the cash a firm could pay out to equity investors. It builds up in three steps. Start with net income. Add back non-cash charges and adjust for the change in non-cash working capital. That gives cash flow from operations. Next, subtract cap ex and cash acquisitions and add divestitures. That gives FCFE before debt. Last, add debt raised and subtract debt repaid. That gives FCFE after debt.
**Formulas:** Cash Flow from Operations = Net Income + Depreciation and Amortization + Other non-cash Expenses +/- Changes in (Accounts Receivables, Inventory, Other Current Assets, Accounts Payable, Taxes Due). FCFE before debt = Cash Flow from Operations - Capital Expenditures + Divestitures of assets - Cash Acquisitions. FCFE after Debt = FCFE before debt + Debt Raised - Debt Repaid.
**Data:**
| Cash Flow Effect | Item |
|---|---|
| Start with | Net Income |
| Plus | Depreciation and Amortization |
| Plus | Other non-cash Expenses |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Accounts Receivables |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Inventory |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Other Current Assets |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Accounts Payable |
| Plus or Minus | Change in Taxes Due |
| Equals | Cash flow from Operations |
| Minus | Capital Expenditures |
| Plus | Divestitures of assets |
| Minus | Cash Acquisitions |
| Equals | FCFE before debt |
| Plus | Debt Raised |
| Minus | Debt Repaid |
| Equals | FCFE after Debt |
