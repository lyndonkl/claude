## p.3 — Misconceptions about Valuation (three myths)
**Teaches:** Three myths, each paired with truths. Myth 1: a valuation is an objective search for "true" value. Truth 1.1: all valuations are biased; the only questions are how much bias and in which direction. Truth 1.2: the direction and size of the bias depend on who pays you and how much you are paid. Myth 2: a good valuation provides a precise estimate of value. Truth 2.1: there are no precise valuations. Truth 2.2: the payoff to valuation is greatest when valuation is least precise. Myth 3: the more quantitative a model, the better the valuation. Truth 3.1: the more inputs a model needs, the less you understand it. Truth 3.2: simpler valuation models do much better than complex ones.
**Rules:** Prefer simpler valuation models over complex ones; expect and diagnose bias in every valuation; seek out imprecise/uncertain valuations because that is where the payoff is greatest.

## p.5 — Approaches to Valuation (the three approaches)
**Teaches:** There are three approaches to valuation. (1) Intrinsic valuation relates the value of an asset to its capacity to generate cash flows and the risk in those cash flows. It is most commonly implemented as discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation, where value = present value of expected future cash flows on the asset. (2) Relative valuation, or pricing, estimates how much to pay for an asset by looking at what others pay for 'comparable' assets. Prices are scaled to a common metric the assets all share — earnings, revenues, subscribers. (3) Contingent claim (real option) valuation: augments the value of assets whose cash flows are contingent on an event happening.

**Tags:** valuation-approaches, intrinsic-valuation, relative-valuation, real-options

## p.6 — Basis for all valuation approaches
**Teaches:** Using valuation models in investment decisions rests on two beliefs. First, that markets are inefficient and make mistakes in assessing value. Second, an assumption about how and when those mistakes will get corrected. In an efficient market, the market price is the best estimate of value; any valuation model then serves only to justify that price. Investment philosophies that claim to beat the market differ in which market mistakes they see, and in how they believe those mistakes get corrected.

**Tags:** market-efficiency, investment-philosophy, valuation-foundations

## p.7 — Discounted Cashflow Valuation (DCF): definition and requirements
**Teaches:** What it is: in DCF valuation, the value of an asset is the present value of the expected cash flows on the asset. Philosophical basis: every asset has an intrinsic value that can be estimated from its traits — cash flows, growth and risk. Information needed: (1) an estimate of the life of the asset, (2) estimates of the cash flows during the life of the asset, (3) the discount rate to apply to these cash flows to get present value. Market inefficiency assumed: markets misprice assets across time. They correct themselves over time as new information comes out.
**Formulas:** Value of asset = present value of expected future cash flows on the asset (conceptual; PV(CF) discounted at a risk-appropriate discount rate — full formula not shown on this slide).
**Compute:** MIXED — discounting is deterministic (cash flows + discount rate + life → present value); estimating those inputs is judgment.
**Tags:** dcf, intrinsic-valuation, discount-rate, cash-flows

## p.8 — Advantages of DCF Valuation
**Teaches:** Four advantages. (1) Because DCF, done right, is based on an asset's fundamentals, it should be less exposed to market moods and perceptions. (2) If good investors buy businesses rather than stocks (the Warren Buffett adage), DCF is the right way to think about what you are getting when you buy an asset. (3) DCF forces you to think about the firm's underlying traits and understand its business. At minimum, it confronts you with the assumptions built into the price you pay. (4) If you accept that value is driven by a company's cash flows, you are immunized (to the extent you have a long time horizon) from what the market thinks about your investment.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, advantages, fundamentals, long-horizon

## p.9 — Disadvantages of DCF valuation
**Teaches:** (1) Because it attempts to estimate intrinsic value, DCF requires far more explicit inputs and information than other approaches. (2) Those inputs are noisy and hard to estimate. Worse, the analyst can tweak them to reach a desired result. Analyst "quality" then becomes skill at hiding that tweaking. (3) In an intrinsic valuation there is no guarantee anything will emerge as under- or over-valued. It is possible to find every stock in a market overvalued. That is a problem for equity research analysts, who must recommend the most under- and over-valued stocks in a sector. It is also a problem for equity portfolio managers, who must stay fully (or nearly fully) invested in equities.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, disadvantages, input-manipulation, analyst-bias

## p.10 — When DCF Valuation works best
**Teaches:** DCF is designed for assets and firms that derive value from their capacity to generate future cash flows. Investments that generate no cash flows (gold, currencies, collectibles) cannot be valued with it. It works best for three kinds of investors. First, those with a long time horizon, which gives the market time to correct its mistakes and lets price revert to "true" value. Second, those who can provide the catalyst that moves price to value — for example, activist investors or potential acquirers of the whole firm. Third, those not easily swayed by peer pressure, meaning market movements that run against their value views.
**Rules:** Use DCF only for cash-flow-generating assets; match DCF to long-horizon, catalyst-capable, contrarian-tolerant investors.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, applicability, investor-horizon, catalyst

## p.11 — Relative Valuation (Pricing): definition and requirements
**Teaches:** What it is: you can estimate the "value" of any asset by looking at how the market prices "similar" or "comparable" assets. Philosophical basis: intrinsic value is impossible (or close to impossible) to estimate. The price of an asset is whatever the market will pay for it, given its traits. Information needed: (1) an identical asset, or a group of comparable or similar assets. (2) A standardized measure of value; in equity, you get this by dividing price by a common variable such as earnings or book value. (3) If the assets are not perfectly comparable, variables to control for the differences. Market inefficiency assumed: pricing errors across similar or comparable assets are easier to spot and easier to exploit. They also get corrected much faster.
**Compute:** MIXED — computing a standardized multiple from price and a common variable is deterministic; choosing comparables and controlling for differences is judgment.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, pricing, multiples, comparables

## p.12 — Advantages of Relative Valuation
**Teaches:** (1) In sync with the market: relative valuation is much more likely than DCF to reflect market perceptions and moods. That is an advantage when the price must reflect those perceptions — when the objective is to sell the asset at that price today (IPO, M&A), or when investing on momentum-based strategies. (2) With relative valuation there will always be a large share of securities that are undervalued and overvalued. Portfolio managers are judged on relative performance, against the market and other money managers, so relative valuation is more tailored to their needs. (3) It generally requires less explicit information than DCF. (4) You are playing the "incremental" game — hoping to make money by getting the next increment (earnings report, news story, etc.) right.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, advantages, momentum, portfolio-management

## p.13 — Disadvantages of Relative Valuation
**Teaches:** (1) A portfolio of stocks undervalued on a relative basis may still be overvalued, even if the analyst's judgments are right. It is just less overvalued than other securities in the market. (2) Relative valuation assumes markets are correct in the aggregate but make mistakes on single securities. To the degree whole markets can be over- or under-valued, relative valuation will fail. (3) Relative valuation only appears to need less information. Implicit assumptions are being made about the other variables that a DCF would have made explicit. To the extent those implicit assumptions are wrong, the relative valuation will also be wrong.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, disadvantages, aggregate-mispricing, implicit-assumptions

## p.14 — When relative valuation works best
**Teaches:** Relative valuation is easiest to use when three conditions hold. Many assets must be comparable to the one being valued. These assets must be priced in a market. And some common variable must exist that can standardize the price. It works best for three kinds of investors. First, those with short time horizons. Second, those judged against a relative benchmark — the market, or other portfolio managers following the same style. Third, those who can act to exploit relative mispricing. A hedge fund can buy the undervalued assets and short the overvalued ones; a long-only investor cannot.
**Rules:** Prerequisites for using multiples: many comparables + market prices + a standardizing variable; suits short-horizon, benchmark-judged, long-short-capable investors.

**Tags:** relative-valuation, applicability, comparables, hedge-funds

## p.15 — Investments that can only be priced (value vs. price by asset type)
**Teaches:** Not every investment can be valued; some can only be priced. Assets that generate cash flows can be both valued and priced. Commodities can be valued only loosely, from utilitarian demand and supply with long lags. Currencies and collectibles cannot be valued at all — only priced.
**Data:**
| Investment type | To value | To price |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Can be valued based upon expected cashflows, with higher cashflows & lower risk = higher value. | Can be priced against similar assets, after controlling for cash flows and risk. |
| Commodity | Can be valued based upon utilitarian demand and supply, but with long lags in both. | Can be priced against its own history (normalized price over time). |
| Currency | Cannot be valued. | Can be priced against other currencies, with greater acceptance & more stable purchasing power = higher price. |
| Collectible | Cannot be valued. | Can be priced based upon scarcity and desirability. |
**Rules:** If an investment generates no cash flows (currency, collectible), it can only be priced, never valued.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification framework).
**Tags:** value-vs-price, asset-classes, commodities, currencies, collectibles

## p.16 — Asset Based Valuation: A Detour
**Teaches:** You can sometimes value a business by valuing its individual assets. This contrasts with valuing it as a going concern (cash flows) or by how similar businesses are priced (relative valuation). It is used in three contexts. (1) Liquidation valuation: valuing the assets for sale. (2) Accounting valuation: valuing individual assets for accounting purposes, such as fair value or goodwill estimation. (3) Sum-of-the-parts valuation: to see whether a company is cheap as an investment, or a good target for acquisition or restructuring. To value the individual assets, though, you must still use either expected cash flows (intrinsic valuation) or the pricing of similar assets (relative valuation). So asset-based valuation is not a truly independent third approach. It is easiest when assets are separable and have stand-alone earnings or cash flows.
**Rules:** Asset-based valuation works best when assets are separable with stand-alone cash flows; it always reduces to intrinsic or relative valuation at the individual-asset level.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, liquidation, sum-of-the-parts, goodwill

## p.18 — Contingent Claim (Option) Valuation: features of options
**Teaches:** Options have three defining features. (1) They derive their value from an underlying asset, which itself has value. (2) A call option pays off only if the underlying asset's value ends up above an exercise price set when the option is created; a put pays off only if the value ends up below that price. If the contingency does not occur, the option is worthless. (3) They have a fixed life. Any security that shares these features can be valued as an option.
**Rules:** Classification rule: a security can be valued as an option if it has all three traits — value derived from an underlying asset, a payoff contingent on an exercise price, and a fixed life.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-valuation, contingent-claims, real-options

## p.19 — Option Payoff Diagrams
**Teaches:** Diagram of payoffs at expiration versus value of the underlying asset. Call option: payoff is zero (flat, at a loss equal to any premium as drawn) for asset values below the strike price, then increases one-for-one as asset value rises above the strike. Put option: payoff increases as asset value falls below the strike, and is flat (zero payoff) for asset values above the strike. Both payoff lines kink at the strike price.
**Formulas:** Call payoff at expiration = max(S − K, 0); Put payoff at expiration = max(K − S, 0), where S = value of the underlying asset at expiration and K = strike (exercise) price. (Formulas implied by the diagram; the slide shows only the payoff graph.)
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given S and K, payoffs follow directly from max(S−K,0) and max(K−S,0).

## p.20 — Direct Examples of Options
**Teaches:** Securities that are explicitly options: (1) Listed options — options on traded assets, issued by, listed on and traded on an option exchange. (2) Warrants — call options on traded stocks issued by the company itself; proceeds from the warrant issue go to the company, and warrants are often traded on the market. (3) Contingent Value Rights (CVRs) — put options on traded stocks, also issued by the firm; proceeds from the CVR issue go to the company. (4) Scores and LEAPs — long-term call options on traded stocks, traded on the exchanges.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (definitions/classification).
**Tags:** options, warrants, contingent-value-rights, leaps

## p.21 — Indirect Examples of Options (real options)
**Teaches:** Some assets and claims behave like options without being labeled as such. (1) Equity in a deeply troubled firm — one with negative earnings and high leverage — can be viewed as an option to liquidate, held by the stockholders. Viewed that way, it is a call option on the assets of the firm. (2) Reserves owned by natural resource firms are call options on the underlying resource, since the firm decides whether and how much to extract. (3) A patent or exclusive license owned by a firm is an option on the underlying product (project). The firm owns this option for the duration of the patent. (4) The rights a firm holds to expand an existing investment into new markets or new products are also options.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** real-options, distressed-equity, natural-resources, patents

## p.22 — Advantages of Using Option Pricing Models
**Teaches:** (1) Option pricing models let us value assets we otherwise could not value. Equity in deeply troubled firms, or the stock of a small biotechnology firm with no revenues and profits, is hard to value with DCF or multiples. Both can be valued using option pricing. (2) Option pricing models provide fresh insights into the drivers of value. When an asset derives its value from option characteristics, more risk or variability can increase value rather than decrease it.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-pricing, advantages, volatility, distressed-valuation

## p.23 — Disadvantages of Option Pricing Models
**Teaches:** (1) When real options (natural resource options, product patents) are valued, many inputs for the option pricing model are hard to obtain. Projects do not trade, so getting a current value or a variance for a project can be daunting. (2) Option pricing models derive value from an underlying asset, so you must first value the assets; option pricing is therefore an addendum to another valuation approach, not a standalone one. (3) Danger of double counting. Suppose an analyst uses a higher growth rate in a DCF for a pharmaceutical firm because it has valuable patents. If the analyst then also values the patents as options and adds them to the DCF value, the patents are counted twice.
**Rules:** Never count an option's value both inside DCF assumptions (e.g., higher growth for patents) and again as a separately added option value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-pricing, disadvantages, double-counting, real-options

## p.24 — In summary
**Teaches:** There are hundreds of valuation models and metrics, but only three valuation approaches. They are: intrinsic valuation (usually, but not always, a DCF), relative valuation or pricing, and contingent claim valuation. The three approaches can yield different value estimates for the same asset at the same point in time. To truly grasp valuation you must understand and use all three. There is a time and place for each, and knowing when to use each one is a key part of mastering valuation.
