## p.2 — The big picture: project purpose
**Teaches:** The corporate finance project is done in self-formed groups of 4-8 people (group size is the students' own judgment call). The premise is that corporate finance is best learned through application in real time, so the project applies everything covered in class to a real company as it happens. It is framed as a "live lab experiment" running over the 15 weeks of the course. The instructor applies the principles of corporate finance to companies he has chosen. Each student applies the same principles to their own company in parallel.
**Rules:** Group size must be between 4 and 8 people.

## p.3 — Picking your companies
**Teaches:** Each person in the group picks one company. The company must be publicly traded, with at least one year of trading history and at least one set of annual financial statements; it can be listed in any market worldwide. Each group needs a common theme, which can be broadly defined — e.g., an entertainment group can mix movie companies, TV broadcasters and syndication companies; an automobile group can mix auto makers, auto suppliers and even an auto dealership. Within the theme, groups should pick as diverse a mix of companies as possible: small and large, domestic and foreign, closely held and widely held.
**Rules:** Company eligibility — publicly traded; ≥1 year of trading history; ≥1 set of annual financial statements; any listing market allowed. Avoid: (1) financial service firms (banks, insurance companies, investment banks); (2) money-losing companies; (3) companies with large capital arms (e.g., GE and the auto companies); (4) real estate investment trusts (REITs).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (selection criteria and diversity choices).
**Tags:** company-selection, project-setup, screening-rules

## p.4 — Part I: Corporate Governance Analysis
**Teaches:** The first section of the project analyzes the company's corporate governance. Questions to answer: Is there a separation between management and ownership, and if so, how responsive is management to stockholders? What other potential conflicts of interest exist in the firm? How does the firm interact with financial markets, and how do markets get information on the firm? How does the firm view its social obligations and manage its image in society? This part sits toward the qualitative end of the quantitative-qualitative spectrum. Applies material from class Sessions 1-4. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/corpgovHP.mp4. Spreadsheets: none. Useful data sets: CEO pay by firm (Forbes); Jensen's Alpha by Industry.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative governance assessment).
**Tags:** corporate-governance, agency-conflicts, stockholders, qualitative-analysis

## p.5 — Part II: Stockholder Analysis
**Teaches:** The second section profiles who owns the firm. Questions: What is the breakdown of stockholders — insiders, individuals and institutional investors? Who is the marginal investor in this stock (the investor most likely to be trading and setting the price, which determines whether risk should be measured from a diversified perspective)? Mostly qualitative with some quantitative input. Applies material from Session 5. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/holders.mp4. Spreadsheets: none. Useful data sets: Insider Holdings by Industry; Institutional Holdings by Industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — tabulating the insider/individual/institutional ownership breakdown is deterministic from holdings data; identifying the marginal investor is judgment.
**Tags:** stockholder-analysis, marginal-investor, ownership-structure

## p.6 — Part III: Risk and Return
**Teaches:** The third section builds the firm's risk profile and hurdle rates. Questions: What is the company's risk profile — how much overall risk, where does it come from (market, firm, industry or currency), and how is it changing? What return would an investor in the stock have earned, would they have under- or out-performed the market, and how much of the performance is attributable to management? How risky is the equity and what is the cost of equity? How risky is the debt and what is the cost of debt? What is the company's current cost of capital? This part is heavily quantitative. Applies material from Sessions 6-11. Webcasts cover: (a) risk-free rate, (b) equity risk premium (ERP), (c) regression beta, (d) bottom-up beta, (e) debt. Spreadsheet: Risk&Ret.xls (if needed). Useful data sets: Betas by Industry; Jensen's Alpha by Industry; Cost of Debt/Capital by Industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — cost of equity, cost of debt and cost of capital are deterministic given inputs (risk-free rate, beta, ERP, spreads, weights); choosing those inputs and attributing performance to management is judgment.
**Tags:** risk-and-return, cost-of-equity, cost-of-debt, cost-of-capital, beta

## p.7 — Part IV: Measuring Investment Returns
**Teaches:** The fourth section evaluates the quality of the firm's investments. Questions: Is there a typical project for this firm, and if so what does it look like in terms of life (long term vs short term), investment needs and cash flow patterns? How good are the projects the company currently has on its books (measured by comparing returns such as ROE/ROC against costs of equity/capital, i.e., EVA)? Are future projects likely to look like past projects — why or why not? Heavily quantitative. Applies material from Sessions 12-16. Webcasts on identifying the typical project and measuring accounting returns. Spreadsheet: capbudg.xls. Useful data sets: ROE and Equity EVA by Sector; ROC and EVA by Sector.
**Compute:** MIXED — computing ROE/ROC and EVA from financials and hurdle rates is deterministic; judging whether future projects will resemble past ones is judgment.
**Tags:** investment-returns, roe, roc, eva, capital-budgeting

## p.8 — Part V: Capital Structure Choices
**Teaches:** The fifth section examines the firm's financing mix qualitatively. Questions: What kinds of financing has the company used to raise funds, and where do those instruments fall on the continuum between debt and equity? How large, in qualitative or quantitative terms, are the advantages to this company from using debt? How large are the disadvantages from using debt? From this qualitative trade-off, does the firm look like it has too much or too little debt? This part leans qualitative. Applies material from Session 17. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/tradeoffdebt.mp4. Spreadsheets: none. Useful data sets: Debt Ratios by Industry; Trade-off Variables by Industry.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative debt trade-off assessment).
**Tags:** capital-structure, debt-equity-tradeoff, financing-choices

## p.9 — Part VI: Optimal Capital Structure
**Teaches:** The sixth section quantifies the optimal financing mix. Questions: Based on the cost of capital approach (finding the debt ratio that minimizes cost of capital), what is the optimal debt ratio for the firm? Bringing reasonable constraints into the decision, what debt ratio would you actually recommend? Does the firm have too much or too little debt relative to its sector and relative to the market? Heavily quantitative. Applies material from Sessions 18-19. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/optdebt.mp4. Spreadsheets: capstru.xls; capstruo.xls. Useful data sets: Earnings Variance by Industry; Market Debt ratio Regression.
**Compute:** MIXED — the cost-of-capital-minimizing debt ratio is deterministic given inputs (spreadsheet-driven); imposing "reasonable constraints" and the final recommendation is judgment.
**Tags:** optimal-capital-structure, cost-of-capital-approach, debt-ratio

## p.10 — Part VII: Mechanics of Moving to the Optimal
**Teaches:** The seventh section asks how a firm whose actual debt ratio differs from its recommended ratio should get to the optimal. Two decisions: (1) speed — should it move gradually over time or right now; (2) method — should it alter the existing mix (buying back stock or retiring debt) or take new projects financed with debt or equity? It also asks what type of financing the firm should use going forward: short term or long term, in what currency, and with what special features (matching debt design to asset characteristics). Applies material from Sessions 20-21. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/debtdesign.mp4. Spreadsheet: macrodur.xls. Useful data set: Firm Value Sensitivity by Industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — firm-value sensitivity/duration analysis (macrodur.xls) is deterministic given inputs; the speed/method and debt-design recommendations are judgment.
**Tags:** debt-design, capital-structure-transition, financing-type

## p.11 — Part VIII: Dividend Policy
**Teaches:** The eighth section reviews how the firm has historically returned cash to owners. Questions: How has the company returned cash — dividends or stock buybacks? How much cash has the firm accumulated over time? Given the firm's characteristics today, how would you recommend it return cash to stockholders (assuming it has excess cash)? Roughly balanced qualitative/quantitative. Applies material from Session 22. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/dividends.mp4. Spreadsheets: none. Useful data sets: Yields/Payout by Industry; Tradeoff Variables by Industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — tallying historical dividends, buybacks and cash accumulation is deterministic from financials; the recommended return-of-cash policy is judgment.
**Tags:** dividend-policy, buybacks, cash-return

## p.12 — Part IX: A Framework for Analyzing Dividends
**Teaches:** The ninth section applies the FCFE-style dividend framework: How much cash could the firm have returned to stockholders over the last few years (cash available after reinvestment and debt payments), and how much did it actually return? Given that comparison and the firm's current cash balance, would you push the firm to change its dividend policy (return more or less cash)? How does the firm's dividend policy compare to its peer group and to the rest of the market? Heavily quantitative. Applies material from Sessions 23-24. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/dividends.mp4. Spreadsheet: dividends.xls. Useful data sets: Cap Ex Ratios by Industry; Working Capital Ratios by Industry; Debt Ratios by Industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — computing cash that could have been returned vs cash actually returned is deterministic from financial statements; the change-of-policy recommendation is judgment.
**Tags:** dividend-framework, fcfe, payout-policy, peer-comparison

## p.13 — Part X: Valuation
**Teaches:** The final section values the company with a discounted cash flow model. Questions: What growth pattern (Stable, 2-stage, 3-stage) fits the firm, and how long will high growth last? What is your estimate of the value of equity, and how does it compare to the market value? What is the "key variable" (risk, growth, leverage, profit margins, ...) driving this value? If you were hired to enhance value at this firm, what path would you choose? Heavily quantitative. Applies material from Session 25. Webcast: http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/podcasts/Webcasts/valuation.mp4. Spreadsheets: choice of valuation spreadsheets. Useful data sets: Betas by Industry; Growth Fundamentals by Industry; Cap Ex and Wkg Cap by Industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — the DCF equity value is deterministic given cash flow, growth and discount-rate inputs; choosing the growth pattern, growth period and value-enhancement path is judgment.
**Tags:** valuation, dcf, growth-patterns, value-enhancement
