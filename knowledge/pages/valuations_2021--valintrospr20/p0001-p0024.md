## p.3 — Misconceptions about Valuation (three myths)
**Teaches:** Three myths about valuation, each paired with "truths." Myth 1: a valuation is an objective search for "true" value. Truth 1.1: all valuations are biased; the only questions are how much bias and in which direction. Truth 1.2: the direction and magnitude of the bias is directly proportional to who pays you and how much you are paid. Myth 2: a good valuation provides a precise estimate of value. Truth 2.1: there are no precise valuations. Truth 2.2: the payoff to valuation is greatest when valuation is least precise. Myth 3: the more quantitative a model, the better the valuation. Truth 3.1: your understanding of a valuation model is inversely proportional to the number of inputs it requires. Truth 3.2: simpler valuation models do much better than complex ones.
**Rules:** Prefer simpler valuation models over complex ones; expect and diagnose bias in every valuation (follow who is paying for it); seek out imprecise/uncertain valuation situations because that is where the payoff is greatest.

## p.5 — Approaches to Valuation (the three approaches)
**Teaches:** There are three approaches to valuation. (1) Intrinsic valuation relates the value of an asset to its capacity to generate cash flows and to the risk in those cash flows. In its most common form, intrinsic value is computed with a discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation: the value of an asset is the present value of expected future cash flows on that asset. (2) Relative valuation, or Pricing, estimates how much to pay for an asset by looking at what others are paying for "comparable" assets. The prices are scaled to a common metric the assets all share (earnings, revenues, subscribers). (3) Contingent claim (or real option) valuation augments the value of assets whose cash flows are contingent on an event happening.

## p.6 — Basis for all valuation approaches
**Teaches:** Using valuation models in investment decisions rests on two beliefs: (a) markets are inefficient and make mistakes in assessing value; (b) you can say how and when those inefficiencies will get corrected. In an efficient market, the market price is the best estimate of value. The purpose of any valuation model is then merely the justification of that price. Investment philosophies all claim to beat the market. They differ in what types of inefficiencies they see in markets and in how they believe those inefficiencies get corrected.

## p.7 — Discounted Cashflow Valuation (DCF) — definition and requirements
**Teaches:** What it is: in DCF valuation, the value of an asset is the present value of the expected cash flows on the asset. Philosophical basis: every asset has an intrinsic value that can be estimated from its cash flows, growth and risk. Information needed: (1) the life of the asset; (2) the cash flows during that life; (3) the discount rate to apply to these cash flows to get present value. Market inefficiency assumption: markets make mistakes in pricing assets across time. They are assumed to correct themselves over time as new information comes out about assets.
**Formulas:** Value of asset = Present value of expected future cash flows, i.e. Value = sum over t of E(CF_t) / (1 + r)^t, where E(CF_t) = expected cash flow in period t, r = discount rate reflecting the riskiness of the cash flows, t = period, over the asset's life. (Stated in words on the slide; formula implied.)

## p.8 — Advantages of DCF Valuation
**Teaches:** Four advantages. (1) DCF, done right, rests on an asset's fundamentals, so it should be less exposed to market moods and perceptions. (2) If good investors buy businesses rather than stocks (the Warren Buffett adage), DCF is the right way to think about what you get when you buy an asset. (3) DCF forces you to think about the underlying characteristics of the firm and to understand its business. At minimum it brings you face to face with the assumptions embedded in the price you pay. (4) If you believe value is driven by a company's cash flows, you are immunized from what the market thinks about your investment — to the extent you have a long time horizon.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, advantages, fundamentals, long-horizon

## p.9 — Disadvantages of DCF valuation
**Teaches:** Three disadvantages. (1) Because DCF tries to estimate intrinsic value, it requires far more explicit inputs and information than other approaches. (2) These inputs are noisy and hard to estimate. Worse, the analyst can manipulate them to reach a desired conclusion; the quality of the analyst then becomes a function of how well the manipulation is hidden. (3) In an intrinsic valuation model there is no guarantee anything will emerge as under- or over-valued. It is possible to find every stock in a market overvalued. That is a problem for equity research analysts, who must recommend the most under- and over-valued stocks in their sector, and for equity portfolio managers, who must be fully or near-fully invested in equities.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, disadvantages, input-manipulation, analyst-incentives

## p.10 — When DCF Valuation works best
**Teaches:** DCF is designed for assets (firms) that derive their value from their capacity to generate future cash flows. Investments that do not generate cash flows (gold, currencies, collectibles) cannot be valued with it. It works best for investors who: (1) have a long time horizon, allowing the market time to correct its valuation mistakes and for price to revert to "true" value; or (2) can provide the catalyst needed to move price to value, as an activist investor or a potential acquirer of the whole firm could; and (3) are not easily swayed by peer pressure, i.e., by market movements contrary to their "value" views.
**Rules:** Use DCF only for cash-flow-generating assets; match DCF to long-horizon, catalyst-capable, peer-pressure-resistant investors.

## p.11 — Relative Valuation (Pricing) — definition and requirements
**Teaches:** What it is: the "value" of any asset can be estimated by looking at how the market prices "similar" or "comparable" assets. Philosophical basis: the intrinsic value of an asset is impossible (or close to impossible) to estimate. The price of an asset is whatever the market is willing to pay for it, based upon its characteristics. Information needed: (1) an identical asset, or a group of comparable or similar assets; (2) a standardized measure of value — in equity, obtained by dividing price by a common variable such as earnings or book value; (3) if the assets are not perfectly comparable, variables to control for the differences. Market inefficiency assumption: pricing errors made across similar or comparable assets are easier to spot, easier to exploit, and much more quickly corrected.

**Tags:** relative-valuation, pricing, multiples, comparables

## p.12 — Advantages of Relative Valuation
**Teaches:** Four advantages. (1) In sync with the market: relative valuation is much more likely than DCF to reflect market perceptions and moods. That helps when the price must reflect those perceptions — when the objective is to sell an asset at that price today (IPO, M&A), or when investing on momentum-based strategies. (2) With relative valuation there will always be a significant proportion of securities that are under-valued and over-valued. Portfolio managers are judged on relative performance, versus the market and other money managers, so relative valuation is more tailored to their needs. (3) It generally requires less explicit information than DCF. (4) In relative valuation you are playing the "incremental" game: you hope to make money by getting the next increment (earnings report, news story, etc.) right.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, advantages, momentum, portfolio-management

## p.13 — Disadvantages of Relative Valuation
**Teaches:** Three disadvantages. (1) A portfolio of stocks that are undervalued on a relative basis may still be overvalued, even if the analysts' judgments are right. It is just less overvalued than other securities in the market. (2) Relative valuation assumes markets are correct in the aggregate but make mistakes on individual securities. To the degree that markets can be over- or under-valued in the aggregate, relative valuation will fail. (3) Relative valuation may require less information only because implicit assumptions are made about other variables that a DCF would have required. To the extent those implicit assumptions are wrong, the relative valuation will also be wrong.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, disadvantages, aggregate-mispricing, implicit-assumptions

## p.14 — When relative valuation works best
**Teaches:** Relative valuation is easiest to use when: (1) there are a large number of assets comparable to the one being valued; (2) these assets are priced in a market; (3) there exists some common variable that can be used to standardize the price. It tends to work best for investors who: (1) have relatively short time horizons; (2) are judged on a relative benchmark — the market, other portfolio managers following the same investment style, etc.; (3) can take actions that exploit the relative mispricing. A hedge fund can buy the undervalued and sell the overvalued assets; a long-only investor cannot.
**Rules:** Preconditions for relative valuation: many comparables, market-priced, standardizable metric; investor fit: short horizon, relative benchmark, ability to go long/short.

## p.16 — Asset Based Valuation: A Detour
**Teaches:** Instead of valuing a business as a going concern (cash flows) or by how similar businesses are priced (relative valuation), you can sometimes value a business by valuing its assets. Asset-based valuation is used in: (1) liquidation valuation — valuing the assets for sale; (2) accounting valuation — valuing individual assets for accounting reasons, such as fair value or goodwill estimation; (3) sum-of-the-parts valuation — to see if a company is cheap as an investment or a good target for acquisition or restructuring. To value the individual assets, though, you still must use either expected cash flows (intrinsic valuation) or the pricing of similar assets (relative valuation). Asset-based valuation is not an independent third method. It is easiest when assets are separable and have stand-alone earnings and cash flows.
**Rules:** Asset-based valuation is easiest when assets are separable with stand-alone cash flows; it always reduces to intrinsic or relative valuation at the individual-asset level.

**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, liquidation, sum-of-the-parts, fair-value

## p.18 — Contingent Claim (Option) Valuation — option features
**Teaches:** Options have several defining features. (1) They derive their value from an underlying asset, which has value. (2) The payoff on a call (put) option occurs only if the value of the underlying asset is greater (lesser) than an exercise price specified when the option is created. If this contingency does not occur, the option is worthless. (3) They have a fixed life. Any security that shares these features can be valued as an option.
**Rules:** If a security has (a) value derived from an underlying asset, (b) a contingent payoff triggered by crossing an exercise price, and (c) a fixed life, it can be valued as an option.

**Tags:** real-options, contingent-claims, option-features

It then rises one-for-one as asset value exceeds the strike. Put option: payoff falls one-for-one as asset value drops below the strike. It is zero (flat) for asset values above the strike.
**Formulas:** Call payoff at expiration = max(S − K, 0); Put payoff at expiration = max(K − S, 0), where S = value of the underlying asset at expiration and K = strike (exercise) price. (Shown graphically on the slide.)

## p.21 — Indirect Examples of Options (real options)
**Teaches:** Assets that behave like options without being labeled as such. (1) Equity in a deeply troubled firm — one with negative earnings and high leverage — can be viewed as an option to liquidate, held by the stockholders. Viewed as such, it is a call option on the assets of the firm. (2) Reserves owned by natural resource firms are call options on the underlying resource, since the firm can decide whether and how much of the resource to extract. (3) A patent or exclusive license owned by a firm is an option on the underlying product (project), owned for the duration of the patent. (4) The rights a firm possesses to expand an existing investment into new markets or new products.

## p.22 — Advantages of Using Option Pricing Models
**Teaches:** Two advantages. (1) Option pricing models let us value assets we otherwise could not value. Equity in deeply troubled firms and the stock of a small biotechnology firm with no revenues and profits are hard to value with DCF or multiples, but can be valued using option pricing. (2) Option pricing models provide fresh insights into the drivers of value. Where an asset derives its value from option characteristics, more risk or variability can increase value rather than decrease it.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-pricing, advantages, volatility, distressed-firms

## p.23 — Disadvantages of Option Pricing Models
**Teaches:** Three disadvantages. (1) When real options — including natural resource options and product patents — are valued, many inputs for the option pricing model are hard to obtain. Projects do not trade, so getting a current value or a variance for a project can be daunting. (2) Option pricing models derive value from an underlying asset, so you first need to value the assets. Option pricing is therefore an addendum to another valuation approach, not a standalone one. (3) There is a danger of double counting assets. An analyst who uses a higher growth rate in a DCF for a pharmaceutical firm because it has valuable patents, and then also values the patents as options and adds them to the DCF value, is double counting the patents.
**Rules:** Never both embed an option's effect in DCF growth assumptions and add the option value separately — that double counts.

## p.24 — In summary
**Teaches:** There are hundreds of valuation models and metrics, but only three valuation approaches: intrinsic valuation (usually, but not always, a DCF), relative valuation or pricing, and contingent claim valuation. The three approaches can yield different estimates of value for the same asset at the same point in time. To truly grasp valuation you must understand and be able to use all three. There is a time and a place for each approach, and knowing when to use each one is a key part of mastering valuation.
