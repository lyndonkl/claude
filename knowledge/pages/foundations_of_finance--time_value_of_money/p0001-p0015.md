## p.2 — Intuition behind present value
**Teaches:** A dollar tomorrow is worth less than a dollar today for three reasons. First, individuals prefer present consumption to future consumption, so they must be offered more in the future to give up consumption today. Second, monetary inflation erodes the value of currency over time; the greater the inflation, the greater the gap between a dollar today and a dollar tomorrow. Third, any uncertainty (risk) attached to a future cash flow makes it less valuable. Other things equal, the value of future cash flows falls as the preference for current consumption rises, as expected inflation rises, and as cash-flow uncertainty rises.
**Rules:** Value of a future cash flow falls when: current-consumption preference rises; expected inflation rises; cash-flow uncertainty rises.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual reasoning, no calculations).
**Tags:** present-value, discounting-intuition, inflation, risk

## p.3 — Discounting and compounding via the discount rate
**Teaches:** The discount rate is the mechanism that factors in the three drivers of time value. It is the rate at which present and future cash flows are traded off. It incorporates: (1) preference for current consumption (greater preference → higher discount rate); (2) expected inflation (higher inflation → higher discount rate); (3) uncertainty in future cash flows (higher risk → higher discount rate). A higher discount rate leads to a lower value for future cash flows. The discount rate is also an opportunity cost: it captures the return an individual could have earned on the next best opportunity of equivalent risk. Discounting converts future cash flows into present cash flows; compounding converts present cash flows into future cash flows.
**Rules:** Higher consumption preference / higher expected inflation / higher risk → higher discount rate; higher discount rate → lower value for future cash flows; the discount rate must reflect the return on the next best alternative of equivalent risk.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing a discount rate requires estimation; the discount/compound operations themselves are deterministic once the rate is set).
**Tags:** discount-rate, opportunity-cost, compounding, discounting

## p.4 — Present value principles
**Teaches:** Principle 1: cash flows at different points in time cannot be compared or aggregated directly. All cash flows must be brought to the same point in time first. That point can be today (present value) or a future date (future value). Principle 2: a good investment rule must be based not only on how much cash you receive from an investment but also on when you receive it.
**Rules:** Never add or compare cash flows occurring at different dates without first converting them to a common point in time; investment decision rules must incorporate both the magnitude and the timing of cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (principles, no computation).
**Tags:** present-value, cash-flow-timing, investment-rules

## p.6 — Five cash flow types
**Teaches:** There are five types of cash flows: simple cash flows, annuities, growing annuities, perpetuities, and growing perpetuities. Most assets are combinations of these. A conventional bond is an annuity (the coupons) plus a simple cash flow (the face value at maturity). A stock may be a combination of a growing annuity and a growing perpetuity.
**Rules:** Decompose any asset's cash flows into these five building blocks to value it (bond = annuity + simple cash flow; stock ≈ growing annuity + growing perpetuity).

## p.7 — Simple cash flows: PV and FV
**Teaches:** A simple cash flow is a single cash flow occurring at a specified future time period t. Its present value discounts it back to today, and a present amount can be compounded forward to a future value.
**Formulas:**
- PV of Simple Cash Flow = CF_t / (1 + r)^t, where CF_t = the cash flow occurring at time t, r = the discount rate per period, t = the number of periods until the cash flow.
- FV of Simple Cash Flow = CF_0 * (1 + r)^t, where CF_0 = the cash flow today (time 0), r = the interest/discount rate per period, t = the number of periods of compounding.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs CF, r, t → PV or FV).
**Tags:** simple-cash-flow, present-value, future-value, discounting

## p.8 — The power of discounting and compounding
**Teaches:** People consistently underestimate the power of discounting and compounding. When projecting revenue growth for a small company, analysts often extrapolate a recent-past growth rate for 5, 8, or 10 years. The compounded revenues in those out-years can become huge, often implausibly so. The reverse also surprises: when discounting a future value, such as the value of a business at the end of a 10-year forecast period, a large share of the value is lost in the discounting.
**Rules:** Sanity-check long-horizon growth extrapolations, because compounding a high growth rate for many years produces extreme values; expect distant future values to shrink substantially once discounted.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (a caution about forecasting behavior; the underlying compounding math is deterministic).
**Tags:** compounding, discounting, growth-rates, forecasting-pitfalls

## p.9 — The frequency of compounding and effective annual rates
**Teaches:** The frequency of compounding affects present and future values. The stated (nominal) interest rate can deviate significantly from the true (effective) interest rate. For example, a 10% annual rate compounded semiannually yields an effective rate of 1.05^2 − 1 ≈ 10.25% (slide writes ".10125 or 10.25%").
**Formulas:** Effective Annual Rate = (1 + r/t)^t − 1, where r = the stated annual rate and t = the number of compounding periods per year; for continuous compounding, Effective Annual Rate = e^r − 1. Semiannual example: Effective Rate = 1.05^2 − 1 = 10.25%.
**Data:**
| Frequency | Stated Rate | t | Formula | Effective Annual Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Annual | 10% | 1 | r | 10.00% |
| Semi-Annual | 10% | 2 | (1+r/2)^2 − 1 | 10.25% |
| Monthly | 10% | 12 | (1+r/12)^12 − 1 | 10.47% |
| Daily | 10% | 365 | (1+r/365)^365 − 1 | 10.5156% |
| Continuous | 10% | — | e^r − 1 | 10.5171% |
**Example:** 10% annual rate with semiannual compounding: (1 + 0.10/2)^2 − 1 = 1.05^2 − 1 = 10.25% effective.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (stated rate r and compounding frequency t → effective annual rate).

## p.11 — Present value of an annuity
**Teaches:** The PV of an annuity can be found by discounting each cash flow back to the present and summing, but there is a closed-form shortcut.
**Formulas:** PV of an Annuity = PV(A, r, n) = A * [ (1 − 1/(1+r)^n) / r ], where A = the constant periodic cash flow (annuity payment), r = the discount rate per period, n = the number of periods.
**Example:** PV of $1000 a year for 5 years at r = 10%: PV = $1000 * [ (1 − 1/(1.10)^5) / 0.10 ] = $3,791.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs A, r, n → PV).
**Tags:** annuity, present-value, annuity-formula

## p.13 — Present value of a growing annuity
**Teaches:** The PV of a growing annuity can be estimated in all cases but one: when the growth rate equals the discount rate (g = r), the formula breaks down. In that specific case, the present value equals the nominal sum of the annuities over the period, without the growth effect.
**Formulas:** PV of a Growing Annuity = PV(A, r, g, n) = A(1+g) * [ (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n) / (r − g) ]. Symbols: A = the current (time-0) cash flow; g = the constant expected growth rate; r = the discount rate per period; n = the number of periods.
**Rules:** If g = r, do not use the formula (division by zero); instead the PV equals the nominal sum of the payments without growth.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs A, r, g, n → PV, with the g = r special case handled separately).
**Tags:** growing-annuity, present-value, growth-rate

## p.14 — Perpetuity
**Teaches:** A perpetuity is a constant cash flow at regular intervals forever. Forever may be a tough concept for humans to grasp, but it makes the mathematics much simpler.
**Formulas:** PV of Perpetuity = A / r, where A = the constant periodic cash flow and r = the discount rate per period.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs A, r → PV).
**Tags:** perpetuity, present-value

## p.15 — Growing perpetuity
**Teaches:** A growing perpetuity is a cash flow expected to grow at a constant rate forever. Its present value is the next year's expected cash flow divided by the difference between the discount rate and the growth rate.
**Formulas:** PV of Growing Perpetuity = CF_1 / (r − g), where CF_1 = the expected cash flow next year, g = the constant growth rate, r = the discount rate. (Implicitly requires r > g.)
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs CF_1, r, g → PV), though estimating g and r is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** growing-perpetuity, present-value, gordon-growth, terminal-value
