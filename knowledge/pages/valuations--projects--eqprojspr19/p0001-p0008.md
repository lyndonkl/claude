## p.3 — Step 1: Pick the companies

**Teaches:** Each group picks one company per member. Together, the set must cover the hard valuation cases. First, one firm must have negative earnings in the most recent financial period. Use year-to-date results if you have them, or else the most recent financial year. Second, one firm must have high growth ahead — revenues expected to grow more than 25% in the near future. That firm could also be losing money, which lets one pick meet two tests at once. Third, one firm must be non-U.S., ideally valued in its local currency and local listing. Fourth, one firm must be a service firm, such as a retail firm or a bank. Damodaran's resource page for both high-growth and negative-earnings firms is http://www.stern.nyu.edu/~adamodar/New_Home_Page/eqass.htm.

**Rules:**
- The group's company set must include at least one each of: negative-earnings firm, high-growth firm, non-U.S. firm (local currency/listing preferred), and service firm.
- "High-growth potential" threshold: expected revenue growth > 25% over the near future.
- One company may satisfy two criteria (e.g., a money-losing high-growth firm).

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (company selection against qualitative and threshold criteria).
**Tags:** company-selection, negative-earnings, high-growth, emerging-markets, service-firms

## p.4 — Step 2: Intrinsic (DCF) valuation

**Teaches:** Start intrinsic valuation by building a narrative for the company. The narrative is your story of how the firm will evolve, given its market and its rivals. Tie that story to the key numbers you will use as inputs. Then value the stock with a discounted cash flow model. Test how much the value shifts when the story changes. Parts 1 and 2 (picking firms and intrinsic valuation) are due midway through the semester, on March 29. This is Damodaran's "narrative and numbers" method: every DCF input should be backed by the story you tell about the firm.

**Compute:** MIXED — the story and the input choices are JUDGMENT. The DCF value is DETERMINISTIC once cash flows, growth, and the discount rate are set (inputs → value per share). Sensitivity runs are deterministic re-runs over story changes you pick by judgment.
**Tags:** intrinsic-valuation, dcf-valuation, narrative-and-numbers, sensitivity-analysis

## p.5 — Step 3: Relative valuation against comparables

**Teaches:** Build a list of "comparable" firms, using criteria you think fit. Pick one multiple to compare firms across the group. You may need to try a few multiples before you settle on one. Then judge your firm against its peers with that multiple, in two ways. First, use simple techniques: adjust the peer average for differences, in a qualitative way. Second, run a sector regression: regress the multiple across firms in the sector on its key drivers. Data on the peer firms can be downloaded from Damodaran's website.

**Rules:**
- Choose peers by clear criteria you can defend.
- Try a few multiples, then pick one for the whole group.
- Use both the adjusted peer average and a sector regression.

**Compute:** MIXED — picking peers and the multiple is JUDGMENT. The peer average and the sector regression are DETERMINISTIC once the sample is set (multiple vs. drivers → predicted multiple → implied value).
**Tags:** relative-valuation, multiples, comparable-firms, sector-regression

## p.6 — Step 4: Relative valuation against the market

**Teaches:** Take the latest market-wide regression posted on Damodaran's site. Combine it with the multiple you chose in Step 3. Use the pair to judge whether your firm is under- or over-valued against the whole market, not just its sector. If your non-U.S. firm has an ADR listed in the U.S., you may use the U.S. market regression. As an optional stretch, run your own regression of the multiple in the foreign market on the variables that drive it. Use the 50 largest firms there to cut the workload.

**Rules:**
- Non-U.S. firm with a U.S.-listed ADR → the U.S. market regression may be used.
- Optional foreign-market regression: the sample can be just the 50 largest firms in that market.

**Compute:** MIXED — plugging the firm's numbers into the posted regression gives a predicted multiple. Comparing it with the actual multiple is DETERMINISTIC (numbers → predicted multiple → under/over-valued signal). Choosing the variables and reading the result is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** market-regression, relative-valuation, multiples, adr

## p.7 — Step 5: When to use option pricing models

**Teaches:** Option pricing (valuing equity as a call option on the firm's assets) is only applied to the group's negative-earnings firm, and only when that firm is highly levered. The decision is a simple threshold rule on market-value leverage.

**Rules:**
- If the negative-earnings firm has a debt-to-capital ratio exceeding 50% in market value terms, value it using the option pricing model.
- If its market-value debt-to-capital ratio does not exceed 50%, do not use the option pricing model.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC decision rule (market-value debt-to-capital ratio → use/don't-use option pricing model); the option valuation itself, if triggered, requires further inputs.
**Tags:** option-pricing, distressed-equity, leverage, decision-rule, negative-earnings

## p.8 — Step 6: Final value estimate and recommendation

**Teaches:** Before you make your call, check the news from your analysis period. Ask whether any event has changed your narrative, and with it your valuation. Update for that news. Then line up the values from all three methods: discounted cash flow, relative valuation, and option pricing. Reconcile the differing value estimates into one view. Close with a final buy, sell, or hold call on the stocks in the group.

**Rules:**
- Update the narrative and the valuation for events during the analysis period before you recommend.
- Reconcile the DCF, relative, and option-based values; do not report them in isolation.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (weighing clashing value estimates and making the call is reasoning, not a formula).
**Tags:** final-recommendation, value-reconciliation, dcf-valuation, relative-valuation, option-pricing
