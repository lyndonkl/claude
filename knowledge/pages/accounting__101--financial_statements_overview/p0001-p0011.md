## p.2 — The Accountant's Role
**Teaches:** Damodaran's view (which he notes many accountants will dispute) is that accounting has three jobs. It should check transactions and operations as they occur. It should record them in a consistent manner. It should report the results in standardized form. It is NOT accounting's role to forecast the future, no matter how tempted, or to value assets or operations. Bluntly put, an accountant is a historian who chronicles events that have already occurred, not one who predicts the future. So read accounting statements as records of the past, not as estimates of value or forecasts.
**Rules:** Accounting DOES: check, record consistently, report in standardized form. Accounting does NOT: forecast the future or value assets/operations. Treat accounting numbers as historical records, not valuations.

## p.3 — The Accounting Questions
**Teaches:** Accounting exists to answer three questions. (1) What do you own? List the assets a business has invested in, how much it spent on them, and perhaps what those assets are worth today. (2) What do you owe? Specify the contractual commitments a business must meet to stay in business. This includes all borrowings but is not restricted to them. (3) How much money did you make? Measure the profits of the business, both with accounting judgments on expenses and based on cash in and cash out. These three questions map directly to the three financial statements on the next page.
**Rules:** "What do you owe" must include all borrowings but is broader than borrowings alone (other contractual commitments count too). Profitability should be measured both on an accrual (accounting-judgment) basis and a cash basis.

## p.4 — The Three Accounting Statements
**Teaches:** There are three core financial statements. The balance sheet sums up what a firm owns and owes at a point in time. It also gives an estimate of what equity is worth through accounting eyes. The income statement reports how much a business earned during the period, with detail on revenues and expenses. The statement of cash flows reports cash inflows and outflows to the firm during the period. It allows a measure of cash earnings, as opposed to accounting earnings. Key point: the balance sheet is a snapshot at a point in time, while the other two cover a period of time.

## p.5 — Balance Sheet Structure
**Teaches:** The balance sheet lays out assets on one side and liabilities plus equity on the other. Assets fall into four groups. Fixed Assets are long-lived physical assets. Current Assets are short-lived assets. Financial Assets are investments in securities and other businesses. Intangible Assets are assets which are not physical. The other side has four groups too. Current Liabilities are short-term obligations. Debt is long-term debt. Other Liabilities are other long-term obligations. Equity is shareholders' equity.

## p.6 — Income Statement Structure
**Teaches:** The income statement is a cascade from revenues down to net income. Start with Revenues: the accountant's estimate of sales the business generated during the period. Net out Cost of Goods Sold (direct costs of producing the products or services sold) to get Gross Profit, a measure of production profitability. Net out Other Operating Expenses — SG&A and other operating costs not directly tied to production — to get Operating Profit, the profitability of the business. Net out Financial Expenses (costs of non-equity capital, especially debt) to get Taxable Income, the income for equity investors before taxes. Net out Taxes to get Net Income, the income for equity investors after taxes.
**Formulas:** Gross Profit = Revenues − Cost of Goods Sold. Operating Profit = Gross Profit − Other Operating Expenses. Taxable Income = Operating Profit − Financial Expenses. Net Income = Taxable Income − Taxes. (Revenues = sales for the period; COGS = direct production costs; Other Operating Expenses = SG&A and similar; Financial Expenses = cost of non-equity capital, mainly interest; Taxes = taxes due on taxable income.)
**Data:**
| Step | Item | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Start with | Revenues | Accountant's estimate of revenues/sales generated by transactions during the period |
| Net out | Cost of Goods Sold | Expenses of producing products/services that represent top-line sales |
| To get | Gross Profit | Production profitability |
| Net out | Other Operating Expenses | SG&A and other operating expenses not directly tied to producing goods and services |
| To get | Operating Profit | Profitability of business |
| Net out | Financial Expenses | Expenses associated with use of non-equity capital, especially debt |
| To get | Taxable Income | Income for equity investors, prior to taxes |
| Net out | Taxes | Taxes due on taxable income |
| To get | Net Income | Income for equity investors, after taxes |

## p.7 — Statement of Cash Flows Structure
**Teaches:** The statement of cash flows sums three parts into the net change in cash. Cash Flows From Operations is the net cash flow from operations, after taxes and interest expenses. Cash Flows From Investing covers buying and selling real assets (capital expenditures), buying and selling financial assets, and cash spent buying other firms. Cash Flows from Financing is the net cash flow from issuing and buying back equity, and from issuing and repaying debt, after dividend payments. The sum of the three equals the Net Change in Cash Balance for the period.
**Formulas:** Net Change in Cash Balance = Cash Flows From Operations + Cash Flows From Investing + Cash Flows From Financing. (Operations = net operating cash after taxes and interest; Investing = capex/divestitures, purchases/disposals of financial assets, firm acquisitions; Financing = equity issues/repurchases, debt issues/repayments, minus dividends.)

## p.8 — Interconnections Between the Three Statements
**Teaches:** The three statements are linked. Depreciation on fixed assets ties the income statement to the balance sheet: the expense reduces fixed-asset values. Net income or loss flows from the income statement into the balance sheet (through equity and retained earnings). It also flows into the cash flow statement as its starting point. Operating, investing and financing effects tie the cash flow statement to the balance sheet, since they change its accounts, including the cash balance. No statement can be fully understood alone.

## p.9 — The Accounting Standards: GAAP and IFRS
**Teaches:** Accounting is a rule-driven process. Over time those rules have been formalized, above all for publicly traded companies. Two concerns drive this. One is standardization, so firms can be compared. The other is first principles, so that earnings, asset value and cash flows measure what they are supposed to measure. Accounting standards worldwide remain different, but they have largely converged around two standards. GAAP (Generally Accepted Accounting Principles) covers US financial reporting; its rules come from FASB, the Financial Accounting Standards Board. IFRS (International Financial Reporting Standards) covers companies listed globally; its rules come from IASB, the International Accounting Standards Board. About 90 countries followed IFRS as of 2020.
**Rules:** US financial reporting → GAAP (FASB); most globally listed companies (~90 countries as of 2020) → IFRS (IASB).

## p.11 — The Bottom Line: Why Analysts Must Understand Accounting
**Teaches:** The raw material for financial analysis and valuation almost always takes the form of accounting statements. So analysts must grasp how accountants think when they put these statements together, even if they disagree with them. The challenge is that accounting thinking keeps changing over time. Analysts have to track those changes — both the what and the why — to keep up.
