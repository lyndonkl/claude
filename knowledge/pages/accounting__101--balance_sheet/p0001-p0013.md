## p.2 — The Balance Sheet: Dueling Views
**Teaches:** Three rival views exist on what a balance sheet should measure. View 1, record of capital invested: it should record how much a business has put into its assets-in-place, the assets that let current operations run. This is Damodaran's own preferred view. View 2, measure of current value: a large, perhaps dominant school of accounting rule-writers holds that it should reflect the value of the business today (fair value). View 3, liquidation value: a third school, led by lenders to the firm, holds that it should show what you would get if you sold off the firm's assets today.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual framing; choosing a view requires reasoning about purpose).
**Tags:** balance-sheet, capital-invested, fair-value, liquidation-value

## p.4 — Fixed and Current Assets: cost vs fair value
**Teaches:** The old way valued fixed and current assets at original cost, net of accounting depreciation. The new way follows the fair value standard: assets are marked to current market value. The gap between the two approaches varies in a set pattern. It is larger for older assets than newer ones, and larger for fixed assets than current assets.
**Rules:** Cost-vs-fair-value divergence is larger for (a) older assets than newer assets, and (b) fixed assets than current assets.

## p.5 — Financial Assets: accounting for cross-holdings
**Teaches:** Financial assets are holdings of securities or part-ownership of other companies, private or public. Holdings of publicly traded securities are now almost always marked to current market prices. For equity stakes in other companies, the accounting depends on the size of the stake and the intent behind it.
**Rules:**
- Majority stake (>50%): full consolidation. All of the subsidiary's revenues and operating income go into the parent's financials. The portion not owned appears as minority (non-controlling) interest on the liability side.
- Minority stake (<50%): the actual stake is shown as an asset.
- Minority stake held for trading: marked to market. Minority stake held as a long-term investment: shown at book value.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given ownership % and holding intent → accounting treatment), though classifying "held for trading" vs "long-term" involves judgment.
**Tags:** financial-assets, cross-holdings, consolidation, minority-interest, mark-to-market

## p.6 — Intangible Assets: talk vs reality
**Teaches:** Accountants talk a big game on intangible assets. From that talk, you would think they know how to value the big intangibles: brand name, management quality, and so on. In reality they are much better at valuing small-bore intangibles like licenses and customer lists. For those, the earnings and cash flows are easy to see and to forecast. They remain poor at valuing the big intangibles.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (valuing intangibles requires estimation).
**Tags:** intangible-assets, brand-value, accounting-limitations

## p.7 — Goodwill: The Most Dangerous Intangible
**Teaches:** Most intangible assets on balance sheets worldwide take the form of one item: goodwill. Goodwill is a plug variable that signifies little. It appears only when a company does an acquisition. It equals the price paid minus the target company's asset value (a "dressed up" book value). It shows up as an asset only because without it the balance sheet would not balance.
**Formulas:** Goodwill = Price paid for the target − Target's restated (dressed-up) book value of assets. Symbols: price paid = what the buyer paid in the deal; restated book value = the target's asset book value, restated at the time of the deal.
**Compute:** MIXED — price paid and target book value → goodwill is DETERMINISTIC; the "dressed up" restating of target assets is JUDGMENT.

## p.8 — Goodwill Impairment: information or make-work?
**Teaches:** Under old rules (much of the last century), goodwill from an acquisition was written off on autopilot. It was often amortized over long periods in equal installments. In the late 1990s both GAAP and IFRS rewrote the rules. Accountants must now revisit goodwill estimates each year and judge whether goodwill has been impaired. They re-value the target company: if value went up, goodwill stays; if value fell, goodwill is impaired. The stated goal was to inform markets. But impairments often track market pricing moves in the sector and lag them by months or years, so their effect on stock prices has been negligible.
**Rules:** Annual impairment test: if the re-estimated value of the acquired target has increased → goodwill left unchanged; if decreased → goodwill impaired (written down).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (impairment requires re-valuing the target each year).
**Tags:** goodwill-impairment, gaap, ifrs, amortization, market-information

## p.9 — Current Liabilities: three groups and working-capital treatment
**Teaches:** Current liabilities break into three groups. Group 1: those that bear no interest, such as accounts payable and supplier credit; these are part of normal operations. Group 2: short-term borrowings that bear interest, such as commercial paper, short-term debt, and the part of long-term debt due within a year. Group 3: deferred salaries, taxes, and other amounts due in the short term. When computing non-cash working capital, leave out the interest-bearing short-term debt and move it into the debt column instead.
**Rules:** In non-cash working capital, do NOT include interest-bearing short-term debt as a current liability — reclassify it as debt.

## p.10 — Debt Due: three forms of borrowing
**Teaches:** When firms borrow money, it takes three forms. Corporate bonds are debt raised from public markets. Bank loans are debt from banks and other lenders. Lease debt comes from lease contracts that require lease payments in future years. Until 2019 only capital leases counted as debt; since 2019, operating lease commitments count too. The mark-to-market push seen on the asset side has been muted on the liability side. Bank debt is mostly recorded at the amount first borrowed, and corporate bonds due are mostly not marked to market.
**Rules:** Since 2019, operating lease commitments must be treated as debt (previously only capital leases).

## p.12 — Shareholders' Equity: old ways, new ways, cynical view
**Teaches:** Under old accounting, shareholders' equity reflected the firm's entire history. It started with the equity brought in at founding, added equity raised over time, and added cumulated retained earnings. Under new accounting, it reflects the jumbled mess of mark-to-market rules, with all their contradictions. Damodaran's cynical view: either way, book equity has little hope of ever measuring the intrinsic value of equity in a business. Accounting's quixotic quest to make it do so will do more damage than good.

## p.13 — More on shareholders' equity: five caveats
**Teaches:** Five caveats for reading shareholders' equity. (1) Par value is a throwback in time and should be ignored. (2) Company age: equity reflects a firm's cumulated history of equity raises and retained earnings. So young firms tend to have far less shareholders' equity than older firms of equal market value. (3) Capitalization effects: only capitalized expenses become part of assets. Equity can thus be skewed by rules and corporate choices on what is capitalized versus expensed. (4) Buyback effects: both dividends and buybacks reduce shareholders' equity, but the sheer size of buybacks makes their effect more dramatic. (5) Negative equity: there is no mathematical reason equity cannot turn negative. It can happen after extended losses or after large buybacks or write-offs.
**Rules:** Ignore par value. Buybacks and dividends reduce book equity. Negative book equity is possible and does not by itself imply insolvency of value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (interpreting book equity across companies requires context on age, capitalization policy, and buyback history).
**Tags:** shareholders-equity, buybacks, negative-equity, par-value, capitalization
