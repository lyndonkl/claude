## p.281 — Value of Harman International: Before Synergy

**Teaches:** Values Harman International as a stand-alone firm (target of a potential Tata Motors acquisition) using a stable-growth perpetuity DCF. With cost of capital 9.67%, expected FCFF next year (2014) of $166.85 million, and perpetual growth of 2.75% in revenues, operating income, depreciation, capex and non-cash working capital, the operating assets are worth $2,476 million. ## p.283 — Independent investments are the exception

**Teaches:** All prior examples analyzed stand-alone investments, where the job was simply to estimate expected cash flows and discount at the right rate. In the real world most investments are NOT independent: taking one investment can mean rejecting another (mutually exclusive projects) at one extreme, or being locked into a future investment (pre-requisite) at the other. More generally, accepting an investment can create side costs for a firm's existing investments in some cases and side benefits for others.

## p.284 — I. Mutually Exclusive Investments

**Teaches:** A good stand-alone investment has positive NPV and generates accounting returns (ROC, ROE) and an IRR exceeding the corresponding costs (cost of capital, cost of equity). But firms sometimes must choose between investments, either because they are mutually exclusive (both serve the same purpose, so taking one makes the other redundant) or because the firm has limited capital and cannot take every positive-NPV/high-IRR project. Crucially, the two standard DCF measures — NPV and IRR — can yield DIFFERENT choices when ranking investments against each other.

**Rules:** Stand-alone acceptance: positive NPV; ROC > cost of capital; ROE > cost of equity; IRR > hurdle rate. Ranking conflicts arise under mutual exclusivity or capital rationing.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — conceptual.

**Tags:** mutually-exclusive, npv, irr, capital-rationing

## p.285 — Comparing projects with the same (or similar) lives

**Teaches:** For investments with the same lives, you can rank by accounting returns (ROC/ROE — pick higher), by NPV (pick higher NPV), or by IRR (pick higher IRR). Accounting-return rankings understandably can differ from DCF rankings, but you would expect NPV and IRR to agree since both are time-weighted, incremental cash-flow return measures — yet (as the following slides show) they can still disagree.

**Rules:** Same-life comparison options: highest ROC/ROE, highest NPV, or highest IRR.

## p.291 — Capital Rationing, Uncertainty and Choosing a Rule

**Teaches:** Which decision rule a firm gravitates to depends on its circumstances. A business with limited access to capital, a stream of surplus-value (positive-NPV) projects, and more uncertain project cash flows is much more likely to use IRR — small, high-growth companies and private businesses fit this profile. A business with substantial funds on hand, good capital access, limited surplus-value projects, and more certain cash flows is much more likely to use NPV. As firms go public and grow, they gain more from using NPV.

**Rules:** Capital-constrained + many good projects + uncertain cash flows → IRR is the more defensible rule. Capital-rich + few surplus projects + certain cash flows → NPV. Public/large firms → NPV.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — rule-selection heuristics.

**Tags:** capital-rationing, decision-rules, irr, npv

## p.293 — An Alternative to IRR with Capital Rationing: Profitability Index

**Teaches:** Under capital rationing, NPV's weakness is that it is an absolute dollar value. **Rules:** With capital rationing, rank projects by PI (NPV per dollar of initial investment) rather than raw NPV.

## p.296 — NPV, IRR and the Reinvestment Rate Assumption

**Teaches:** The NPV rule assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the hurdle rate (based on what projects of comparable risk should earn). The IRR rule assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the IRR itself — implicitly assuming the firm has an infinite stream of projects yielding similar IRRs. Conclusion: when the IRR is high (the project creates significant surplus value) and the project life is long, the IRR will overstate the true return on the project.

**Rules:** High IRR + long project life → IRR overstates the true return; prefer NPV or a modified IRR.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — conceptual.

**Tags:** reinvestment-assumption, irr, npv, hurdle-rate

## p.297 — Solution to Reinvestment Rate Problem: Modified IRR

**Teaches:** The Modified Internal Rate of Return (MIRR) fixes IRR's reinvestment bias. Compound each intermediate cash flow forward to the end of the project at the hurdle rate (here 15%) to get a terminal value. Then solve for the rate that equates the initial investment to that terminal value. Example (Figure 6.3): invest $1,000; cash flows $300, $400, $500, $600 in years 1–4. Reinvested at 15%: 300(1.15)^3 = $456; 400(1.15)^2 = $529; 500(1.15) = $575; 600 = $600; Terminal Value = $2,160. Ordinary IRR = 24.89%; Modified IRR = 21.23% (the rate r solving 1000(1+r)^4 = 2160).

**Formulas:** Terminal Value = sum over t of CF_t × (1 + hurdle rate)^(n−t), where CF_t = cash flow in year t and n = project life. MIRR solves: Initial Investment × (1 + MIRR)^n = Terminal Value.

**Example:** −$1,000; +300/+400/+500/+600 over 4 years; hurdle 15% → TV $2,160; IRR 24.89% vs. MIRR 21.23%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — cash flows + hurdle rate + life → terminal value and MIRR.

**Tags:** mirr, irr, reinvestment-assumption, hurdle-rate

## p.298 — Why NPV and IRR may differ even with same lives

**Teaches:** Summary of the three sources of NPV/IRR divergence for same-life projects. (1) A project has only one NPV but can have more than one IRR (multiple sign changes). (2) NPV is a dollar surplus value while IRR is a percentage return. NPV therefore tends to be larger for large-scale projects, and IRR higher for small-scale projects. (3) NPV assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the hurdle rate (what comparable-risk investments earn). IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself.

## p.300 — Why NPVs cannot be compared when projects have different lives

**Teaches:** NPVs of mutually exclusive projects with different lives cannot be compared directly because there is a bias toward longer-life projects. To make NPVs comparable you must either (a) replicate the projects until they have the same life, or (b) convert the NPVs into equivalent annuities. The IRR, by contrast, is unaffected by project life, so under an IRR rule you can simply choose the project with the higher IRR.

**Rules:** Different-life mutually exclusive projects: never compare raw NPVs — replicate to a common life or use equivalent annuities; IRR needs no life adjustment.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — methodological rule.

**Tags:** project-lives, npv, equivalent-annuity, replication

## p.301 — Solution 1: Project Replication

**Teaches:** Replicating the 5-year Project A back-to-back (a second −$1,000 investment at year 5, extending the $400 cash flows to year 10) makes the lives equal at 10 years. NPV of Project A replicated = $693, versus NPV of Project B = $478 (−$1,500, $350/year for 10 years). With matched lives, A is superior — reversing the naive comparison ($442 vs $478) from the raw NPVs.

**Example:** A replicated: −$1,000 at t=0, −$1,000 at t=5, $400/year for 10 years → NPV $693. B: −$1,500, $350/year for 10 years → NPV $478. Choose A.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — replicated cash flows + hurdle rate (12%) → NPVs.

**Tags:** project-replication, project-lives, npv

## p.302 — Solution 2: Equivalent Annuities

**Teaches:** Converts each project's NPV into a constant annual annuity over its own life at the 12% hurdle rate, making projects of different lives directly comparable on a per-year value basis. Equivalent annuity of 5-year Project A = $442 annuitized over 5 years at 12% = $122.62/year; equivalent annuity of 10-year Project B = $478 annuitized over 10 years at 12% = $84.60/year. Project A wins, consistent with the replication approach. (The slide writes this as NPV × PV(A, 12%, n years), i.e., NPV divided by the present-value-of-annuity factor for n years at 12%.)

**Formulas:** Equivalent Annuity = NPV / [PV annuity factor at rate r for n years], where PV annuity factor = (1 − (1+r)^(−n)) / r. Here: 442 → $122.62 (r = 12%, n = 5); 478 → $84.60 (r = 12%, n = 10).

**Example:** A: $442 over 5 yrs at 12% → $122.62/yr. B: $478 over 10 yrs at 12% → $84.60/yr. Pick A.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — NPV, rate, life → equivalent annuity.

## p.305 — II. Side Costs and Benefits

**Teaches:** Most projects create side costs and side benefits for the business considering them. Side costs include opportunity costs from using resources the business already owns and lost revenues on the firm's other projects (cannibalization). Side benefits not captured in traditional capital budgeting include project synergies (cash-flow benefits accruing to other projects) and options embedded in projects (options to delay, expand, or abandon). Project returns should incorporate ALL of these costs and benefits.

**Rules:** Incremental analysis must include opportunity costs and cannibalization on the cost side, and synergies and embedded options on the benefit side.

## p.306 — A. Opportunity Cost

**Teaches:** An opportunity cost arises when a project uses a resource the firm has already paid for. Such a resource must be priced at its next best alternative use. (1) Sale of the asset: opportunity cost = expected sale proceeds net of capital gains taxes. (2) Renting/leasing it out: opportunity cost = expected present value of after-tax rental or lease revenues. (3) Use elsewhere in the business: opportunity cost = the cost of replacing it.

**Rules:** Price owned resources at the next best alternative: sale → after-tax proceeds; rent/lease → PV of after-tax rents; internal use → replacement cost.

**Compute:** MIXED — identifying the best alternative use is judgment; valuing it (after-tax proceeds, PV of rents, replacement cost) is deterministic given inputs.

**Tags:** opportunity-cost, incremental-cashflows, capital-budgeting

## p.307 — Case 1: Foregone Sale? (Disney Rio land)

**Teaches:** Opportunity-cost quiz. ## p.309 — Cost of capital for Bookscape investment

**Teaches:** The online venture is riskier than Bookscape's book business, so its beta is re-estimated from publicly traded online retailers: unlevered total beta 3.02 (a total beta because Bookscape is a private, undiversified owner). The project is assumed to be funded with Bookscape's existing mix (D/E = 21.41%, Debt/Capital = 17.63%), with Bookscape's 40% tax rate and 4.05% pre-tax cost of debt. Resulting project cost of capital 18.12% — much higher than the 10.30% cost of capital computed for Bookscape as a whole, reflecting the online venture's higher risk (project-specific hurdle rates).

**Formulas:**
- Levered Beta = Unlevered Beta × [1 + (1 − tax rate) × (D/E)] = 3.02 × [1 + (1 − 0.4)(0.2141)] = 3.41.
- Cost of Equity = Riskfree rate + Beta × ERP = 2.75% + 3.41 × 5.5% = 21.48%.
- Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × (E/Capital) + Pre-tax Cost of Debt × (1 − tax) × (D/Capital) = 21.48% × 0.8237 + 4.05% × (1 − 0.4) × 0.1763 = 18.12%.

**Example:** Bookscape online service: unlevered total beta 3.02, D/E 21.41%, D/(D+E) 17.63%, riskfree 2.75%, ERP 5.5%, pre-tax cost of debt 4.05%, tax 40% → levered beta 3.41, cost of equity 21.48%, cost of capital 18.12% (vs. 10.30% for the whole firm).

## p.313 — Case 3: Excess Capacity (Vale)

## p.314 — A Framework for Assessing the Cost of Using Excess Capacity

**Teaches:** Three-question framework for pricing excess-capacity use: (1) If I do NOT add the new product, when will I run out of capacity? (2) If I DO add the new product, when will I run out of capacity (earlier)? (3) When I run out, what will I do? — If the answer is cut back on production, the cost of using the capacity is the PV of the after-tax cash flows from the lost sales. If the answer is buy new capacity, the cost is the difference in PV between the earlier and later capacity investment (the cost of accelerating the expansion).

**Rules:** Excess-capacity cost = PV of after-tax lost-sales cash flows (if production is cut back) OR the PV difference between earlier and later capacity investment (if capacity is bought sooner).

**Compute:** MIXED — forecasting capacity exhaustion dates and the response is judgment; the PV calculations from those inputs are deterministic.

**Tags:** excess-capacity, opportunity-cost, present-value, capacity-planning

## p.315 — Product and Project Cannibalization: A Real Cost?

**Teaches:** Quiz on cannibalization. If 20% of Rio Disney's revenues come from people who would have gone to Disney's US parks anyway, should you count only incremental revenues (80%), total revenues, or an intermediate number? Then: would the answer differ for a new Disney cable-channel Saturday show expected to draw 20% of its viewers from ABC (also Disney-owned)? The underlying principle: cannibalized revenue is a real cost only to the extent the lost sales would NOT have been lost anyway to competitors. It depends on competitive context. An exclusive, differentiated product like a Disney park differs from a competitive market like TV, where the viewers might have been lost to rivals regardless.

**Rules:** Count cannibalization as a cost only for sales that would have been retained absent the new project; in highly competitive settings where the sales would likely be lost to competitors anyway, cannibalization is not fully incremental.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — depends on competitive assessment.

**Tags:** cannibalization, incremental-revenue, disney, side-costs

## p.316 — B. Project Synergies

**Teaches:** A project may provide benefits to a firm's OTHER projects. Example: a typical Disney animated movie costing $50 million to produce and promote generates, beyond theatrical revenues, merchandise sales (toys, figures, clothes), increased theme-park attendance, stage shows (Beauty and the Beast, Lion King), and TV series based on the movie. In practice these synergies are often left unquantified and used loosely to justify overriding negative investment-analysis results (i.e., to rationalize negative-NPV projects). The correct treatment: if synergies exist — and they often do — they must be VALUED and shown explicitly in the initial project analysis.

**Rules:** Never invoke unquantified "synergy" to override a negative NPV; value synergies explicitly as incremental cash flows in the original analysis.

## p.319 — Estimating the cost of capital to use in valuing synergy

**Teaches:** The synergy cash flows must be discounted at a rate reflecting THEIR risk characteristics, not the acquirer's or target's overall rate. Business risk: synergies flow from optional add-ons in auto sales, so start with Tata Motors' levered beta of 1.10. Geographic risk: synergies come from India, so add India's country risk premium of 3.60% to the mature-market premium of 5.5%. Financing: expansion is entirely in India at Tata's existing debt-to-capital ratio of 29.28%, rupee cost of debt 9.6%, marginal tax rate 32.45%; the rupee riskfree rate is 6.57%. Result: rupee cost of capital of 13.63% for the synergy cash flows.

**Formulas:**
- Cost of equity (Rs) = Riskfree(Rs) + Beta × (Mature ERP + CRP) = 6.57% + 1.10 × (5.5% + 3.60%) = 16.59%.
- After-tax cost of debt (Rs) = 9.6% × (1 − 0.3245) = 6.50%.
- Cost of capital (Rs) = 16.59% × (1 − 0.2928) + 6.50% × 0.2928 = 13.63%.

**Rules:** Discount synergy cash flows at a rate matching the synergy's own business risk, geography (country risk premium), currency, and financing mix.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — beta, riskfree, ERP, CRP, debt ratio, cost of debt, tax rate → cost of capital 13.63%.

## p.320 — Estimating the value of synergy and what Tata can pay for Harman

**Teaches:** Values the synergy as a growing perpetuity starting in year 4, discounted back 3 years, converted to dollars, and added to Harman's stand-alone equity value. Value of synergy at end of year 3 = Expected cash flow in year 4 / (cost of capital − g) = 10,000 / (0.1363 − 0.04) = Rs 103,814 million. Value today = 103,814 / (1.1363)^3 = Rs 70,753 million. At the prevailing Rs 60/$ exchange rate, synergy = 70,753/60 = $1,179 million. Total value of Harman's equity to Tata = $2,678M (intrinsic stand-alone) + $1,179M (synergy) = $3,857 million. Since Harman's equity trades at $5,248 million, the acquisition still does not make sense even WITH the synergy incorporated.

**Formulas:**
- Value of synergy (Year 3) = Expected Cash Flow_Year4 / (Cost of Capital − g) = 10,000 / (0.1363 − 0.04) = Rs 103,814 million.
- Value of synergy today = Value_Year3 / (1 + Cost of Capital)^3 = 103,814 / (1.1363)^3 = Rs 70,753 million.
- Dollar value = Rs value / exchange rate = 70,753 / 60 = $1,179 million.
- Maximum value of target = Stand-alone equity value + Value of synergy = 2,678 + 1,179 = $3,857 million.

**Rules:** Pay at most stand-alone value + synergy value; if market price ($5,248M) exceeds that ($3,857M), walk away.

**Example:** Tata/Harman: CF year 4 Rs 10,000M, r = 13.63%, g = 4%, Rs 60/$ → synergy $1,179M; total $3,857M vs. market $5,248M → do not acquire at market price.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — year-4 cash flow, growth, cost of capital, lag, exchange rate, stand-alone value → synergy value and maximum price.
