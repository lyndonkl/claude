## p.42 — Market crises do not disprove markets

**Teaches:** Critics point to bubbles and crises as proof that markets do not work. The September–December 2008 turmoil is cited as evidence that free markets are the problem, not the solution. Damodaran offers two counters. First, the 2008 crisis showed we depend more than ever on functioning, liquid markets with risk-taking investors. No government, bank, or even Buffett was big enough to step in and save the day. Second, the firms that caused the collapse (banks, investment banks) were among the most regulated businesses anywhere. Their failures trace to exploiting regulatory loopholes: badly designed insurance programs and capital measurements that missed risky assets, especially derivatives.

## p.43 — Firms and society: social costs and benefits

**Teaches:** This is the fourth potential breakdown of stock-price maximization ("IV. Firms and Society"). In theory, all costs and benefits of a firm's decisions can be traced back to the firm. In practice, financial decisions create social costs and benefits. A social cost or benefit accrues to society as a whole, not to the firm making the decision. Examples of social costs: environmental costs (pollution, health costs) and quality-of-life costs (traffic, housing, safety). Examples of social benefits: creating jobs in high-unemployment areas, supporting inner-city development, and creating access to goods where none exists.

## p.44 — Why social costs and benefits are hard to quantify

**Teaches:** Three reasons social costs and benefits resist quantification:
- Cannot know the unknown: they may be unknown at decision time. A firm may think its product helps society and discover large costs only later. Asbestos was seen as a wonderful product — light and easy to work with. Its health consequences surfaced only after decades.
- Eyes of the beholder: they are person-specific. Different decision makers weigh the same social cost very differently.
- Decision paralysis: carried to extremes, weighing every social cost paralyzes decision making.

## p.46 — What can go wrong: the full agency-conflict picture

**Teaches:** A summary diagram of everything that can break in the stock-price-maximization chain, centered on Managers with four linkages:
- Stockholders ↔ Managers: stockholders have little control over managers; managers put their own interests first.
- Bondholders ↔ Managers: bondholders lend money and can get ripped off (expropriated).
- Managers ↔ Financial Markets: managers delay bad news or mislead; markets make mistakes and can overreact.
- Managers ↔ Society: firms create significant social costs; some costs cannot be traced to the firm.

This is the broken mirror image of the classical objective-function diagram.

## p.47 — When traditional corporate financial theory breaks down

**Teaches:** Traditional theory (maximize stock price) breaks down when any of four conditions holds:
- Managerial self-interest: decision makers' interests conflict with stockholders'.
- Unprotected debt holders: bondholders (lenders) are not protected against expropriation by stockholders.
- Inefficient markets: financial markets do not operate efficiently, so stock prices do not reflect underlying firm value.
- Large social side costs: significant social costs arise as a by-product of stock price maximization.

**Rules:** Stock-price maximization is defensible only if managers answer to stockholders, lenders are protected, markets are reasonably efficient, and social costs are small. If any condition fails, the objective is undermined.

## p.48 — Three possible solutions when the theory breaks down

**Teaches:** Three candidate fixes when traditional theory breaks down:
- A non-stockholder based governance system: assign the job of monitoring managers to someone other than stockholders.
- A better objective than maximizing stock prices: shift to a different metric or to different stakeholder group(s).
- Maximize stock prices but minimize side costs: keep the objective but reduce conflict. Make managers and employees into stockholders. Protect lenders from expropriation. Provide honest, prompt information to financial markets. Minimize social costs.

The following slides examine each option. Damodaran ultimately favors the third.

## p.49 — Alternative governance: German and Japanese cross-holdings

**Teaches:** Solution I examined. Germany and Japan built an alternative governance mechanism on corporate cross-holdings. In Germany banks form the core. In Japan it is the keiretsus. Other Asian countries copied Japan, with family companies at the core of corporate families. At their best, the most efficient firms in the group bring the less efficient firms up to par. They provide a corporate welfare system and a more stable corporate structure. At their worst, the least efficient firms drag down the best-run firms. The cross-holdings also make it very hard for outsiders — including investors in these firms — to tell how well or badly the group is doing.

## p.50 — End game: Managerial Corporatism

**Teaches:** A flow diagram of "Managerial Corporatism" — the equilibrium when managers face no effective check:
- Shareholders are small, dispersed, and powerless. The board acts as a managerial rubber stamp. Annual meetings are scripted, ineffective events.
- Banks and bondholders collect interest and principal but leave corporate decisions to managers.
- The sector consists of larger manager-dominated companies. They stay competitive enough to be profitable, but not too aggressive.
- The central objective: focus on managerial interests, while delivering just enough to other stakeholders to neutralize or neuter them.
- Labor is bought off with wage contracts and benefits. Employees are co-opted with packages that are just good enough.
- Societal actions happen only if they also improve managerial standing. Customer interests are served only if they converge with managerial interests.

The Managerial End Game: the surviving companies are the ones that keep managers happy, economically or with side benefits. Other stakeholders are served well or badly depending on whether their interests converge with managers'.

## p.51 — A skewed version: Crony Corporatism

**Teaches:** A flow diagram of "Crony Corporatism" — governance captured by founders, families, or government:
- A founder, family, or government official owns a controlling stake. They control the company through voting rights and a compliant board.
- Government and rule writers are co-opted or corrupted to do corporate bidding.
- Lenders, often government banks, impose few or no constraints on the company.
- The objective: maximize founder wealth, with government officials benefiting in the process.
- Market competition is reduced or eliminated. The government tilts the playing field in the company's favor.
- Rules that protect society are rewritten or not enforced. Laws on competition and monopoly power go unenforced.
- The government takes the company's side against employees. Employees get paid less to do more.
- Society bears large side costs while receiving few side benefits. Customers pay higher prices for products and services.

The Connections End Game: the most politically connected companies dominate or monopolize their markets, exploiting customers, employees, and society.

## p.52 — Solution IIa: choose a different metric to maximize

## p.55 — Confused Corporatism

**Teaches:** A flow diagram of "Confused Corporatism" — the equilibrium under stakeholder wealth maximization:
- Shareholders own the company but share control with other stakeholders. The board promotes stakeholder interests over shareholder interests.
- Lenders get paid only if payment does not endanger other stakeholders. Their rights are enforced only after balancing against other stakeholder interests.
- The firm maximizes "stakeholder wealth." It keeps the sector competitive by holding back on its own advantages. It gains market share but holds back on dominance.
- It avoids actions that cost society and actively creates societal benefits — "protect society's interests at any cost."
- It holds back on pricing power to charge less. It maximizes customer satisfaction even when that does not translate into repeat business or profits.
- Employee unions or a strong labor market even the game. Employees earn a living wage, profitability and competitive effects notwithstanding.

The Confused End Game: in trying to serve all stakeholders, none are served. Managers face no accountability, and companies become less competitive and efficient.

## p.56 — Cautionary notes on confused corporatism

**Teaches:** Two real-world warnings about multi-objective ("stakeholder") management:
- Government-owned companies: their managers got a laundry list of objectives, much like the stakeholder list, and were told to deliver on all of them. The result was some of the most inefficient companies on earth. Every stakeholder group felt ill-served.
- US research universities: they lack a central focus. Whose interests dominate shifts depending on who you talk to and when. The result is economically inefficient operations that can run deficits no matter how much tuition is collected. Every stakeholder group feels aggrieved.

## p.57 — Solution III: maximize stock price, subject to constraints

**Teaches:** The strength of stock-price maximization is its internal self-correction mechanism. Excesses on any linkage, if unregulated, trigger counter-actions that reduce or eliminate those excesses. Concretely:
- Managers taking advantage of stockholders led to a much more active market for corporate control.
- Stockholders taking advantage of bondholders led bondholders to protect themselves at the time of issue.
- Firms revealing incorrect or delayed information led markets to become more "skeptical" and "punitive."
- Firms creating social costs led to more regulation, plus investor and customer backlashes.

## p.59 — The hostile acquisition threat

**Teaches:** The market for corporate control disciplines bad managers. The typical target firm in a hostile takeover has three traits. Its return on equity is almost 5% lower than its peer group. Its stock significantly underperformed the peer group over the previous 2 years. Its managers hold little or no stock in the firm. So the best defense against a hostile takeover is to run the firm well and earn good returns for stockholders. Conversely, banning hostile takeovers mostly protects poorly run firms, not well-managed ones.

**Rules:** Hostile-takeover-target profile: ROE about 5 percentage points below peers; 2-year stock underperformance versus peers; low or no managerial stock ownership. Best takeover defense = good performance.

## p.60 — Boards are becoming more independent

**Teaches:** Trends showing US boards responding to governance pressure (1970s versus 1998):
- Boards became smaller. Median board size fell from 16–20 in the 1970s to 9–11 in 1998. Smaller boards are less unwieldy and more effective.

## p.63 — Changes in corporate governance at Disney (2004 reforms)

**Teaches:** Seven governance changes Disney adopted after the 2004 shareholder revolt:
1. Required at least two executive sessions of the board each year, without the CEO or other management present.
2. Created the position of non-management presiding director. Senator George Mitchell was appointed to lead the executive sessions and help set the board's work agenda.
3. Adopted a new, more rigorous definition of director independence.
4. Required that a substantial majority of the board meet the new independence standards.
5. Reduced committee size and rotated committee and chairmanship assignments among independent directors.
6. Added new provisions for management succession planning, plus evaluations of both management and board performance.
7. Provided enhanced continuing education and training for board members.

## p.68 — Is there a payoff to better corporate governance?

**Teaches:** Empirical evidence on governance and value. The most comprehensive study built a governance index for each of 1,500 firms, based on 24 distinct corporate governance provisions. Findings:
- Buying stocks with the strongest investor protections, while shorting those with the weakest, generated an annual excess return of 8.5%.
- Every one-point increase in the index toward fewer investor protections decreased market value by 8.9% in 1999.
- Firms scoring high on investor protections also had higher profits, higher sales growth, and made fewer acquisitions.

Separately, the link between board composition and firm value is weak, though smaller boards do tend to be more effective. Anecdotally, problem companies share a common theme: an ineffective board that fails to ask tough questions of an imperial CEO.

**Data:** Governance-index study: 1,500 firms; 24 governance provisions; long-strong/short-weak protection portfolio earned +8.5% annual excess return; each 1-point index move toward fewer protections cost -8.9% of market value (1999).

## p.69 — The bondholders' defense against stockholder excesses

**Teaches:** How lenders protect themselves against expropriation:
- More restrictive covenants on investment, financing, and dividend policy now appear in private lending agreements and bond issues. The goal: prevent future "Nabiscos" (the RJR Nabisco LBO that hurt bondholders).
- New bond types explicitly protect against sudden leverage increases or other risk-raising actions. Two examples exist. Puttable bonds let the bondholder put the bond back to the firm at face value if the firm takes actions that hurt bondholders. Ratings sensitive notes carry an interest rate that adjusts to the rate appropriate for the firm's current rating.
- More hybrid bonds carry an equity component, usually a conversion option or warrant. This lets bondholders become equity investors when they feel it serves their interests.

## p.70 — The financial market response

**Teaches:** How markets counter firms' information games:
- Analysts still issue more buy than sell recommendations. But the payoff to uncovering negative news is large enough that such news is eagerly sought and quickly revealed, at least to a limited group of investors.
- As investor access to information improves, firms find it much harder to control when and how information reaches markets.
- Option trading has become more common, so it is much easier to trade on bad news. In the process, the news is revealed to the rest of the market.

## p.75 — The modified objective function

**Teaches:** The right objective depends on market efficiency and lender protection:
- Publicly traded firm, reasonably efficient markets, bondholders (lenders) protected: maximize stock price. This will also maximize firm value.
- Publicly traded firm, inefficient markets, bondholders protected: maximize stockholder wealth. This also maximizes firm value but might not maximize the stock price.
- Publicly traded firm, inefficient markets, bondholders not fully protected: maximize firm value. Stockholder wealth and stock prices may not be maximized at the same point.
- Private firm: maximize stockholder wealth if lenders are protected, or firm value if they are not.

**Rules:** Objective selection matrix — efficient markets + protected lenders → maximize stock price; inefficient markets + protected lenders → maximize stockholder wealth; inefficient markets + unprotected lenders → maximize firm value; private firm → stockholder wealth if lenders protected, else firm value.

## p.77 — First Principles (framework map)

**Teaches:** The corporate finance first-principles map under "Maximize the value of the business (firm)." The investment-decision box is highlighted as the current topic.
- The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it (highlighted). The return should reflect the magnitude and timing of the cash flows, as well as all side effects.
- The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt for your firm and the right mix of debt and equity to fund operations. The optimal mix of debt and equity maximizes firm value. The right kind of debt matches the tenor of your assets.
- The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments that make your minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to owners. How much cash you can return depends on current and potential investment opportunities. How you return it depends on whether owners prefer dividends or buybacks.

## p.78 — The notion of a benchmark (hurdle rate)

**Teaches:** Financial resources are finite, so projects must cross a hurdle before being deemed acceptable. The hurdle should be higher for riskier projects than for safer projects. Every risk and return model in finance tries to answer two basic questions: How do you measure risk? How do you translate that risk measure into a risk premium?

**Formulas:** Hurdle rate = Riskless Rate + Risk Premium. The riskless rate is the return on an investment with no risk. The risk premium is the extra return demanded as compensation for the investment's risk.

**Compute:** MIXED — the formula itself is DETERMINISTIC (riskless rate + risk premium → hurdle rate), but estimating the risk premium is JUDGMENT.

## p.79 — What is risk?

**Teaches:** Traditional definitions treat risk as purely negative. Webster's dictionary defines risk as "exposing to danger or hazard." The Chinese characters shown on the slide, 危机, capture it better: the first symbol means "danger," the second means "opportunity." Risk is a mix of danger and opportunity — you cannot have one without the other. Risk is therefore neither good nor bad; it is a fact of life. The question for businesses is not whether to avoid risk, but how best to incorporate it into decision making.

## p.80 — A good risk and return model should…

**Teaches:** Five criteria for a good risk and return model:
1. It should produce a measure of risk that applies to all assets, not an asset-specific one.
2. It should clearly delineate which types of risk are rewarded and which are not, with a rationale for the delineation.
3. It should produce standardized risk measures. An investor seeing an asset's risk measure should be able to tell whether the asset is above-average or below-average risk.
4. It should translate the measure of risk into a rate of return the investor should demand as compensation for bearing the risk.
5. It should work well not only at explaining past returns, but also at predicting future expected returns.

**Rules:** Model-evaluation checklist: universal risk measure; rewarded versus unrewarded risk delineated; standardized measure; risk translated into expected return; explanatory and predictive power.
