## p.121 — Beyond the default spread: scaling for equity risk (Brazil/China/India)

**Teaches:** Sovereign default spreads and equity risk premiums are highly correlated, but equity is riskier than bonds, so the extra country premium for equities should exceed the bond default spread. One way to scale up is to multiply the country default spread by the relative volatility of that country's equity market to its government bond market. The resulting country risk premium is added to the mature market premium (5.50% here) to get the country's total equity risk premium.
**Formulas:** Country Risk Premium = Country Default Spread * (sigma_Equity / sigma_CountryBond), where sigma_Equity = annualized standard deviation of the country's equity index, sigma_CountryBond = annualized standard deviation of the country's government bond. Total ERP = Mature market premium + Country Risk Premium.
**Example:** Brazil: equity index std dev = 21%, C-bond std dev = 14%, default spread = 2.00%; Brazil ERP = 5.50% + 2.00%*(21%/14%) = 8.50%. China: 5.50% + 0.80%*(18%/10%) = 6.94%. India: 5.50% + 2.25%*(24%/17%) = 9.10%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs (mature ERP, default spread, equity and bond volatilities) → country ERP.

## p.122 — A composite way of estimating ERP for countries (3-step recipe)

**Teaches:** A three-step procedure for country equity risk premiums. Step 1: estimate a mature market ERP — preferably a forward-looking implied ERP for the US (Damodaran's November 2013 estimate: 5.5%). Step 2: define a mature market measurably — any AAA rated country is treated as mature. Step 3: for non-mature markets, add an additional premium, chosen either as the country's default spread (from sovereign ratings or the CDS market) or as a scaled-up default spread that adjusts upward for the extra risk in equity markets.
**Rules:** AAA rated country → mature market → ERP = mature market ERP with no country premium. Below AAA → add default spread or volatility-scaled default spread.
**Compute:** MIXED — the addition is deterministic once spreads are chosen; choosing spread source (rating vs CDS, scaled vs unscaled) is JUDGMENT.

## p.124 — Estimating ERP for Disney: incorporation vs. operations weighting

**Teaches:** Convention estimates ERP by country of incorporation (US ERP for Disney, Brazil ERP for Vale). The more sensible practice weights ERP by where the company actually operates — here by the geographic breakdown of revenues. Disney, though a US company, gets a revenue-weighted ERP of 5.76%, slightly above the pure US 5.50%.
**Formulas:** Company ERP = sum over regions of (revenue weight_i * regional ERP_i).
**Data:**

| Region/Country | Proportion of Disney's Revenues | ERP |
|---|---|---|
| US & Canada | 82.01% | 5.50% |
| Europe | 11.64% | 6.72% |
| Asia-Pacific | 6.02% | 7.27% |
| Latin America | 0.33% | 9.44% |
| **Disney** | **100.00%** | **5.76%** |

**Example:** Disney Nov 2013: 0.8201*5.50% + 0.1164*6.72% + 0.0602*7.27% + 0.0033*9.44% = 5.76%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — revenue weights + regional ERPs → weighted company ERP.

## p.126 — The anatomy of a crisis: implied ERP Sep 12, 2008 – Jan 1, 2009

**Teaches:** Chart of the daily implied equity risk premium against the S&P 500 during the 2008 financial crisis. As the S&P 500 fell from about 1250 to the 750–900 range, the implied ERP rose from roughly 4.3% to a peak near 8% (late November 2008), ending 2008 around 6.4%. The average implied ERP over 1960–2007 was about 4%. Point: the ERP is not constant — it spikes in crises, so a fixed historical premium misses the market's changing price of risk.

## p.127 — An updated implied ERP for the S&P 500 (January 1, 2020)

**Teaches:** Worked implied-ERP calculation for the S&P 500 on 1/1/2020. Base year cash to investors (trailing 12 months): dividends 57.71 + buybacks 92.80 = 150.50. Cash flows grow at analysts' top-down earnings growth estimate of 3.96% for 5 years, then at 1.92% forever (set equal to the risk-free rate). Set the present value of these cash flows equal to the index level (3230.78) and solve for the discount rate. This gives an implied expected return on stocks of r = 7.12%. Subtracting the T-bond rate of 1.92% gives an implied ERP of 5.20%.
**Formulas:** Index level = CF1/(1+r) + CF2/(1+r)^2 + ... + CF5/(1+r)^5 + CF5*(1+g)/((r-g)(1+r)^5). Here CFt = expected dividends + buybacks in year t; g = stable growth rate (set equal to the risk-free rate); r = implied expected return. Implied ERP = r - risk-free rate. Numerically: 3230.78 = 156.46/(1+r) + 162.65/(1+r)^2 + 169.08/(1+r)^3 + 175.77/(1+r)^4 + 182.73/(1+r)^5 + 182.73(1.0192)/((r-0.0192)(1+r)^5). (Note: the slide's equation shows 162.25 in the year-2 term; the cash-flow table shows 162.65.)
**Data:** Expected earnings by year: last 12 months 153.52; 2020 159.59; 2021 165.90; 2022 172.47; 2023 179.29; 2024 186.38; terminal year 189.96. Expected dividends+buybacks: 150.50; 156.46; 162.65; 169.08; 175.77; 182.73; terminal 186.24.
**Example:** Result: r = 7.12%, T-bond rate 1.92%, implied ERP (1/1/20) = 7.12% - 1.92% = 5.20%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — index level, cash flows, growth rates → solve polynomial for r → implied ERP.
**Tags:** implied-erp, sp500, dcf, buybacks, 2020

## p.129 — Composite ERP estimation procedure flowchart (January 1, 2020)

**Teaches:** The full four-step ERP estimation pipeline as of 1/1/2020. Step 1 (monthly): estimate the implied ERP for the S&P 500 as the mature market premium — 5.20% on 1/1/2020. Step 2: assess country risk via the Moody's sovereign local currency rating (if unavailable, use S&P converted to Moody's equivalent). Step 3 (updated every six months, in January and July): convert country risk into an equity premium. If the rating is AAA, add no extra premium. If it is below AAA, get a default spread from one of three sources: the US$ sovereign bond spread, the CDS spread, or a ratings table. Then multiply that spread by relative equity market volatility. Relative volatility = std dev of the emerging market equity index / std dev of the emerging market bond index = 1.18 in January 2020. If no sovereign rating exists, use a PRS composite country risk score to estimate an ERP. Step 4: country ERP = US ERP + default spread * relative equity market volatility (or PRS-based ERP).
**Formulas:** ERP_country = US ERP + Default Spread * Relative Equity Market Volatility; Relative Equity Market Volatility = sigma(emerging market equity index) / sigma(emerging market bond index) = 1.18 (Jan 2020).
**Rules:** Sovereign rating AAA → ERP_country = US ERP. Rating below AAA → add scaled default spread. No rating → use PRS score.
**Compute:** MIXED — the arithmetic is deterministic; selecting spread source and treating PRS-only countries is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** equity-risk-premium, country-risk, estimation-procedure, moodys, prs

## p.132 — Estimating beta: the regression approach

**Teaches:** The standard procedure for estimating beta regresses the stock's returns on the market's returns. The slope of the regression is the beta and measures the stock's riskiness relative to the market. The regression R-squared estimates the proportion of the firm's total risk (variance) attributable to market risk; the remainder (1 - R^2) is firm-specific risk.
**Formulas:** R_j = a + b * R_m, where R_j = stock returns, R_m = market returns, a = intercept, b = slope = beta. Market-risk share of variance = R^2; firm-specific share = 1 - R^2.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — return series → regression slope (beta), intercept, R^2.
**Tags:** beta, regression, market-risk, r-squared

## p.133 — Estimating performance from the regression intercept (Jensen's alpha)

**Teaches:** The regression intercept measures the stock's performance during the regression period relative to the CAPM. Rewriting the CAPM as R_j = R_f(1-b) + b*R_m and comparing to the regression R_j = a + b*R_m, the benchmark for the intercept is R_f(1-b). Jensen's alpha is the difference between the intercept and R_f(1-b); positive alpha means the stock beat CAPM expectations over the period.
**Formulas:** CAPM: R_j = R_f + b(R_m - R_f) = R_f(1-b) + b*R_m; Regression: R_j = a + b*R_m; Jensen's alpha = a - R_f(1-b), where R_f = riskfree rate over the period (matching the return interval), b = beta.
**Rules:** a > R_f(1-b) → stock did better than expected during the period; a = R_f(1-b) → as expected; a < R_f(1-b) → worse than expected.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — intercept, beta, riskfree rate → Jensen's alpha and performance verdict.
**Tags:** jensens-alpha, capm, regression-intercept, performance

## p.134 — Setting up the beta regression: period, interval, returns, index

**Teaches:** Four choices before running a beta regression. (1) Estimation period: services use 2–5 years; longer gives more data but firms change; shorter is more affected by one-off firm-specific events. (2) Return interval (daily/weekly/monthly): shorter intervals give more observations but more noise — non-trading biases all betas toward one. (3) Compute stock returns including dividends, counting dividends only in the ex-dividend month. (4) Choose a market index and compute its returns (with dividends) over the same intervals.
**Formulas:** Return = (Price_End - Price_Beginning + Dividends_Period) / Price_Beginning.
**Rules:** Longer period → more data but stale firm profile. Shorter interval → more observations but non-trading noise biasing beta toward 1. Include dividends only in ex-dividend months.
**Compute:** MIXED — return computation is deterministic; period/interval/index choices are JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** beta, regression-setup, return-interval, estimation-period

## p.158 — Determinant 3 of beta: financial leverage (levered beta formula)

**Teaches:** Borrowing creates fixed interest payments that make earnings to equity investors more volatile, which raises the equity beta. The equity (levered) beta is a function of the unlevered (asset) beta and the market debt-to-equity ratio, with a tax adjustment. A regression beta (like Disney's) is a LEVERED beta, because it is estimated from stock prices, which reflect the firm's leverage.
**Formulas:** Beta_L = Beta_u * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)), where Beta_L = levered or equity beta, Beta_u = unlevered or asset beta, D/E = market value debt-to-equity ratio, t = marginal tax rate.
**Rules:** Regression betas are levered betas. Higher D/E → higher equity beta.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — unlevered beta, tax rate, D/E → levered beta (and inverse).
