## p.321 — III. Project Options: intro

**Teaches:** A key limitation of traditional (static) investment analysis such as NPV is that it fails to capture the options embedded in investments. Three such options exist. First, the option to delay taking a project until a later date, when the firm has exclusive rights to it. Second, the option to expand: taking one project may let the firm take other projects in the future. Third, the option to abandon a project if its cash flows do not measure up. These options all add value. They can turn a project that looks "bad" under traditional analysis into a good one.
**Rules:** If a project has significant embedded options (delay, expand, abandon), a negative traditional NPV alone is not sufficient grounds for rejection — the option value must be added.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual framework; identifying and valuing embedded options requires reasoning).
**Tags:** real-options, project-options, npv-limitations, capital-budgeting

## p.322 — The Option to Delay

**Teaches:** When a firm has exclusive rights to a project or product for a specific period, it can delay taking it until a later date. Traditional investment analysis only asks whether the project is "good" if taken today. Yet the rights to a currently "bad" (negative-NPV) project can still have value. The PV of expected cash flows may rise above the required initial investment before the rights expire. The slide's payoff diagram is that of a call option. The x-axis is the present value of expected cash flows on the product. The payoff line kinks upward at the strike, which equals the initial investment in the project. NPV is positive to the right of the strike, where PV of cash flows exceeds the initial investment.
**Rules:** The option to delay has the payoff structure of a call option on the project, with exercise price = initial investment and underlying = PV of expected cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual; actual option valuation would require estimated inputs).
**Tags:** option-to-delay, real-options, call-option, exclusive-rights

## p.323 — Insights for Investment Analyses (option to delay)

**Teaches:** Three insights follow from viewing exclusive rights as an option. First, exclusive rights to a product or project are valuable even if the product or project is not viable today. Second, the value of these rights rises with the volatility of the underlying business; as with any option, higher volatility raises option value. Third, the rights cost money to acquire — by buying them or by spending on development such as R&D — and that cost has to be weighed against these benefits.
**Rules:** Higher volatility of the underlying business → higher value of the rights/option. Acquire the rights only if their option value exceeds the acquisition/development cost.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-to-delay, volatility, r-and-d, real-options

## p.324 — The Option to Expand / Take Other Projects

**Teaches:** Taking a project today may allow a firm to consider and take other valuable projects in the future. Thus, even a project with a negative NPV may be worth taking if the option it provides the firm (to take other projects later) has more-than-compensating value. The payoff diagram is again a call option: x-axis = cash flows on expansion; strike = additional investment to expand. The firm will not expand while the PV of expansion cash flows sits below the additional investment. Expansion becomes attractive to the right of that point, where the PV of cash flows from expansion exceeds the additional investment.
**Rules:** Accept a negative-NPV project if (Option value of future expansion opportunities) + NPV > 0.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-to-expand, real-options, strategic-investment, negative-npv

## p.325 — The Option to Abandon

**Teaches:** A firm may have the option to abandon a project if cash flows do not measure up to expectations. If abandoning allows the firm to save itself from further losses, this option makes the project more valuable. The payoff diagram is that of a put option. The x-axis is the present value of expected cash flows on the project. The payoff declines as PV of cash flows rises, then flattens at a floor beyond the abandonment point. The kink occurs at the cost of abandonment. The abandonment option limits downside losses.
**Rules:** The option to abandon has the payoff structure of a put option on the project; it adds value by truncating downside outcomes.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** option-to-abandon, real-options, put-option, downside-protection

## p.326 — IV. Assessing Existing or Past Investments

**Teaches:** The techniques and principles of investment analysis apply just as strongly to existing investments as to new ones. With existing investments the analyst can address one of two questions. Post-mortem: look back at existing investments and see whether they created value for the firm. What next: use investment-analysis tools to decide whether to keep, expand, or abandon existing investments.

## p.327 — Analyzing an Existing Investment (timeline diagram)

**Teaches:** Diagram (Figure 6.13: Analysis of Existing Project) of a project analyzed mid-life at the end of period 2 of a 10-period project. Cash-flow estimates come from two vintages. The initial analysis gave forecasts F0, F1, F2, ..., F10. At the analysis date (end of period 2), periods 0-2 are sunk, with actual cash flows A0 and A1 plus the current flow. For the remaining periods, new forecasts NF0 ... NF8 replace the original F3 ... F10. Two uses: in a post-mortem you compare actual cash flows against the original forecasts; alternatively you reassess expected cash flows based on what you have learned and decide whether to expand, continue, or divest (abandon) the investment.
**Formulas:** Notation definitions: F_n = forecast of cash flows in period n in the initial analysis; A_n = actual cash flow in period n; NF_n = new forecast of cash flows in period n made at the end of period 2. Past cash flows are sunk; only future (new-forecast) cash flows matter for the go-forward decision.
**Rules:** Sunk (past) cash flows are irrelevant to the keep/expand/divest decision; that decision uses only the new forecasts of future cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (forecast revision requires estimation; the comparison of actuals vs. forecasts is mechanical once numbers exist).

## p.328 — a. Post Mortem Analysis

**Teaches:** Actual cash flows from an investment can differ from original forecasts for reasons that fall into two groups. Chance: risk means actual outcomes differ from expectations; even best-information forecasts will invariably be wrong in hindsight because of unexpected shifts in macro variables (inflation, interest rates, economic growth) and micro variables (competitors, company). Bias: if original forecasts were biased, actuals will differ from expectations; evidence on capital budgeting shows managers tend to be over-optimistic about cash flows, and the bias is worse with over-confident managers. On an individual project you cannot tell whether chance or bias is to blame. Across projects and across time you can. If chance is the culprit, errors should be symmetric: actuals are about as likely to beat forecasts as to fall short. If bias is the reason, errors will tend to run in one direction.
**Rules:** Diagnostic: symmetric forecast errors across projects/time → chance; one-directional errors → bias (typically managerial over-optimism).
**Compute:** MIXED — computing the distribution of forecast errors across projects is deterministic given actuals and forecasts; attributing cause (chance vs. bias) is judgment.
**Tags:** post-mortem, forecast-error, managerial-bias, overconfidence

## p.329 — b. What should we do next? (liquidate / terminate / divest / continue)

**Teaches:** Decision rules for an existing project, based on the present value of newly forecast future cash flows discounted at rate r, compared against zero, salvage value, and divestiture value.
**Formulas:** PV of future cash flows = sum from t=0 to n of NF_t / (1+r)^t. Here NF_t = new forecast of cash flow in period t, counted from the analysis date forward; r = discount rate appropriate to the project; n = remaining project life. (The slide writes the summand as NF_n/(1+r)^n under a sum over t.)
**Rules:**
- If sum of NF_t/(1+r)^t < 0 → Liquidate the project.
- If sum of NF_t/(1+r)^t < Salvage Value → Terminate the project.
- If sum of NF_t/(1+r)^t < Divestiture Value → Divest the project.
- If sum of NF_t/(1+r)^t > 0 and > Divestiture Value → Continue the project.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given new forecast cash flows NF_t, discount rate r, salvage value, and divestiture value, a script outputs the PV and the liquidate/terminate/divest/continue decision. (Producing the forecasts themselves is judgment.)
**Tags:** divestiture, liquidation, salvage-value, decision-rules, existing-investments

## p.330 — Example: Disney California Adventure — the 2008 judgment call

**Teaches:** Setup of a real keep/abandon/expand decision. Disney opened Disney California Adventure (DCA) in 2001 at a cost of $1.5 billion, expecting about 60% of Disneyland visitors to cross over and about $100 million in annual after-tax cash flows. By 2008 DCA had underperformed: of 15 million Disneyland visitors in 2007 only 6 million visited DCA, and cash flow averaged only $50 million per year over 2001-2007. In early 2008 Disney faced three choices. Option 1: shut down DCA and recover an estimated $500 million of its initial investment. Option 2: continue the status quo, recognizing future cash flows will be closer to the actual $50 million than to original projections. Option 3: invest about $600 million to expand and modify the park, adding attractions for families with children. Expansion was expected to raise the share of Disneyland visitors coming to DCA from 40% to 60%. It would also increase annual after-tax cash flow by 60%, from $50 million to $80 million.
**Example:** Company: Disney (DCA theme park). Inputs: initial cost $1.5B (2001, sunk); expected CF $100M/yr vs. actual average $50M/yr (2001-2007); abandonment recovery $500M; expansion cost $600M raising annual CF from $50M to $80M. The evaluation is on the next page.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (this page states inputs and alternatives; the valuation follows on p.331).
**Tags:** disney, worked-example, abandonment, expansion, existing-investments

## p.331 — DCA: Evaluating the alternatives

**Teaches:** Values Disney's three 2008 choices for DCA using growing perpetuities. Continuing operation: assume the current $50 million after-tax cash flow continues in perpetuity, growing at the 2% inflation rate, discounted at Disney's 2008 theme-park cost of capital of 6.62% → value of status quo ≈ $1.103 billion. Abandonment: abandoning now recovers only $500 million of the original investment, so abandonment value = $500 million. Expansion: the up-front $600 million cost raises annual cash flow from $50 to $80 million; the incremental $30 million growing perpetuity is worth about $662 million, which exceeds the $600 million cost. Conclusion implied: continuing beats abandoning ($1.103B > $500M), and expanding adds value ($662M incremental value > $600M cost).
**Formulas:** Value of DCA (status quo) = Expected cash flow next year / (Cost of capital − g) = 50(1.02) / (0.0662 − 0.02) = $1.103 billion. Here g = perpetual growth rate (2% inflation); cost of capital = 6.62% (theme-park cost of capital, 2008). Value of CF from expansion = Increase in CF next year / (Cost of capital − g) = 30(1.02) / (0.0662 − 0.02) = $662 million.
**Example:** Disney DCA, 2008. Status quo: 50 × 1.02 / (0.0662 − 0.02) = 51/0.0462 ≈ $1,103M. Abandonment value = $500M (< $1,103M, so do not abandon). Expansion: incremental CF $30M/yr → 30 × 1.02 / 0.0462 ≈ $662M vs. cost $600M → expansion adds ≈ $62M of value.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given current CF, growth rate, cost of capital, abandonment value, expansion cost, and incremental CF, a script computes the three alternative values and picks the best. (Choosing the input estimates is judgment.)
**Tags:** disney, growing-perpetuity, abandonment-value, expansion, worked-example

## p.332 — First Principles (corporate finance map)

**Teaches:** Section-closing recap of the corporate-finance first-principles map. Objective: maximize the value of the business (firm). Three decisions hang off it. The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate — the hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it; the return should reflect the magnitude and timing of the cash flows as well as all side effects. The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt for your firm and the right mix of debt and equity to fund operations — the optimal mix of debt and equity maximizes firm value, and the right kind of debt matches the tenor of your assets. The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments that make your minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to the owners — how much cash you can return depends on current and potential investment opportunities, and how you return it (dividends vs. buybacks) depends on what owners prefer.
**Rules:** Invest only when return > risk-adjusted hurdle rate; debt tenor should match asset tenor; return cash to owners when acceptable investments are unavailable.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework/recap page).
**Tags:** first-principles, investment-decision, financing-decision, dividend-decision
