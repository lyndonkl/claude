## p.3 — First Principles of Corporate Finance
**Teaches:** The objective of corporate finance is to maximize the value of the business (firm). This splits into three decisions. The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it. The return should reflect the magnitude and timing of the cashflows and all side effects. The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt and the right mix of debt and equity to fund operations; the optimal mix maximizes firm value, and the right kind of debt matches the tenor of your assets. The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments that make your minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to owners. How much cash you can return depends on current and potential investment opportunities; the form (dividends vs. buybacks) depends on what owners prefer.
**Rules:** Invest only if return > hurdle rate. Hurdle rate must reflect risk and financing mix. Match debt tenor to asset life. Return cash to owners when no investments clear the hurdle rate.

## p.4 — The Corporate Stakeholders
**Teaches:** A map of the stakeholders around corporate managers, who decide what to invest in, how much debt to take, and how much cash to return to shareholders. Shareholders invest in equity, own the company, and exercise control through the board of directors and annual meetings. Banks and bondholders lend to the company; debt covenants restrict corporate actions, with veto power over some. Employees help make the products, with wages and benefits set by the labor market. Customers pay for and receive the products; product-market competition and consumer-protection laws protect them. Competitors offer similar products and compete for market share. Society receives side benefits and bears side costs of corporate actions, governed by laws and societal norms on acceptable behavior.

## p.5 — The Objective in Decision Making
**Teaches:** In traditional corporate finance, the objective is to maximize the value of the firm. A narrower objective is to maximize stockholder wealth. When the stock is traded and markets are viewed as efficient, the objective becomes maximizing the stock price. A financial balance sheet frames the choice. Assets = Assets in Place (existing investments that generate cashflows today, including long-lived fixed assets and short-lived working capital) plus Growth Assets (expected value from future investments). Liabilities = Debt (fixed claim on cash flows, little or no role in management, fixed maturity, tax deductible) plus Equity (residual claim on cash flows, significant role in management, perpetual life). "Maximize firm value" targets the whole asset side; "maximize equity value" targets equity; "maximize market estimate of equity value" targets the stock price.
**Rules:** Use stock price as the objective only when the stock is traded and markets are efficient; otherwise fall back to equity value or firm value.

## p.6 — Why focus on maximizing stockholder wealth
**Teaches:** You can have only one objective — one interest group whose interests come first. Corporate finance picks shareholders because they hold the residual claim. Every other claimholder has a contractual claim that they can negotiate to protect their interests. If the company is traded, stock price becomes the metric because it is easily observable and constantly updated. If investors are rational, stock prices reflect the wisdom of decisions, short term and long term, right away.

## p.7 — The Strawman Version: Cutthroat Corporatism
**Teaches:** A strawman caricature of wealth maximization. A founder, family, or investor group owns a controlling stake and runs the company through voting rights and a compliant board. Lenders, controlled by or beholden to the founder, impose few or no constraints. The firm maximizes founder wealth, with other shareholders along for the ride. It uses market power to drive out competition and bargaining power to minimize wages and benefits, so employees get paid less to do more. It ignores or subverts laws designed to protect society, so society bears large side costs. It uses market dominance to drive up prices, so customers pay more. The "Darwinian End Game": winning companies dominate or monopolize their markets, exploiting customers, employees, and society while enriching their founders and shareholders.

## p.8 — Real Choices or False Ones?
**Teaches:** Maximizing stock price is not incompatible with meeting employee needs. Employees are often stockholders in many firms, and firms that maximize stock price are generally profitable firms that can afford to treat employees well. Maximizing stock price does not mean customers are unimportant; in most businesses, keeping customers happy is the route to stock price maximization. Maximizing stock price also does not imply that a company has to be a social outlaw.

## p.9 — The Classical Objective Function
**Teaches:** The classical model of how stock-price maximization works when every link holds. Stockholders hire and fire managers through the board and the annual meeting, so managers maximize stockholder wealth. Bondholders lend money, and their interests are protected. Managers reveal information honestly and on time. Financial markets are efficient and assess the effect of decisions on value. There are no social costs: all costs can be traced to the firm. Under these assumptions, maximizing stock price maximizes stockholder wealth without hurting anyone else.
**Rules:** The classical objective needs four assumptions: stockholders control managers; bondholders are protected; information is honest and markets are efficient; all social costs are traceable to the firm.

## p.10 — Utopian Corporatism
**Teaches:** The ideal version of shareholder capitalism. Shareholders own the company with equal voting rights. The board operates as a check on the CEO, and shareholders vote at annual meetings. Bondholders are fully protected, explicitly through covenants or implicitly by reputation concerns. The firm maximizes stock prices with efficient markets and full information. It plays to win by offering better products or lower prices, so the sector is winnowed to the best companies. Unions or a strong labor market even the game, so employees get fair wages. All costs created by the firm are traced and charged to it, so society faces no costs. Customers are treated well so they become repeat customers, and they get a good deal. The Utopian End Game: managers maximizing stock prices also maximize stockholder wealth, all other stakeholders get their rightful dues, and society and the economy are better off.

## p.11 — What can go wrong?
**Teaches:** The failure modes of each link in the classical objective function. Stockholders have little control over managers, so managers put their own interests first. Bondholders who lend money can get ripped off. Managers delay bad news or provide misleading information to financial markets. Markets make mistakes and can overreact. There are significant social costs, and some costs cannot be traced to the firm. Each broken link weakens stock-price maximization as the right objective.

## p.13 — The Annual Meeting as a disciplinary venue
**Teaches:** Three factors dilute stockholder power at annual meetings. Most small stockholders do not attend, because the cost of going exceeds the value of their holdings. Incumbent management starts with a clear advantage on proxies: proxies that are not voted become votes for incumbent management. Large stockholders who dislike managers take the path of least resistance and vote with their feet (sell). Annual meetings are also tightly scripted and controlled. That makes it hard for outsiders and rebels to raise issues management dislikes.

## p.15 — The CEO often hand-picks directors
**Teaches:** Three reasons boards fail as a check on CEOs. ## p.21 — When the cat is idle, the mice will play: entrenchment tactics
**Teaches:** When managers do not fear stockholders, they often put their own interests over stockholder interests. The slide lists five mechanisms, ordered from "no stockholder approval needed" to "stockholder approval needed." Greenmail: managers of a hostile-takeover target buy out the potential acquirer's existing stake at a price much greater than what the raider paid, in return for a signed "standstill" agreement. Golden parachutes: employment-contract provisions that pay a lump sum or cash flows over a period if the covered managers lose their jobs in a takeover. Poison pills: securities whose rights or cashflows are triggered by an outside event, generally a hostile takeover. Shark repellents: anti-takeover charter amendments that also aim to dissuade hostile takeovers, but differ on one important count — they require stockholder assent to be instituted. Overpaying on takeovers: acquisitions often driven by management interests rather than stockholder interests.
**Rules:** Greenmail, golden parachutes, and poison pills need no stockholder approval; shark repellents require stockholder approval.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** greenmail, golden-parachute, poison-pill, shark-repellent, takeover-defenses, agency-costs

## p.32 — II. Stockholders' objectives vs. Bondholders' objectives
**Teaches:** In theory, there is no conflict of interest between stockholders and bondholders. In practice, they have different objectives. Bondholders care most about safety and about ensuring they get paid their claims. Stockholders are more likely to think about upside potential. This asymmetry — fixed claim versus residual claim — creates room for stockholders to expropriate wealth from bondholders.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** stockholder-bondholder-conflict, agency-costs, fixed-claim, residual-claim

## p.33 — Examples of the stockholder-bondholder conflict
**Teaches:** Three ways stockholders can transfer wealth from lenders. A dividend/buyback surge: when firms pay cash out as dividends, lenders are hurt and stockholders may be helped, because the firm becomes riskier without the cash. Risk shifting: lenders set interest rates on their perception of how risky the firm's investments are; if stockholders then take on riskier investments than agreed at the outset, lenders are hurt. Borrowing more on the same assets: if lenders do not protect themselves, the firm can borrow more money and make all existing lenders worse off.
**Rules:** The three expropriation channels: cash payouts, risk shifting, and claim dilution through new borrowing.

## p.35 — III. Firms and Financial Markets
**Teaches:** In theory, financial markets are efficient. Managers convey information honestly and in a timely manner, and markets make reasoned judgments about what that information means for "true value." Consequences of the assumption: a company that invests in good long-term projects will be rewarded; short-term accounting gimmicks will not increase market value; and stock price performance is a good measure of company performance. In practice, there are holes in the "Efficient Markets" assumption.

## p.38 — Some critiques of market efficiency
**Teaches:** The base critique is investor irrationality. Prices often move for no reason at all. Prices are far more volatile than the fundamentals justify. Earnings and dividends are much less volatile than stock prices. Three manifestations of irrationality follow. Reaction to news: some believe investors overreact to news, both good and bad; others believe investors sometimes underreact to big news stories. An insider conspiracy: financial markets are manipulated by insiders, and prices have no relationship to value. Short-termism: investors are short-sighted and ignore the long-term implications of firm actions.

## p.40 — Counter-evidence that markets are not short term
**Teaches:** Three pieces of counter (albeit not conclusive) evidence against market short-termism. Value of young firms: hundreds of start-ups and small firms with no earnings expected in the near future raise money on financial markets; a myopic market that cared only about short-term earnings would not attach high prices to them. Current earnings vs. future growth: if anything, markets do not value current earnings and cashflows enough. They value future earnings and cashflows too much. Studies suggest low PE stocks are underpriced relative to high PE stocks. Market reaction to investments: the market response to research and development and investment expenditures is generally positive.
