## p.161 — Betas are weighted averages

**Teaches:** The beta of any portfolio is always the market-value weighted average of the betas of the individual investments in it. Two applications: the beta of a mutual fund is the weighted average of the betas of its holdings, and the beta of a firm after a merger is the market-value weighted average of the betas of the merging companies.
**Formulas:** Beta_portfolio = sum over i of (w_i * Beta_i), where w_i = market value of investment i / total market value of the portfolio, and Beta_i = beta of investment i.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (individual betas + market-value weights → portfolio/merged beta).
**Tags:** beta, portfolio-beta, mergers, weighted-average

## p.163 — Disney/Cap Cities beta estimation: Step 1 (unlever and combine)

**Teaches:** Step 1 of estimating the merged firm's beta: unlever each firm's equity beta using its own D/E ratio, then take the firm-value-weighted average of the unlevered betas. Firm values (not just equity values) are used as weights because unlevered betas reflect the risk of the entire business, not just the equity.
**Formulas:** Unlevered beta = Levered beta / (1 + (1 - tax rate) * D/E); here (1 - t) = 0.64, so tax rate = 36%. Combined unlevered beta = Beta_u,Disney * (FirmValue_Disney / Total) + Beta_u,CapCities * (FirmValue_CapCities / Total).
**Example:** Disney unlevered beta = 1.15/(1 + 0.64*0.10) = 1.08. Cap Cities unlevered beta = 0.95/(1 + 0.64*0.03) = 0.93. Combined unlevered beta = 1.08*(34286/53401) + 0.93*(19115/53401) = 1.026.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (levered betas, D/E ratios, tax rate, firm values → combined unlevered beta).
**Tags:** beta, unlevered-beta, mergers, disney

## p.164 — Disney/Cap Cities beta estimation: Step 2 (relever under financing mix)

**Teaches:** Step 2: relever the combined unlevered beta using the D/E ratio implied by how the merger is financed. The financing choice changes the post-merger equity beta: an all-equity deal leaves the beta near the unlevered level, while borrowing to fund the acquisition raises leverage and thus the levered beta.
**Formulas:** New levered beta = Combined unlevered beta * (1 + (1 - tax rate) * new D/E ratio), with (1 - t) = 0.64.
**Example:** (a) All-equity purchase (Disney assumes Cap Cities debt, issues $18.5B equity): Debt = 3,186 + 615 = $3,801m; Equity = 31,100 + 18,500 = $49,600m; D/E = 3,801/49,600 = 7.66%; New beta = 1.026*(1 + 0.64*0.0766) = 1.08. (b) Actual deal — Disney borrowed $10B, funded the remaining $8.5B of the $18.5B equity price with new equity, and assumed Cap Cities debt: Debt = 3,186 + 615 + 10,000 = $13,801m; Equity = 31,100 + 8,500 = $39,600m; D/E = 13,801/39,600 = 34.82%; New beta = 1.026*(1 + 0.64*0.3482) = 1.25.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (unlevered beta, financing mix, tax rate → post-merger levered beta).
**Tags:** beta, levered-beta, mergers, acquisition-financing

## p.166 — Bottom-up versus top-down beta

**Teaches:** A top-down beta comes from a regression of the firm's stock returns on the market. A bottom-up beta is built from comparables: (1) identify the businesses the firm operates in; (2) find unlevered betas of other firms in those businesses; (3) take a weighted average (by sales or operating income) of those unlevered betas; (4) lever up using the firm's own debt/equity ratio. The bottom-up beta is a better estimate because its standard error is much lower (averaging across many firms) and because it can reflect the firm's current or even expected future business mix rather than the historical mix embedded in a regression.
**Rules:** Prefer bottom-up beta over regression (top-down) beta; weight business betas by sales or operating income; relever at the firm's own D/E.
**Compute:** MIXED — the averaging and levering are deterministic; choosing businesses and comparable firms is judgment.
**Tags:** bottom-up-beta, beta-estimation, comparable-firms

## p.170 — Backing into a pure play beta: Studio Entertainment

**Teaches:** Worked mechanics of converting a median regression (equity) beta into a pure-play business beta: unlever using the gross D/E ratio, then strip out the cash effect using cash/firm value, treating the company as a portfolio of the operating (movie) business and a zero-beta cash business. An alternative shortcut uses the net debt-to-equity ratio, which gives a slightly different answer.
**Formulas:** (1) Unlevered beta = Levered beta / (1 + (1 - t)(D/E)). (2) Portfolio identity: (Cash/Value)*(0) + (1 - Cash/Value)*(Beta of operating business) = Company unlevered beta, so Beta of operating business = Company unlevered beta / (1 - Cash/Value). (3) Net D/E ratio = (Debt - Cash)/Equity; Unlevered beta (net-debt method) = Levered beta / (1 + (1 - t)(Net D/E)).
**Example:** Median regression equity beta = 1.24; gross D/E = 21.30/78.70 = 27.06%; unlevered beta = 1.24/(1 + (1-0.4)(0.2706)) = 1.0668. Cash/value = 2.96%: (0.0296)(0) + (1-0.0296)(Beta_movies) = 1.0668 → Beta_movies = 1.0668/(1-0.0296) = 1.0993. Alternative: Net D/E = (21.30-2.96)/78.70 = 23.30%; unlevered beta for movies = 1.24/(1 + (1-0.4)(0.233)) = 1.0879. Value decomposition table: Movie Business value 97.04 (beta 1.0993), Cash Business 2.96 (beta 0), Movie Company 100 (beta 1.0668); Debt 21.3 (beta 0), Equity 78.7 (beta 1.24).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (equity beta, D/E, tax rate, cash/value → pure-play business beta).
**Tags:** pure-play-beta, unlevered-beta, cash-adjustment, studio-entertainment

## p.175 — Vale: converting cost of equity across currencies (US$ to nominal R$)

**Teaches:** A discount rate estimated in one currency can be converted to another using only the expected inflation rates in the two currencies — currency differences in discount rates are purely differential-inflation effects. Vale's 11.23% US$ cost of equity converts to about 18.87% in nominal Brazilian reais using 9% Brazilian vs 2% US inflation. The cross-check — building the R$ cost of equity directly from the R$ riskfree rate — gives a nearly identical answer.
**Formulas:** Cost of Equity_Nominal R$ = (1 + Cost of Equity_US$) * (1 + Expected Inflation_R$)/(1 + Expected Inflation_US$) - 1. Alternative: Cost of Equity in R$ = R$ riskfree rate + Beta * ERP.
**Example:** (1.1123)*(1.09/1.02) - 1 = 18.87%. Alternatively: 10.18% (R$ riskfree) + 1.15*(7.38%) = 18.67%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cost of equity in one currency + two expected inflation rates → cost of equity in the other currency); inflation expectations themselves are JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** currency-conversion, cost-of-equity, inflation-differential, vale

## p.180 — Estimating Bookscape's levered beta and cost of equity

## p.181 — Is beta an adequate measure of risk for a private firm?

**Teaches:** Concept-check question: beta measures only the risk added to a diversified portfolio, but owners of most private firms are not diversified. Therefore using beta to get a private firm's cost of equity will (a) underestimate, (b) overestimate, or (c) could under- or over-estimate it. The implied answer is (a) underestimate: an undiversified owner bears total risk, not just market risk, so the beta-based cost of equity is too low.
**Rules:** For undiversified private-firm owners, a market-beta-based cost of equity understates the required return.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** private-firms, total-risk, diversification, cost-of-equity

## p.182 — Total beta: total risk versus market risk

**Teaches:** For undiversified owners, adjust the market beta upward to a "total beta" that reflects total risk rather than just market risk. Since the R-squared of the beta regression measures the proportion of risk that is market risk, dividing the market beta by the correlation with the market (the square root of R-squared) scales it to total risk. For Bookscape, this raises the beta from 0.8558 to 1.6783 and the cost of equity from 7.46% to 11.98%.
**Formulas:** Total Beta = Market Beta / Correlation of the sector with the market = Market Beta / sqrt(R squared). Total Cost of Equity = Riskfree rate + Total Beta * ERP.
**Example:** Bookscape: market beta 0.8558; median R² of comparable public firms = 26.00%, so correlation = sqrt(0.26) = 0.5099. Total beta = 0.8558/0.5099 = 1.6783. Total cost of equity = 2.75% + 1.6783*(5.5%) = 11.98%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (market beta, R² → total beta and total cost of equity).
**Tags:** total-beta, private-firms, r-squared, cost-of-equity

## p.185 — What is debt?

**Teaches:** Defines debt by three characteristics: (1) a commitment to make fixed payments in the future; (2) the fixed payments are tax deductible; (3) failure to make the payments can lead to default or loss of control of the firm to the party owed. Consequently, debt for cost-of-capital purposes should include any interest-bearing liability, short-term or long-term, and any lease obligation, whether operating or capital.
**Rules:** Include in debt: all interest-bearing liabilities (short and long term) and all lease obligations (operating and capital). Non-interest-bearing liabilities (e.g., accounts payable) are not debt.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification), following stated criteria.
**Tags:** debt-definition, leases, capital-structure

## p.186 — Estimating the cost of debt: the decision tree

**Teaches:** How to estimate a firm's cost of debt: if the firm has traded bonds, use the yield to maturity on a long-term straight (no special features) bond. If the firm is rated, use the rating and a typical default spread for that rating. If the firm is not rated: use the interest rate on a recent long-term bank borrowing, or estimate a synthetic rating and use it to get a default spread and cost of debt. The cost of debt must be in the same currency as the cost of equity and the cash flows in the valuation.
**Rules:** Traded bonds → YTM on long-term straight bond; rated firm → rating + default spread; unrated firm → recent bank borrowing rate or synthetic rating. Currency consistency is required across cost of debt, cost of equity, and cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of route), then deterministic within each route.

## p.194 — Costs of hybrids: preferred stock and convertible debt

**Teaches:** Preferred stock shares features of debt (pre-specified dividend, paid before common dividends) and of equity (dividends not tax deductible); if viewed as perpetual, its cost is the preferred dividend yield. Convertible debt is part debt (the bond) and part equity (the conversion option); the best treatment is to break it into its debt and equity components and eliminate the convertible as a separate category.
**Formulas:** k_ps = Preferred Dividend per share / Market Price per preferred share, where k_ps = cost of preferred stock.
**Rules:** Treat perpetual preferred at its dividend yield; decompose convertibles into debt + equity rather than treating them as a third category.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC for preferred (dividend, price → cost); MIXED for convertibles (option-value split requires estimation).

## p.195 — Weights for cost of capital calculation

**Teaches:** The weights in the cost of capital must be market values. Three specious arguments for book value are rebutted: (1) "book value is more reliable because less volatile" — its stability is a weakness (it fails to update), not a strength; (2) "book value is more conservative" — for most companies book-value weights actually yield a LOWER cost of capital (more debt weight), which is anti-conservative; (3) "consistency with accounting returns computed on book value" — apparent consistency, but it makes no economic sense.
**Rules:** Always use market-value weights for debt and equity in cost of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (methodological principle).
**Tags:** cost-of-capital, market-value-weights, book-value

## p.196 — Disney: from book value to market value for interest-bearing debt

**Teaches:** Market value of debt can be estimated by treating the entire book debt as one coupon bond: annual coupon = total interest expense, face value = book value of debt, maturity = weighted-average maturity of the debt (from footnoted maturity schedule), discounted at the current pre-tax cost of debt. Disney's maturity schedule gives a weighted average maturity of 7.92 years; the footnoted amounts ($12,139m) do not add to book debt ($14,288m) because Disney does not break down the maturity of all its debt.
**Formulas:** Estimated MV of debt = Interest expense * [ (1 - 1/(1+r)^M) / r ] + Book debt / (1+r)^M, where r = pre-tax cost of debt and M = weighted-average maturity. For Disney: 349*[(1 - 1/1.0375^7.92)/0.0375] + 14,288/1.0375^7.92 = $13,028 million.
**Example:** Disney 2013: book debt $14,288m, interest expense $349m, pre-tax cost of debt 3.75%, weighted average maturity 7.92 years → market value of debt = $13,028 million.
**Data:** Disney debt maturity schedule ($ millions): due 0.5yr $1,452 (11.96%, wt*mat 0.06); 2yr $1,300 (10.71%, 0.21); 3yr $1,500 (12.36%, 0.37); 4yr $2,650 (21.83%, 0.87); 6yr $500 (4.12%, 0.25); 8yr $1,362 (11.22%, 0.90); 9yr $1,400 (11.53%, 1.04); 19yr $500 (4.12%, 0.78); 26yr $25 (0.21%, 0.05); 28yr $950 (7.83%, 2.19); 29yr $500 (4.12%, 1.19); total $12,139, weighted average maturity 7.92 years.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (book debt, interest expense, cost of debt, maturity schedule → market value of debt).

## p.197 — Operating leases at Disney

**Teaches:** The "debt value" of operating leases is the present value of future lease commitments discounted at a rate reflecting their risk, usually the pre-tax cost of debt (3.75% for Disney). Disney's $1,784 million of commitments beyond year 5 is annuitized as 5 years at $356.8 million each (based on the average commitment over the first 5 years).
