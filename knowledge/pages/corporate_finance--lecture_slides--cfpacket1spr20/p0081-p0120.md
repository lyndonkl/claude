## p.81 — The Capital Asset Pricing Model (overview)

**Teaches:** The CAPM in five statements. (1) It uses the variance of actual returns around an expected return as its measure of risk. (2) A portion of variance can be diversified away; only the non-diversifiable portion is rewarded. (3) It measures non-diversifiable risk with beta, which is standardized around one. (4) It translates beta into an expected return. (5) Damodaran's pragmatic defense: it works as well as the next best alternative in most cases.
**Formulas:** Expected Return = Riskfree Rate + Beta * Risk Premium, where Beta = the asset's standardized non-diversifiable (market) risk and Risk Premium = the premium demanded for the average-risk (market) investment over the riskfree rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (riskfree rate, beta, risk premium → expected return); estimating the inputs is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** capm, risk-models, beta, expected-return

## p.84 — Do you live in a mean-variance world? (thought experiment)

**Teaches:** A concept-check question probing whether investors truly care only about mean and variance. Two investments have the same expected return (15%) and the same standard deviation (25%), but investment A carries a very small chance of quadrupling your money while investment B's best possible payoff is a 60% return. A strict mean-variance investor is indifferent; a preference for A reveals a taste for positive skewness (upside potential), a preference for B reveals a taste for safety. The follow-up flips the framing to downside: A's worst case is losing 100%, B's worst case is -50% — if that changes your answer, you care about skewness/downside risk, not just variance, and you do not fully live in a mean-variance world.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (behavioral/conceptual question).

## p.85 — The importance of diversification: risk types

**Teaches:** Risk lies on a continuum from firm-specific (affects only one firm) to market-wide (affects all investments). The breakdown (Figure 3.5): projects may do better or worse than expected (purely firm-specific); competition may be stronger or weaker than anticipated (affects the firm and a few rivals); an entire sector may be affected by an action; exchange-rate and political risk affect many firms; interest rates, inflation and news about the economy affect all investments (market risk). Firms can reduce the firm-specific end by investing in lots of projects, acquiring competitors, diversifying across sectors, and diversifying across countries — but cannot affect market risk. Investors can mitigate by diversifying across domestic stocks, diversifying globally, and diversifying across asset classes.
**Rules:** Classification: risk that affects only one firm = firm-specific (diversifiable); risk that affects all investments = market risk (non-diversifiable); risks in between (competitive, sector, international) are partially diversifiable and diversify away as the portfolio broadens.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of risk types).
**Tags:** diversification, firm-specific-risk, market-risk, risk-types

## p.86 — Why diversification reduces/eliminates firm-specific risk

**Teaches:** Firm-specific risk can be reduced, if not eliminated, by increasing the number of investments in the portfolio (being diversified); market-wide risk cannot. Two economic reasons. (a) Each investment becomes a much smaller percentage of the portfolio, muting the effect (positive or negative) of any one firm's fortunes on the whole. (b) Firm-specific events can be good or bad; in a large portfolio these effects average out to zero. For every firm where something bad happens, there will be some other firm where something good happens.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual argument).
**Tags:** diversification, firm-specific-risk, portfolio-theory

## p.87 — The role of the marginal investor

**Teaches:** The marginal investor in a firm is the investor most likely to be the buyer or seller on the next trade and thereby to influence the stock price. The marginal investor must both own a lot of the stock and trade it regularly. Because trading is required, the largest investor may not be the marginal investor — especially a founder/manager who holds but rarely trades (examples: Larry Ellison at Oracle, Mark Zuckerberg at Facebook). Key assumption: in all risk and return models in finance, the marginal investor is assumed to be well diversified — which is why only non-diversifiable risk is priced.
**Rules:** Marginal investor = large holder + regular trader; a non-trading founder/insider is not the marginal investor even if the largest holder.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** marginal-investor, diversification, risk-pricing

## p.91 — The limiting case: the market portfolio

**Teaches:** If diversification costs nothing (no transactions costs) and all assets can be traded, the limit of diversification is to hold a portfolio of every single asset in the economy, in proportion to market value — the market portfolio. Consequence: individual investors adjust for risk not by picking different stocks but by changing their allocation between this one market portfolio and a riskless asset (such as a T-Bill).

## p.92 — The risk and expected return of an individual asset

**Teaches:** The essence: the risk of any asset is the risk it adds to the market portfolio; statistically this is measured by how much the asset moves with the market (the covariance). The measure: beta is a standardized version of this covariance, obtained by dividing the covariance of the asset with the market by the variance of the market; it captures the asset's non-diversifiable risk. The result: the required return on an investment is a linear function of its beta.
**Formulas:** Beta = Covariance(asset returns, market returns) / Variance(market returns). Expected Return = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Expected Return on the Market Portfolio - Riskfree Rate), where the bracketed term is the market (equity) risk premium.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (return series → covariance, variance → beta; riskfree rate, beta, market premium → expected return).
**Tags:** beta, covariance, capm, expected-return

## p.93 — Limitations of the CAPM

**Teaches:** Three lines of criticism. (1) The model makes unrealistic assumptions (no transactions costs, no private information). (2) Its parameters cannot be estimated precisely: the market index used can be wrong, and the firm may have changed during the estimation period. (3) The model does not work well empirically. If it were right, returns and betas should be linearly related and beta should be the only variable explaining returns. In reality the beta-return relationship is weak, and other variables (size, price/book value) seem to explain differences in returns better.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** capm, model-limitations, empirical-evidence, beta

## p.94 — Alternatives to the CAPM

**Teaches:** A three-step map of all risk-and-return models. Step 1 (defining risk): risk is measured by variance in actual returns around an expected return (riskless = no spread; low vs. high risk = narrow vs. wide distributions). Step 2 (differentiating risk): firm-specific risk can be diversified away (each investment is a small proportion of the portfolio; risk averages out), market risk cannot; since the marginal investor is assumed diversified, only market risk is rewarded and priced. Step 3 (measuring market risk) is where the models differ. CAPM: assumes no private information and no transactions costs, so everyone holds the market portfolio. Market risk = risk added by any investment to the market portfolio, measured by the asset's beta relative to the market portfolio (from a regression). APM: if there are no arbitrage opportunities, market risk is captured by betas relative to unspecified market factors (from a factor analysis). Multi-Factor Models: market risk must come from macroeconomic factors. Market risk = risk exposures of any asset to macro factors, via betas relative to specified macroeconomic factors (from a regression). Proxy Models: in an efficient market, long-period return differences must be due to market risk differences. Variables correlated with returns then proxy for that risk; market risk = captured by the proxy variables, via an equation relating returns to proxy variables (from a regression).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (model-selection framework).
**Tags:** capm, apm, multi-factor-models, proxy-models, risk-models

## p.95 — Why the CAPM persists

**Teaches:** Despite its critics and limitations, the CAPM has survived as the default risk model for equity valuation and corporate finance. Alternatives (APM, multifactor models) have made inroads in performance evaluation but not in prospective analysis. Reasons: (a) the richer models explain past returns better, but their effectiveness drops off in estimating expected future returns, since the fitted factors and loadings shift over time; (b) they are more complicated and require more information; (c) for most companies, the expected returns they produce are not different enough from the CAPM's to be worth estimating several additional betas.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** capm, model-choice, apm, practical-finance

## p.99 — The riskfree rate and time horizon

**Teaches:** On a riskfree asset the actual return equals the expected return, so there is no variance around the expected return. For an investment to be riskfree two conditions must be met: (1) no default risk — which generally means a government issuer, though not all governments are default-free; (2) no reinvestment-rate uncertainty — which implies a zero-coupon security with the same maturity as the cash flow being analyzed.
**Rules:** Riskfree asset criteria: government-issued (default-free) AND zero coupon with maturity matched to the cash flow's horizon.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (definitional criteria).
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, default-risk, reinvestment-risk, time-horizon

## p.100 — Riskfree rate in practice

**Teaches:** Definition: the riskfree rate is the rate on a zero-coupon default-free bond matching the time horizon of the cash flow being analyzed. Implication: theoretically this means a different riskfree rate for each cash flow — the 1-year zero rate for the year-1 cash flow, the 2-year zero rate for year 2, and so on. Practical solution: when there is substantial uncertainty about expected cash flows, the present-value effect of using time-varying riskfree rates is small enough that it may not be worth it. Bottom line: in corporate finance almost everything is long term, so using a single long-term default-free rate as the riskfree rate makes sense.
**Rules:** Use one long-term default-free government rate for long-term corporate finance analysis rather than matching a separate zero rate to each cash flow.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of rate); applying a chosen rate is deterministic.
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, practical-finance, duration-matching

## p.101 — The bottom line on riskfree rates

**Teaches:** Currency matching: the riskfree rate must be in the same currency as the cash flows — US-dollar cash flows need a US-dollar riskfree rate; Euro cash flows need a Euro riskfree rate. Conventional practice is to use the government bond rate of the government controlling that currency; in November 2013 the 10-year US Treasury rate of 2.75% served as the US-dollar riskfree rate. If the government is default-free, using a long-term government rate (even on a coupon bond) for all cash flows in a long-term analysis yields a close approximation of the true value; for short-term analysis a short-term government security rate is entirely appropriate.
**Rules:** (1) Match riskfree-rate currency to cash-flow currency. (2) Default-free government → use its long-term bond rate for long-term analysis, short-term rate for short-term analysis.

## p.104 — What if there is no default-free entity? (November 2013)

**Teaches:** Three fixes when no default-free issuer exists in a currency. (1) Adjust the local-currency government borrowing rate for default risk: subtract the sovereign default spread implied by the government's local-currency rating. (2) Do the analysis in an alternate currency where the riskfree rate is easier — e.g., for Vale in 2013, analyze in US dollars (riskfree rate = US Treasury bond rate) rather than estimating a riskfree rate in Brazilian R$. (3) Do the analysis in real terms, using a real riskfree rate; the inflation-indexed treasury (TIPS) rate is a measure of a real riskfree rate.
**Formulas:** Riskfree rate in local currency = Local-currency government bond rate - Sovereign default spread (based on the government's local-currency rating).
**Example:** India, November 2013: rupee government bond rate 8.82%, Moody's local-currency rating Baa3, Baa3 default spread 2.25% → Riskfree rate in Rupees = 8.82% - 2.25% = 6.57%. China: renminbi government bond rate 4.30%, rating Aa3, default spread 0.8% → Riskfree rate in RMB = 4.30% - 0.8% = 3.5%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (government bond rate, rating-based default spread → riskfree rate); choosing rating/spread source is judgment.
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, sovereign-default-spread, emerging-markets, local-currency

## p.105 — Three paths to estimating sovereign default spreads

**Teaches:** Three ways to estimate a country's default spread. (1) Sovereign dollar/euro-denominated bonds: the difference between the rate on the country's US-dollar sovereign bond and the US Treasury bond rate. (2) CDS spreads: read the sovereign's default spread directly from the credit default swap market. (3) Average (rating-based) spread: if you know the sovereign rating, use the average default spread for that rating class.
**Formulas:** Default spread = Yield on country's US$-denominated sovereign bond - US Treasury bond rate of same maturity.
**Example:** Brazil, November 2013: (1) 10-year Brazil US$ bond yield 4.25% vs. US 10-year T-Bond 2.75% → default spread = 4.25% - 2.75% = 1.50%; (2) Brazil CDS spread = 2.50%; (3) Brazil rated Baa2 → rating-based default spread = 2%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC per method (bond yields → spread; CDS quote; rating → table spread); choosing among the three estimates is judgment.
**Tags:** sovereign-default-spread, cds, ratings, brazil

## p.108 — Measurement of the equity risk premium

**Teaches:** The equity risk premium (ERP) is the premium investors demand for investing in an average-risk investment, relative to the riskfree rate. As a general proposition the premium should be: greater than zero; increasing in the risk aversion of investors in that market; and increasing in the riskiness of the "average" risk investment.
**Rules:** Sanity checks on any ERP estimate: ERP > 0; ERP rises with market-wide risk aversion; ERP rises with the riskiness of the average investment.

## p.114 — 2. The historical premium approach

**Teaches:** The default approach for the premium: (1) define an estimation period (1928-present, last 50 years, etc.); (2) calculate average returns on a stock index over the period; (3) calculate average returns on a riskless security over the period; (4) take the difference between the two averages and use it as the forward-looking premium. Limitations: it assumes investor risk aversion has not changed systematically over time (only mean-reverting year-to-year variation), and it assumes the riskiness of the "risky" portfolio (the stock index) has not changed systematically over time.
**Formulas:** Historical ERP = Average return on stock index over period - Average return on riskless security over same period.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (index return series + riskless return series + chosen period → historical premium); choices of period, index, riskless security, and averaging method are judgment.
**Tags:** equity-risk-premium, historical-premium, estimation

## p.116 — 3. A forward-looking ERP (implied premium concept)

**Teaches:** If you know the price paid for an asset and have estimates of its expected cash flows, you can compute the IRR of those cash flows — and if you paid that price, the IRR is your expected return. Applied to the market: if stocks are correctly priced in the aggregate and you can estimate expected cash flows from buying stocks, the expected rate of return on stocks is the discount rate that makes the present value of those cash flows equal to the price paid. Subtracting the riskfree rate from that expected return yields the implied equity risk premium — a forward-looking number that can be updated as often as you want.
**Formulas:** Implied ERP = r - Riskfree rate, where r solves: Market price = sum of PV of expected cash flows to equity investors discounted at r (i.e., r is the IRR of the market's expected cash flows given its current price).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (index level, expected cash flows, growth, riskfree rate → r → implied ERP by IRR solve); estimating the expected cash flows and growth is judgment.
**Tags:** equity-risk-premium, implied-premium, irr, forward-looking

## p.118 — The bottom line on equity risk premiums (November 2013)

**Teaches:** For mature markets in November 2013 the class uses an ERP of 5.5%, set equal to the implied premium at that time — deliberately higher than the then-prevailing historical geometric premium of 4.20% (1928-2012, stocks minus T-Bonds). For emerging markets, a melded default-spread approach is used: the sovereign default spread is scaled up to reflect the additional volatility of equities relative to bonds, and that country risk premium is added to the mature-market premium — so countries with lower sovereign ratings carry ERPs above 5.5%.
**Formulas:** Emerging Market ERP = 5.5% + Country Default Spread * (sigma_Equity / sigma_CountryBond), where sigma_Equity = standard deviation of the country's equity market and sigma_CountryBond = standard deviation of the country's government bond, so the ratio scales the bond default spread up to an equity-level risk premium.
**Data:** Historical premium for the US through 2012:

| Period | Arithmetic: Stocks - T.Bills | Arithmetic: Stocks - T.Bonds | Geometric: Stocks - T.Bills | Geometric: Stocks - T.Bonds |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1928-2012 | 7.65% | 5.88% | 5.74% | 4.20% |
| Std Error (1928-2012) | 2.20% | 2.33% | — | — |
| 1962-2012 | 5.93% | 3.91% | 4.60% | 2.93% |
| Std Error (1962-2012) | 2.38% | 2.66% | — | — |
| 2002-2012 | 7.06% | 3.08% | 5.38% | 1.71% |
| Std Error (2002-2012) | 5.82% | 8.11% | — | — |

**Rules:** Mature market ERP (Nov 2013) = 5.5% (implied premium); emerging market ERP = mature premium + scaled country default spread; lower sovereign rating → higher ERP.
**Compute:** MIXED — the emerging-market formula is deterministic given default spread and the two volatilities; choosing 5.5% for mature markets is judgment.

## p.119 — Equity risk premiums for other markets: the data problem

**Teaches:** Historical return data outside the United States covers much shorter periods, and the problem is worse in emerging markets. The historical premiums that emerge from such data carry much greater estimation error. Computing implied equity risk premiums for these markets is possible in principle, but getting the inputs — especially long-term growth estimates — is difficult. This motivates the default-spread-based country risk premium approach on the next page.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

## p.120 — Bond default spreads as country risk premiums (November 2013)
