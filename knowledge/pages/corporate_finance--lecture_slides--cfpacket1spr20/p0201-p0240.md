## p.201 — Divisional costs of capital: Disney and Vale

**Teaches:** Companies with multiple businesses should compute a separate cost of capital for each division. Each divisional rate combines the division's cost of equity (from a bottom-up levered beta for that business), the company-wide cost of debt, the marginal tax rate, and the division's debt ratio. Disney's divisional rates range from 5.69% (Consumer Products) to 8.96% (Interactive), around a company-wide 7.81%. Vale's rates are computed both in US$ and in Brazilian $R (nominal reais). The same business has a much higher rate in a high-inflation currency.

**Formulas:** Cost of capital = Cost of equity * (E/(D+E)) + After-tax cost of debt * (D/(D+E)), where E = market value of equity, D = market value of debt, After-tax cost of debt = pre-tax cost of debt * (1 - marginal tax rate). Here "Debt ratio" = D/(D+E).

## p.202 — Costs of capital: Tata Motors, Baidu and Bookscape

**Teaches:** The same weighted-average cost of capital math, applied to Tata Motors (in Indian rupees), Baidu (in Chinese RMB), and privately held Bookscape. For a private business like Bookscape, the cost of capital depends on the beta used. A market beta assumes a diversified owner; a total beta assumes an undiversified owner. The total-beta version is much higher.

**Formulas:** Cost of capital = Cost of equity * (1 - D/(D+E)) + After-tax cost of debt * (D/(D+E)). Tata Motors (rupees): Cost of capital = 14.49% * (1 - 0.2928) + 6.50% * (0.2928) = 12.15% (14.49% = cost of equity, 6.50% = after-tax cost of debt, 29.28% = debt ratio). Baidu (RMB): Cost of capital = 12.91% * (1 - 0.0523) + 3.45% * (0.0523) = 12.42%.

## p.204 — Choosing a hurdle rate

**Teaches:** Either the cost of equity or the cost of capital can serve as a project's hurdle rate. The choice depends on what returns are being measured. The key rule is consistency: match the hurdle rate to the claimholders whose returns you measure.

**Rules:** If returns are measured to equity investors → hurdle rate = cost of equity. If returns are measured to capital / the whole firm (all claimholders) → hurdle rate = cost of capital.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — a matching/consistency rule, not a computation.
**Tags:** hurdle-rate, cost-of-equity, cost-of-capital, consistency-principle

## p.205 — Back to first principles (framework recap)

**Teaches:** Recap of the corporate finance first-principles map. The objective is to maximize the value of the business (firm). The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn more than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it. The return should reflect the magnitude and timing of cash flows plus all side effects. The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt and the optimal debt/equity mix that maximizes firm value, with debt matching the tenor of assets. The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments beating the hurdle rate, return cash to owners. How much depends on investment opportunities; the form (dividends vs buybacks) depends on owner preference. The highlighted box here is the hurdle rate, marking the end of the hurdle-rate section.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — conceptual framework.
**Tags:** first-principles, corporate-finance-framework, hurdle-rate

## p.208 — Measures of return: earnings versus cash flows

**Teaches:** Two principles govern accounting earnings. Accrual accounting records revenues when goods/services are sold or provided, not when paid, and matches expenses to those revenues. The operating-vs-capital-expenditure distinction treats only expenses tied to current-period revenues as operating expenses. Expenditures creating multi-period benefits are written off over time as depreciation/amortization. To convert accounting earnings to cash flows: (1) add back non-cash expenses like depreciation. (2) Subtract cash outflows that are not expensed, such as capital expenditures. (3) Adjust for changes in working capital to turn accrual revenues/expenses into cash.

**Formulas:** Cash flow = Accounting earnings + non-cash expenses (e.g. depreciation) - capital expenditures - change in working capital (conceptual bridge stated in words on the slide).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given earnings, depreciation, capex, and working-capital change → cash flow.
**Tags:** accrual-accounting, earnings-vs-cash-flows, depreciation, working-capital

## p.209 — Measuring returns right: the basic principles

**Teaches:** Three principles for measuring project returns. (1) Use cash flows rather than earnings; you cannot spend earnings. (2) Use incremental cash flows — only cash flows that occur as a consequence of the investment decision, not total cash flows. (3) Use time-weighted returns, valuing earlier cash flows more than later ones. Summarized as the Return Mantra: "Time-weighted, Incremental Cash Flow Return."

**Rules:** Use cash flows, not earnings; use incremental, not total, cash flows; time-weight the cash flows.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — guiding principles.
**Tags:** incremental-cash-flows, time-value, return-measurement, capital-budgeting

## p.210 — What is an investment/project?

**Teaches:** An investment or project spans a wide spectrum. It includes major strategic entries into new businesses or markets. It includes acquisitions of other firms, which are projects too despite attempts to treat them under separate rules. It also covers new ventures within existing businesses, decisions changing how existing ventures are run, and decisions on how best to deliver a necessary service. Broadly, every choice a firm makes can be framed as an investment.

## p.221 — What should this return be compared to?

## p.222 — Should there be a risk premium for foreign projects?

**Teaches:** Exchange-rate risk is diversifiable, and hence should command no premium in the discount rate, if the company operates in many countries or its investors are globally diversified. For Disney this means no cost-of-capital adjustment for investments in other mature markets (Germany, UK, France). The same diversification argument applies to some political risk. But aspects of political risk in emerging markets are hard to diversify and may reduce expected life or cash flows. For Disney investing in Brazil (or any emerging market), this non-diversifiable risk is incorporated into the cost of capital.

**Rules:** No currency-risk premium if the firm is geographically diversified or investors are globally diversified; no premium for mature-market projects; add a country risk premium for emerging-market projects whose political risk cannot be diversified.

## p.224 — Estimating a hurdle rate for Rio Disney

**Teaches:** The 6.61% theme-park cost of capital (built from a bottom-up levered beta of 0.7537) does not reflect the additional risk of operating in Brazil, an emerging market. The fix has three steps. First, compute a Brazil country risk premium by multiplying Brazil's default spread by the relative equity market volatility, and add it to the mature-market premium. Second, re-estimate the cost of equity with the theme-park beta. Third, recombine with the theme-park debt ratio and after-tax cost of debt to get a project-specific cost of capital of 8.46% in US$.

**Formulas:** Total equity risk premium for Brazil project = mature market premium + Brazil country risk premium = 5.5% + 3% = 8.5%. Cost of equity in US$ = riskfree rate + beta * total ERP = 2.75% + 0.7537 * (8.5%) = 9.16%. Cost of capital in US$ = cost of equity * (E/(D+E)) + after-tax cost of debt * (D/(D+E)) = 9.16% * (0.8976) + 2.40% * (0.1024) = 8.46%. Symbols: 2.75% = US T-bond riskfree rate. 0.7537 = bottom-up levered beta for theme parks. 5.5% = mature market equity risk premium. 3% = Brazil country risk premium (default spread scaled by relative equity volatility). 10.24% = theme-park debt ratio. 2.40% = after-tax cost of debt.

**Example:** Rio Disney: inputs above → project cost of capital 8.46% (vs 6.61% for a mature-market theme park).

**Compute:** MIXED — the arithmetic is deterministic given inputs; estimating the country risk premium and choosing to apply it is judgment.
**Tags:** rio-disney, country-risk-premium, hurdle-rate, cost-of-capital, brazil

## p.227 — Why return on capital is a flawed accounting number

**Teaches:** Return on Invested Capital = after-tax operating income / capital invested in existing assets. Invested capital = book value of equity + book value of debt - cash & cross holdings. The ratio proxies for returns on existing assets, but every piece can be distorted. The numerator suffers from abnormal (unusually good or bad) trailing-12-month earnings, misclassification of leases and R&D, and unusual income or expenses. The life-cycle effect adds bias: current earnings understate long-term potential for young and infrastructure firms. The denominator is distorted by write-offs (writing off mistakes shrinks invested capital and flatters ROIC). Misclassifying capital expenses (R&D) and financial expenses (leases) as operating expenses understates invested capital. Inflation does too, when older asset book values are not adjusted.

**Formulas:** Return on Invested Capital = After-tax operating income / Capital invested in existing assets; After-tax operating income = operating income of most recent 12 months net of the effective tax rate paid; Invested capital = BV of equity + BV of debt - cash & cross holdings.

**Compute:** MIXED — the ratio is deterministic from statements; diagnosing and correcting the distortions is judgment.

## p.232 — Depreciation methods: straight line vs accelerated

**Teaches:** Depreciation methods are broadly straight line (capital expense spread evenly over time) or accelerated (more depreciation in early years, less later). Quiz: for a large investment made this year, which method gives higher net income this year, and which gives higher cash flows this year? (Answers: straight line gives higher near-term net income because it deducts less; accelerated gives higher near-term cash flows because larger early depreciation produces larger early tax savings.)

**Rules:** Straight line → higher reported income early; accelerated → higher cash flows early (via larger early tax shields). Firms should prefer accelerated for tax purposes when maximizing value.

## p.233 — The capital expenditures effect

**Teaches:** Capital expenditures are not accounting expenses but do cause cash outflows. They come in two kinds: new (growth) capex that creates new assets and future growth, and maintenance capex that keeps existing assets going. Both reduce cash flows. Maintenance capex needs grow with project life — a 25-year project requires more maintenance capex than a 2-year project.

**Rules:** Classify capex as growth vs maintenance; longer-lived projects carry heavier maintenance capex; always subtract both from cash flows.

## p.235 — The working capital effect

**Teaches:** Money tied up in inventory or accounts receivable cannot be used elsewhere, so it drains cash flows; financing part of it with supplier credit (accounts payable) reduces the drain. Investments in working capital are cash outflows: any increase in working capital reduces that year's cash flow, any decrease increases it. For closure, working capital is salvaged (recovered) at the end of the project life. Proposition 1: ignoring working capital in capital budgeting overstates cash flows and makes projects look better than they are. Proposition 2: other things equal, reducing working capital requirements increases cash flows on all of a firm's projects.

**Rules:** Increase in WC → cash outflow that year; decrease in WC → cash inflow; salvage remaining WC at project end.

## p.238 — Sunk costs

**Teaches:** A sunk cost is any expenditure already incurred that cannot be recovered even if the project is rejected — e.g. a test market for a consumer product or R&D for a drug. The sunk cost rule: when analyzing a project, sunk costs should not be considered because they are not incremental. Behavioral aside: psychological and behavioral research shows managers find it almost impossible to ignore sunk costs.

**Rules:** Sunk cost rule — exclude already-incurred, unrecoverable expenditures from project analysis.

## p.239 — Test marketing and R&D: the quandary of sunk costs

**Teaches:** Quiz sharpening the sunk-cost rule: a consumer products company spent $100 million on test marketing; ignoring that sunk cost, the project's incremental cash flows create $25 million in value. ## p.240 — Allocated costs

**Teaches:** Firms allocate centralized-pool costs (like G&A) to projects based on some project characteristic — commonly sales or earnings. For large firms these allocations can be big enough to cause project rejections. To the degree the allocated costs are not incremental (they would exist anyway), rejecting projects because of them makes the firm worse off. Only the incremental component of allocated costs should appear in project analysis.

**Rules:** Include only the incremental portion of allocated costs in project cash flows; exclude fixed allocations that would exist regardless of the project.
