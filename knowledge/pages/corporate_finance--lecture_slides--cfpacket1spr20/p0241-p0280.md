## p.242 — Why cash flows must be time-weighted

**Teaches:** Incremental cash flows in earlier years are worth more than incremental cash flows in later years (time value of money). Cash flows occurring at different times cannot simply be added up — they must be brought to the same point in time before aggregation. Moving cash flows through time is called discounting when future cash flows are brought back to the present, and compounding when present cash flows are taken forward to the future.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — conceptual principle (the mechanics are on the next page).

**Tags:** time-value-of-money, discounting, compounding, cash-flows

## p.243 — Present value mechanics: discounting and compounding formulas

**Teaches:** Reference table of discounting and compounding formulas for the five standard cash flow types: simple cash flow, annuity, growing annuity, perpetuity, and growing perpetuity.

**Formulas:**
- Simple CF, discounting: PV = CF_n / (1+r)^n, where CF_n = cash flow at end of period n, r = discount rate per period, n = number of periods.
- Simple CF, compounding: FV = CF_0 * (1+r)^n, where CF_0 = cash flow today.
- Annuity, discounting (PV of an annuity): PV = A * [1 - 1/(1+r)^n] / r, where A = constant annuity cash flow per period.
- Annuity, compounding (FV of an annuity): FV = A * [((1+r)^n - 1) / r].
- Growing annuity, discounting: PV = A*(1+g) * [1 - (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n] / (r - g), where A = cash flow just occurred (grows at g), g = constant growth rate, valid for r ≠ g.
- Perpetuity: PV = A / r, where A = constant cash flow forever.
- Growing perpetuity: PV = (Expected cash flow next year) / (r - g).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs: A or CF, r, g, n → output: PV or FV via the formulas above.

**Tags:** present-value, annuity, perpetuity, growing-perpetuity, discounting-formulas

## p.244 — NPV and IRR defined, with decision rules

**Teaches:** Net Present Value (NPV) is the sum of the present values of ALL cash flows from a project, including the initial investment, discounted at the appropriate hurdle rate: use the cost of capital if the cash flows are cash flows to the firm, and the cost of equity if they are cash flows to equity investors. Internal Rate of Return (IRR) is the discount rate that sets NPV equal to zero; it is a percentage rate of return based on incremental, time-weighted cash flows.

**Formulas:** NPV = sum over all t of CF_t / (1+r)^t, including the initial investment (t=0), where r = hurdle rate (cost of capital for firm cash flows, cost of equity for equity cash flows). IRR = the r that makes NPV = 0.

**Rules:**
- Accept a project if NPV > 0.
- Accept a project if IRR > hurdle rate.
- Match discount rate to cash flow: cost of capital with cash flows to the firm; cost of equity with cash flows to equity.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs: cash flow stream and hurdle rate → outputs: NPV, IRR, accept/reject decision.

**Tags:** npv, irr, decision-rules, hurdle-rate, capital-budgeting

## p.245 — Closure on cash flows: salvage value and terminal value

**Teaches:** For a project with a finite and short life, compute a salvage value: the expected proceeds from selling all the investment in the project at the end of its life, usually set equal to the book value of fixed assets plus working capital. For a project with an infinite or very long life, compute cash flows for a reasonable estimation period and then a terminal value — the present value of all cash flows occurring after the estimation period ends. For the Disney (Rio Disney) theme park, assuming the project lasts forever and cash flows after year 10 grow at 2% (the inflation rate) forever.

**Formulas:** Terminal Value in year 10 = CF in year 11 / (Cost of Capital − Growth Rate), i.e. a growing perpetuity valued one year before the first cash flow.

**Rules:** Short finite life → use salvage value (≈ book value of fixed assets + working capital); long/infinite life → use terminal value as a growing perpetuity; a common conservative choice for perpetual growth is the inflation rate.

**Example:** Rio Disney: CF in year 10 = $715 million, growth = 2%, cost of capital = 8.46%. Terminal Value in year 10 = 715 * 1.02 / (0.0846 − 0.02) = $11,275 million.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (CF_10, g, cost of capital → terminal value); choosing g and estimation period is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** terminal-value, salvage-value, growing-perpetuity, project-life

## p.249 — What the IRR suggests; NPV vs IRR differences

**Teaches:** The Rio Disney project's IRR of 12.60% exceeds the cost of capital of 8.46%, so it is a good project by the IRR rule. NPV and IRR usually agree on accept/reject but can rank projects differently, for three reasons. (1) A project has only one NPV but can have more than one IRR (when cash flows change sign more than once). (2) NPV is a dollar surplus while IRR is a percentage. NPV therefore tends to be larger for large-scale projects, while IRR tends to be higher for small-scale projects. (3) NPV assumes intermediate cash flows are reinvested at the hurdle rate — what you can earn on comparable-risk investments. IRR assumes reinvestment at the IRR itself.

**Rules:** When ranking projects, prefer NPV when scale differs or reinvestment at IRR is unrealistic; watch for multiple IRRs when cash flows change sign more than once.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — interpreting and choosing between the two criteria.

## p.251 — The consistency rule for cash flows

**Teaches:** The cash flows on a project and the discount rate used must be defined in the same terms. If cash flows are in a given currency (e.g., Brazilian Reais, $R), the discount rate must be in that same currency; if cash flows are nominal (or real), the discount rate must be nominal (or real). If this consistency is maintained, project conclusions (NPV, accept/reject) will be identical no matter which currency or nominal/real convention is used.

**Rules:** Currency of cash flows = currency of discount rate; nominal cash flows with nominal rates, real cash flows with real rates. With consistency, NPV is invariant to the choice.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — a consistency principle, not a calculation.

**Tags:** consistency-rule, currency, nominal-vs-real, discount-rate

## p.252 — Disney theme park analysis in Brazilian Reais ($R): setup

**Teaches:** To redo the Rio Disney analysis in Brazilian Reais: inflation was assumed 9% in Brazil and 2% in the US; the spot rate was 2.35 $R/dollar. Expected future exchange rates are derived from purchasing power parity, depreciating the $R at the inflation differential. Expected growth after year 10 remains the inflation rate, but now the 9% $R inflation rate. The $R cost of capital is derived from the dollar cost of capital scaled by the inflation differential.

**Formulas:**
- Expected Exchange Rate_t = Exchange Rate today * (1.09/1.02)^t, i.e. spot rate * ((1 + Brazil inflation)/(1 + US inflation))^t (purchasing power parity).
- $R Cost of Capital = (1 + US$ Cost of Capital) * (1 + Expected Inflation_Brazil)/(1 + Expected Inflation_US) − 1.

**Example:** $R Cost of Capital = (1.0846)(1.09/1.02) − 1 = 15.91%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs: US$ cost of capital, two inflation rates, spot exchange rate → outputs: expected exchange rates by year and $R cost of capital.

## p.256 — Sensitivity analysis and what-if questions

**Teaches:** NPV, IRR, and accounting returns all change as input variable values change; sensitivity analysis checks how sensitive the decision measure is to changes in key assumptions. Two caveats: (1) sensitivity analysis typically changes one variable holding all else constant, but in the real world variables move together; (2) the objective is better decisions, not more tables and numbers. Corollaries: less is more — not everything is worth varying; and a picture is worth a thousand numbers (and tables).

**Rules:** Vary only the assumptions that matter; beware of one-at-a-time changes when variables are correlated; present sensitivity results graphically where possible.

**Compute:** MIXED — recomputing NPV/IRR for changed inputs is deterministic; choosing which variables to vary and interpreting results is judgment.

## p.261 — Equity analysis: the parallels

**Teaches:** The entire investment analysis can be done in equity terms instead of firm terms: returns, cash flows, and hurdle rates are all then defined from the equity investors' perspective. If using accounting returns: the return measure is Return on Equity (ROE) = Net Income / Book Value of Equity, and ROE must exceed the cost of equity. If using discounted cash flow models: cash flows are cash flows after debt payments (cash flows to equity), and the hurdle rate is the cost of equity.

**Formulas:** ROE = Net Income / Book Value of Equity.

**Rules:** Equity-side accounting test: accept if ROE > cost of equity. Equity-side DCF: discount cash flows to equity (after debt payments) at the cost of equity.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC for ROE given net income and book equity; the framework matching is a rule.

## p.267 — From project ROE to firm ROE: the equity return spread

**Teaches:** Just as return on capital vs cost of capital measures overall project quality at the firm level, return on equity vs cost of equity measures whether a firm is creating value for its equity investors. Compute ROE (net income as a percentage of book equity) and compare it to the cost of equity; the difference is the equity return spread. This measure is particularly useful for financial service firms, where capital, return on capital, and cost of capital are difficult to nail down. For non-financial firms it is a secondary (and more volatile) performance measure; it usually gives the same general answer as the excess return from return on capital, but the two measures can diverge.

**Formulas:** Equity Return Spread = Return on Equity − Cost of Equity, where ROE = Net Income / Book Value of Equity.

**Rules:** Positive equity return spread → value creation for equity investors. Prefer ROE-based spread for financial service firms; treat it as secondary for non-financial firms.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given ROE and cost of equity; interpretation and choice of measure is judgment.

## p.275 — A decision framework for hedging risk

**Teaches:** Flowchart for deciding whether a firm should hedge a given risk. Start with the cost to the firm of hedging the risk. If the cost is negligible: hedge if there is a significant benefit in higher cash flows or a lower discount rate; otherwise be indifferent. If the cost is high, first ask whether there is a significant benefit in higher expected cash flows or a lower discount rate. If not, do not hedge — the benefits are small relative to costs. If there is a benefit, ask whether marginal investors can hedge this risk more cheaply than the firm can. If they cannot, the firm should hedge. If they can, ask whether the benefits persist when investors hedge instead of the firm. If yes, let the risk pass through to investors and let them hedge it; if no, the firm should hedge. Side panels list the value trade-off sources: cash flow benefits (tax benefits, better project choices), survival benefits / truncation risk (protection against catastrophic risk, reduced default risk), and discount rate benefits (hedging macro risks lowers cost of equity; reduced default risk lowers cost of debt or raises debt capacity); and a pricing trade-off: (effect on earnings multiple) x (earnings level and volatility).

**Rules:**
- Hedging cost negligible + significant cash-flow or discount-rate benefit → hedge; no benefit → indifferent.
- Hedging cost high + no significant benefit → do not hedge.
- Hedging cost high + benefit exists + investors cannot hedge cheaper → hedge.
- Investors can hedge cheaper + benefits persist when they do → pass the risk through to investors.
- Investors can hedge cheaper but benefits do not persist → firm hedges.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — qualitative decision tree.

## p.276 — Acquisitions are projects too

**Teaches:** An acquisition is an investment/project like any other, so all the rules for traditional investments apply. An acquisition makes sense only if it has positive NPV: the present value of expected cash flows from the acquisition must exceed the price paid. Equivalently, the IRR of the cash flows to the firm (or equity) must exceed the cost of capital (or cost of equity) on the acquisition. Cash flow estimates should include any possible synergy cash flows. Crucially, the discount rate must be based on the risk of the investment — the target company — not on the acquiring company.

**Rules:** Accept an acquisition if PV of expected cash flows (including synergies) > price paid, i.e. NPV > 0; equivalently IRR > cost of capital (firm cash flows) or cost of equity (equity cash flows). Always discount at a rate reflecting the target's risk, never the acquirer's.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT framing; the NPV test itself is deterministic given cash flows, price, and rate.
