## p.2 — Income statement across the corporate life cycle
**Teaches:** How the shape of the income statement changes as a firm moves through the corporate life cycle. Revenues follow an S-curve and then decline. Earnings lag revenues: they start negative, turn positive as the firm scales, peak in maturity, and then fall — often faster than revenues — in decline. The stages carry Damodaran's life-cycle names: The Lightbulb (Idea) Moment, The Product Test, The Bar Mitzvah, The Scaling-up Test, The Midlife Crisis, The End Game. The point: what you should expect in an income statement (losses vs. profits, growth vs. no growth) depends on where the company sits in its life cycle.
**Data:**
| Lifecycle stage | Revenue & revenue growth | Profit pattern |
|---|---|---|
| Start-up | Small, but high growth rate | Losses, as business is built up, but sales are in future |
| Young Growth | Acquiring substance, with high growth | Losses accelerate, as expenses grow faster than revenues |
| High Growth | Large and still growing | Losses decline & profits emerge, as revenues grow faster than expenses |
| Mature Growth | Large, but growth rate drops off | Profits climb, as economies of scale kick in |
| Mature Stable | Large, but stagnant | Profits level off, and grow with revenues |
| Decline | Revenues start dropping | Profits decline, often faster than revenues |
**Rules:** Treat losses at a start-up or young-growth firm as a normal life-cycle feature (expenses growing faster than revenues), not automatically a sign of a bad business. Treat declining profits at an aging firm as expected; profits often fall faster than revenues.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classifying a firm's life-cycle stage requires assessment of its revenue growth and profit pattern).

## p.3 — Young company example: Peloton's income statement (2019 prospectus)
**Teaches:** What a young (pre-IPO) company's income statement looks like, using Peloton Interactive's Consolidated Statements of Operations and Comprehensive Loss from its 2019 IPO prospectus (fiscal years ended June 30; $ millions). Four features are highlighted. First, explosive revenue growth: total revenue $218.6M in 2017 → $435.0M in 2018 → $915.0M in 2019. Second, positive and growing gross profit: $73.9M → $189.6M → $383.6M. Third, widening losses before taxes ($(71.1)M → $(47.8)M → $(195.6)M) because operating expenses grow with scale. Fourth, large and rising R&D spending: $13.0M → $23.4M → $54.8M. Revenue is broken into Connected Fitness Products ($719.2M in 2019), Subscription ($181.1M), and Other ($14.7M), each with its own cost of revenue. Net loss per share (basic and diluted) was $(5.97), $(2.18), $(10.72) across the three years; 2019 net loss attributable to common stockholders was $(245.7)M on 22,911,764 weighted-average shares. This is the classic case of the "young growth" stage from p.2: high revenue growth, positive gross margins, but operating losses.
**Example:** Peloton FY2019 ($ millions). Total revenue 915.0 − total cost of revenue 531.4 = gross profit 383.6. Gross profit 383.6 − total operating expenses 585.8 = loss from operations (202.3). Adding total other income 6.7 gives loss before income taxes (195.6). After the tax provision, net loss was (195.6).

## p.4 — Growth company example: Netflix's income statement (2020 filing, FY2017-2019)
**Teaches:** What a growth company's income statement looks like, using Netflix's Consolidated Statements of Operations ($ thousands, years ended Dec 31). Five features are highlighted. First, cost of revenues is the dominant expense (content costs: $12,440,213 in 2019 vs revenues $20,156,447). Second, "Technology and development" is Netflix's label for what other firms call R&D ($1,545,149 in 2019). Third, basic and diluted share counts differ (2019: 437,799 basic vs 451,765 diluted thousand shares; EPS $4.26 basic vs $4.13 diluted). Fourth, foreign currency translation adjustments sit in other comprehensive income ($(3,939) in 2019, $975 in 2018, $27,409 in 2017). Fifth, changes in unrealized gains/losses on available-for-sale securities appear net of tax ($599 in 2017). Unlike Peloton, Netflix is profitable and growing: revenues $11.69B (2017) → $15.79B (2018) → $20.16B (2019), operating income $838.7M → $1,605.2M → $2,604.3M, net income $558.9M → $1,211.2M → $1,866.9M. Comprehensive income (net income plus other comprehensive income) was $1,862,977 thousand in 2019.
**Example:** Netflix 2019 ($ thousands). Revenues 20,156,447 − cost of revenues 12,440,213 − marketing 2,652,462 − technology & development 1,545,149 − G&A 914,369 = operating income 2,604,254. Subtract interest expense 626,023 and add interest and other income 84,000 to get income before taxes 2,062,231. Subtract the tax provision 195,315 to get net income 1,866,916. Net income + other comprehensive loss (3,939) = comprehensive income 1,862,977.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (line items → operating income, pre-tax income, net income, EPS = net income / weighted-average shares, comprehensive income); labeling choices (e.g., technology & development as R&D) are JUDGMENT.

## p.5 — Mature company example: Coca-Cola (2019)
**Teaches:** What a mature company's income statement looks like, using Coca-Cola's 2019 statement ($ millions, years ended Dec 31). Three features are highlighted. First, cost of goods sold is the classic direct-cost line ($14,619 in 2019). Second, selling, general and administrative expenses form the combined operating-expense line ($12,103 in 2019). Third, "Equity income (loss) — net" ($1,049 in 2019) is income from minority holdings (e.g., bottlers) accounted for under the equity method, shown below operating income. The statement shows the mature-firm pattern: large, slow-growing revenues with high and stable profits. Net operating revenues were $36,212 in 2017, $34,300 in 2018, and $37,266 in 2019. Operating income was $10,086 in 2019 and consolidated net income $8,985, of which $8,920 went to Coca-Cola shareowners after $65 to noncontrolling interests. Basic EPS $2.09, diluted $2.07 in 2019, on 4,276M basic / 4,314M diluted average shares (effect of dilutive securities: 38M).
**Example:** Coca-Cola 2019 ($ millions). Net operating revenues 37,266 − COGS 14,619 = gross profit 22,647. Subtract SG&A 12,103 and other operating charges 458 to get operating income 10,086. Add interest income 563, subtract interest expense 946, add equity income 1,049 and other income 34 to get income before income taxes 10,786. Subtract income taxes 1,801 to get consolidated net income 8,985. Subtract noncontrolling interests 65 to get net income to Coca-Cola shareowners 8,920.

## p.7 — Aging company example: Toyota (FY2019-FY2020)
**Teaches:** What an aging (mature, late-cycle) company's income statement looks like, using Toyota's Consolidated Statements of Income (yen in millions; FY2019 ended Mar 31, 2019 and FY2020 ended Mar 31, 2020). Three features are highlighted. First, Toyota reports revenues and costs from financing operations separately from product sales, because it runs a lending business alongside manufacturing. Financing revenue was ¥2,120,343 in FY2019 and ¥2,170,243 in FY2020; cost of financing operations was ¥1,392,290 and ¥1,379,620. Second, foreign exchange gain (loss), net, appears as a distinct other-income item. It flipped from a ¥12,400 gain in FY2019 to a ¥(79,020) loss in FY2020. Third, equity in earnings of affiliated companies (¥360,066 in FY2019, ¥271,152 in FY2020) reflects Toyota's cross-holdings. The aging-firm pattern shows in stagnant-to-declining revenues (total net revenues ¥30,225,681 → ¥29,929,992, a decrease of ¥295,689) and flat operating income (¥2,467,545 → ¥2,442,869). Net income attributable to Toyota Motor Corporation rose from ¥1,882,873 to ¥2,076,183, driven partly by non-operating items.
**Example:** Toyota FY2020 (¥ millions). Total net revenues 29,929,992 − total costs and expenses 27,487,123 = operating income 2,442,869. Add total other income 111,738 to get income before income taxes and equity in affiliates 2,554,607. Subtract the tax provision 683,430 and add equity in earnings of affiliates 271,152 to get net income 2,142,329. Subtract noncontrolling interests 66,146 to get net income to Toyota 2,076,183.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (line items → operating income, pre-tax income, net income); recognizing the embedded finance business and aging profile is JUDGMENT.

## p.8 — Sector and industry differences in income statements
**Teaches:** Accounting standards for measuring revenues and earnings are broadly the same across companies, but some sector-specific differences exist. For commodity companies, the differences arise mainly from the costs of exploring for commodity reserves and of extracting the commodity. For financial service companies, the challenge lies in defining revenues and debt: for banks, debt is less a source of capital and more raw material.
**Rules:** When reading a commodity company's statement, look for exploration/extraction cost treatment; when reading a financial service company's statement, do not treat debt as ordinary financing capital — treat it as an operating input.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual classification of sector differences).
**Tags:** sector-differences, commodity-companies, financial-services, accounting-standards

## p.11 — Financial service company example: HSBC in 2019
**Teaches:** What a bank's income statement looks like, using HSBC's consolidated income statement ($m, years ended Dec 31). Banks do not report "revenues − COGS". The core revenue lines are net figures instead. Net interest income is interest income $54,695m minus interest expense $(24,233)m, giving $30,462m in 2019. Net fee income is fee income $15,439m minus fee expense $(3,416)m, giving $12,023m. Here interest expense is an operating cost, not a financing cost — this is p.8's point that debt is raw material for banks. Total operating income ($71,024m in 2019) is the bank's top-line total. The change in expected credit losses and other credit impairment charges ($(2,756)m in 2019) is the loan-loss provision line, a bank-specific expense. Goodwill impairment ($(7,349)m in 2019) was a large one-off charge. Profit before tax was $13,347m and tax expense $(4,639)m. Profit for the year was $8,708m ($15,025m in 2018, $11,879m in 2017), mainly attributable to ordinary shareholders of the parent ($5,969m). Basic and diluted EPS were both $0.30 in 2019 (vs $0.63 in 2018, $0.48 in 2017).
**Formulas:** Net interest income = Interest income − Interest expense. Net fee income = Fee income − Fee expense. (Bank revenue lines are nets of matched income and expense.)
**Rules:** For financial service firms, treat interest expense as an operating expense inside revenue measurement (netted into net interest income), not as a financing cost below operating income; expect a credit-loss/impairment provision line as a recurring operating expense.
**Example:** HSBC 2019 ($m). Net interest income 30,462 + net fee income 12,023 + trading and other income lines = total operating income 71,024. After payments to policyholders (14,926), net operating income before credit impairments = 56,098. Subtract expected credit losses (2,756) to get net operating income 53,342. Subtract total operating expenses (42,349), which include goodwill impairment (7,349), to get operating profit 10,993. Add share of profit in associates/JVs 2,354 to get profit before tax 13,347. Subtract tax (4,639) to get profit for the year 8,708.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (income/expense lines → net interest income, net fee income, operating profit, profit before tax, profit for the year); deciding how to define a bank's revenue/debt is JUDGMENT.

## p.13 — Bottom line: convergence and comparability of income statements
**Teaches:** Three closing takeaways. First, despite much talk about differences in accounting standards around the world and across businesses, standards are converging globally rather than diverging. Second, revenues and operating expenses take different forms for different companies — product sales, subscriptions, interest income, commodity sales. The end game is still the same: gross profit, operating income, and net income all measure profits at different levels of the income statement. Third, in theory these numbers should therefore be comparable across companies, but in practice accounting inconsistencies in how expenses are measured and categorized can create problems for cross-company comparison.
**Rules:** Use gross profit, operating income, and net income as the common comparison anchors across firms, but adjust for inconsistent expense measurement/categorization before comparing.
