## p.2 — The essence of relative valuation (pricing)

**Teaches:** In relative valuation the value of an asset is derived by comparing it to market prices of similar or comparable assets, not from its own cash flows. The process has three steps. (1) Identify comparable assets and obtain their market values. (2) Convert those market values into standardized values, because absolute prices cannot be compared directly; this standardization creates price multiples. (3) Compare the multiple for the asset being analyzed to the multiples of comparables, controlling for differences between firms that might affect the multiple, to judge whether the asset is under- or over-valued.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing comparables and controlling for differences requires reasoning).
**Tags:** relative-valuation, multiples, pricing, comparables

## p.3 — Relative valuation is pervasive

**Teaches:** Most asset valuations in practice are relative. Almost 85% of equity research reports are based on a multiple and comparables, and more than 50% of all acquisition valuations are based on multiples; rules of thumb built on multiples often drive final valuation judgments. Even discounted cash flow valuations in consulting and corporate finance are often relative valuations in disguise: analysts back into numbers obtained from multiples, and terminal values in many DCFs are estimated using a multiple.

## p.5 — The market imperative: when/why relative valuation is used

**Teaches:** Relative valuation is much more likely than DCF to reflect current market perceptions and moods — an advantage when the objective is to sell a security at today's price (e.g., an IPO) or when investing on momentum-based strategies. With relative valuation there will always be a significant proportion of securities that look undervalued and overvalued (it is a relative game). Because portfolio managers are judged on relative performance (versus the market and other managers), relative valuation is tailored to their needs. It also generally requires less information than DCF, especially when multiples are used as screens.

## p.6 — Anatomy of a multiple: numerator and denominator choices

**Teaches:** A multiple is a standardized estimate of price: Multiple = Numerator (what you are paying for the asset) / Denominator (what you are getting in return). Numerator choices: Market value of equity; Firm value = Market value of equity + Market value of debt; Enterprise value (EV) = Market value of equity + Market value of debt − Cash (market value of operating assets only). Denominator choices fall into four groups. Revenues: accounting revenues, or drivers like number of customers, subscribers, or units. Earnings: to equity investors, net income or EPS; to the firm, operating income (EBIT). Cash flow: to equity, net income + depreciation or free cash flow to equity; to the firm, EBIT + D&A (EBITDA) or free cash flow to firm. Book value: equity = BV of equity; firm = BV of debt + BV of equity; invested capital = BV of equity + BV of debt − Cash.
**Formulas:** Firm value = MV of equity + MV of debt. Enterprise Value (EV) = MV of equity + MV of debt − Cash. Invested Capital = BV of equity + BV of debt − Cash. Multiple = price measure / return measure.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given market/book values and cash → firm value, EV, invested capital, any multiple).
**Tags:** multiples, enterprise-value, firm-value, ebitda, book-value

## p.7 — The four steps to deconstructing multiples

**Teaches:** Four-step framework for using any multiple. (1) Define the multiple: the same multiple can be defined differently by different users, so when using someone else's multiples you must know how they were estimated. (2) Describe the multiple: you need its cross-sectional distribution across the market to judge whether a given value is high or low. (3) Analyze the multiple: understand which fundamentals drive it and the nature (usually non-linear) of the relationship. (4) Apply the multiple: defining the comparable universe and controlling for differences is far harder in practice than in theory.
**Rules:** Always run a multiple through define → describe → analyze → apply before using it.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** multiples, framework, relative-valuation

## p.8 — Definitional tests: consistency and uniformity

**Teaches:** Two definitional tests for any multiple. Consistency — Proposition 1: numerator and denominator must belong to the same claimholders. Value of equity should be divided by equity earnings or equity book value. Firm value should be divided by firm earnings or firm book value. Never mix them; price/EBITDA is inconsistent. Uniformity — the variables used must be estimated the same way across all firms in the comparable list; if earnings- or book-value-based multiples are used, accounting rules must be applied consistently across the assets.
**Rules:** Proposition 1: match claimholders in numerator and denominator (equity value ÷ equity measure; firm value ÷ firm measure). Estimate variables uniformly across the comparable set.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification test).
**Tags:** multiples, consistency, definitional-tests, claimholders

## p.9 — PE ratio: definition and variants

**Teaches:** PE = Market Price per Share / Earnings per Share. Variants arise from how price and earnings are defined. Price is usually the current price, sometimes the average price for the year. EPS can be: EPS in the most recent financial year (current PE), EPS in the trailing 12 months (trailing PE), forecasted EPS next year (forward PE), or forecasted EPS in a future year.
**Formulas:** PE = Market Price per Share / Earnings per Share.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (price and EPS → PE), but choosing the variant is JUDGMENT.

## p.11 — EV/EBITDA multiple: definition and consistency questions

**Teaches:** The enterprise value to EBITDA multiple nets cash out against debt to get enterprise value and divides by EBITDA. Cash is netted out because the income earned on cash (interest income) is not part of EBITDA — so leaving cash in the numerator while excluding its income from the denominator would be inconsistent. The slide also asks what to do with cross holdings. Minority interests arise from consolidated subsidiaries not fully owned: their full EBITDA sits in the denominator, but only part of the equity value is yours. Majority and minority passive holdings pose similar problems. Both create numerator/denominator mismatches that must be corrected.
**Formulas:** EV/EBITDA = (Market Value of Equity + Market Value of Debt − Cash) / Earnings before Interest, Taxes and Depreciation.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (MV equity, MV debt, cash, EBITDA → EV/EBITDA); handling cross holdings is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** ev-ebitda, enterprise-value, cross-holdings, consistency

## p.13 — Descriptive tests for a multiple

**Teaches:** Questions to answer about a multiple's distribution before using it: What is the average and standard deviation across the market? What is the median (often a more reliable comparison point than the mean)? How large are the outliers and how are they handled — throwing out outliers seems obvious, but since outliers of multiples usually lie on one side (large positive numbers), trimming biases the estimate. Are there cases where the multiple cannot be estimated (e.g., negative earnings), and does ignoring those cases bias the sample? How has the multiple changed over time?
**Rules:** Prefer the median to the mean for skewed multiples; beware one-sided outlier trimming and survivorship of estimable firms — both bias comparisons.

## p.20 — Analytical tests: fundamentals drive multiples

## p.21 — A simple analytical device: derive the intrinsic version of any multiple

**Teaches:** Recipe for connecting any multiple to fundamentals in three steps: start with a basic intrinsic value model (simple), divide both sides by the denominator of the multiple you want to deconstruct, and you end up with an intrinsic version of the multiple expressed in terms of its value drivers. For an equity multiple: start with a dividend or FCFE model, e.g. Price = EPS * Payout / (r − g); divide by book value to get Price/Book = ROE * Payout / (r − g), so Price/Book = f(ROE, r, g, Payout). For an EV multiple: start with an operating asset model, EV = EBIT(1 − t)(1 − RIR)/(WACC − g); divide by sales to get EV/Sales = After-tax Operating Margin * (1 − RIR)/(WACC − g), so EV/Sales = f(after-tax operating margin, RIR, WACC, g). RIR = reinvestment rate, t = tax rate, r = cost of equity, g = growth rate.
**Formulas:** Price = EPS * Payout ratio / (r − g). Price/Book = ROE * Payout ratio / (r − g). EV = EBIT(1 − t)(1 − RIR) / (WACC − g). EV/Sales = After-tax operating margin * (1 − RIR) / (WACC − g). Where r = cost of equity, g = stable growth rate, ROE = return on equity, RIR = reinvestment rate, t = tax rate, WACC = cost of capital.
**Rules:** Equity multiples → deconstruct with an equity DCF model; EV multiples → deconstruct with an operating-asset (firm) DCF model. Divide the value model by the multiple's denominator.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given ROE, payout, r, g → P/B; given margin, RIR, WACC, g → EV/Sales).
**Tags:** intrinsic-multiples, price-to-book, ev-sales, value-drivers

## p.22 — PE ratio fundamentals from a stable-growth DCF

**Teaches:** To find the fundamentals of PE, start with a basic equity DCF. With the stable-growth dividend discount model, P0 = DPS1/(r − gn); dividing both sides by current EPS gives PE as a function of payout, growth, and risk. If FCFE replaces dividends, the same algebra substitutes FCFE/Earnings for payout. So PE increases with the payout ratio (or FCFE/earnings) and growth, and decreases with the cost of equity r.
**Formulas:** P0 = DPS1 / (r − gn), where P0 = price today, DPS1 = expected dividends per share next year, r = cost of equity, gn = stable growth rate. P0/EPS0 = PE = [Payout Ratio * (1 + gn)] / (r − gn). FCFE version: P0 = FCFE1 / (r − gn); P0/EPS0 = PE = [(FCFE/Earnings) * (1 + gn)] / (r − gn).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (payout, g, r → PE).
**Tags:** pe-ratio, dividend-discount-model, fcfe, fundamentals

## p.23 — Fundamental PE for a high-growth firm (two-stage model)

**Teaches:** The PE ratio of a high-growth firm can also be tied to fundamentals via the two-stage dividend discount model: value equals the present value of dividends during n years of high growth g plus the present value of the terminal value based on stable growth gn. Dividing the whole expression by EPS0 gives a two-stage intrinsic PE. For firms that do not pay out what they can afford, substitute FCFE/Earnings for the payout ratio.
**Formulas:** P0 = [EPS0 * Payout Ratio * (1+g) * (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)] / (r − g) + [EPS0 * Payout Ratio_n * (1+g)^n * (1+gn)] / [(r − gn)(1+r)^n]. Dividing by EPS0: P0/EPS0 = [Payout Ratio * (1+g) * (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)] / (r − g) + [Payout Ratio_n * (1+g)^n * (1+gn)] / [(r − gn)(1+r)^n]. Symbols: EPS0 = current earnings per share, g = high-growth-phase growth rate, n = years of high growth, r = cost of equity, gn = stable growth rate, Payout Ratio = payout during high growth, Payout Ratio_n = payout in stable phase.
**Rules:** For firms not paying what they can afford in dividends, substitute FCFE/Earnings for the payout ratio.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (payout ratios, g, gn, n, r → intrinsic PE).
**Tags:** pe-ratio, two-stage-model, high-growth, intrinsic-multiples

## p.24 — Worked example: intrinsic PE for a two-stage firm

**Teaches:** Applies the two-stage intrinsic PE formula to a hypothetical firm.
**Example:** Inputs — High growth phase: expected growth 25%, payout ratio 20%, beta 1.00, lasting 5 years; Stable phase: growth 8%, payout 50%, beta 1.00, forever after year 5. Riskfree rate = T.Bond rate = 6%; required return r = 6% + 1(5.5%) = 11.5% (CAPM with 5.5% equity risk premium). Computation: P0/EPS0 = [0.20 * 1.25 * (1 − 1.25^5/1.115^5)] / (0.115 − 0.25) + [0.50 * 1.25^5 * 1.08] / [(0.115 − 0.08) * 1.115^5] = 28.75. Result: intrinsic PE = 28.75.
**Formulas:** Same two-stage PE formula as p.23, with r from CAPM: r = riskfree rate + beta * equity risk premium.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (growth rates, payouts, beta, riskfree, ERP, n → PE = 28.75).

## p.35 — PEG ratio: definition and fundamental derivation

**Teaches:** PEG Ratio = PE ratio / expected growth rate in EPS. For consistency, the growth rate must reflect the same EPS used in the PE (e.g., forward PE with forward growth) and should cover the same time period. The fundamentals of PEG come from the two-stage equity DCF. Dividing the two-stage price equation by earnings gives PE; dividing PE again by expected growth g gives the intrinsic PEG. This shows PEG is itself a function of payout, risk, and growth — not a growth-neutral number.
**Formulas:** PEG = PE / expected growth rate in EPS. Two-stage price: P0 = [EPS0 * Payout Ratio * (1+g) * (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)]/(r − g) + [EPS0 * Payout Ratio_n * (1+g)^n * (1+gn)]/[(r − gn)(1+r)^n]. Intrinsic PEG: PEG = [Payout Ratio * (1+g) * (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)]/[g(r − g)] + [Payout Ratio_n * (1+g)^n * (1+gn)]/[g(r − gn)(1+r)^n]. Symbols: g = high growth rate (in decimal, and in % terms for the divisor convention), n = high-growth years, r = cost of equity, gn = stable growth, Payout Ratio_n = stable payout.
**Rules:** Growth rate used in PEG must match the EPS basis of the PE and the same time period.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (payout, g, gn, n, r → intrinsic PEG).
**Tags:** peg-ratio, intrinsic-multiples, two-stage-model, growth

## p.36 — PEG ratios and fundamentals

**Teaches:** Risk and payout, which affect PE, continue to affect PEG ratios — so comparing PEG across companies embeds implicit assumptions about those variables. Crucially, dividing PE by expected growth does not neutralize growth's effect, because the relationship between growth and value is non-linear and fairly complex even in a two-stage model. A low PEG is therefore not automatically cheap.
**Rules:** Do not treat PEG as growth-neutral; companies with different risk, payout, or growth levels are not directly comparable on PEG.
