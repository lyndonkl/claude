## p.81 — Pricing across the entire market: why not?
**Teaches:** Instead of pricing a stock against a small "comparable firm" peer group, you can use the entire cross-section of firms in a market to predict PE ratios. The simplest way to summarize the whole market's information is a multiple regression with the PE ratio as the dependent variable and proxies for risk, growth and payout as the independent variables. This lets you control for fundamental differences statistically rather than by hand-picking peers.

## p.84 — Problems with the regression methodology
**Teaches:** Market-wide PE regressions have three problems. (1) They assume a linear relationship between PE and the financial proxies. That may not be appropriate. (2) The relationship between PE and fundamentals may not be stable over time. If it shifts year to year, predictions are unreliable. (3) The independent variables are correlated with each other; for example, high-growth firms tend to be high-risk. This multicollinearity makes coefficients unreliable and may explain their large period-to-period swings.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** regression, multicollinearity, model-limitations, pe-ratio

## p.85 — Statistically insignificant coefficients
**Teaches:** A statistically insignificant coefficient only adds noise to a regression's predictions. Use t-statistics to judge significance, and with small samples don't overload the regression with independent variables. If a variable is insignificant, drop it even if fundamentals say it should matter — in pricing, the market determines what matters.
**Rules:** t-statistic > 2 = very good (significant); t between 1 and 2 = marginal; t < 1 = noise. If a coefficient is insignificant, remove the variable from the regression.
**Compute:** MIXED (t-stat thresholds are deterministic; deciding to drop a variable is a rule applied with judgment).
**Tags:** regression, statistical-significance, t-statistic, heuristics

## p.100 — The pricing game: choices
**Teaches:** Pricing a company with multiples involves four choices. Each choice has its own questions. (1) Value measure: enterprise, equity or firm value? Ask whether it is a financial service business and whether leverage differs a lot across firms. (2) Scalar: revenues, earnings, cash flows or book value? Ask how you measure value, whether the scaling number is positive, and how much accounting choices affect it. (3) Timing and normalizing: current, trailing, forward or "really forward" numbers? Ask where the firm sits in the life cycle, how cyclical the number is, and whether forecasts exist. (4) Comparable group: global or local peers, similar size or all firms? Ask how much companies share globally, whether size affects economics, how big a sample you need, and how you will control for differences.
**Rules:** Match the value measure to the business (equity multiples for financial service firms); ensure the scaling variable is positive; normalize for cyclicality/life-cycle stage; choose peer breadth deliberately and plan how to control for differences.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (all four choices are analyst decisions).
**Tags:** multiples, pricing-framework, comparables, decision-checklist

## p.101 — Relative valuation: closing propositions
**Teaches:** Proposition 1: relative valuation only concludes a stock is under/over valued relative to your comparable group — your relative judgment can be right while the stock is still hopelessly overvalued in absolute terms. Proposition 2: no two assets are identical; if you don't control for fundamental differences in risk, cash flows and growth across firms, your conclusions reflect your flawed judgments rather than market misvaluations. Bottom line: relative valuation is pricing, not valuation.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, pricing-vs-value, propositions

## p.102 — Reviewing: the four steps to understanding multiples
**Teaches:** Any multiple demands a four-step discipline. (1) Define it: check numerator/denominator consistency and uniform estimation across firms. (2) Describe it: multiples have skewed distributions, so averages are seldom good indicators of the typical multiple. Also check for bias when the multiple cannot be estimated (e.g., negative-earnings firms get dropped). (3) Analyze it: identify the companion variable that drives the multiple and examine the nature of the relationship. (4) Apply it.
**Rules:** Define consistently; use medians not means (skewed distributions); watch for sample bias from inestimable multiples; always find the companion variable before applying a multiple.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** multiples, methodology, four-steps, checklist

## p.104 — What is asset based valuation?
**Teaches:** There are three ways to value a business. Intrinsic valuation values it from the cash flows you expect it to generate over time. Relative valuation values it from how similar businesses are priced. Asset based valuation values the business by valuing its individual assets, which may be tangible or intangible.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (definitional).
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, definitions, intrinsic-vs-relative

## p.105 — Why would you do asset based valuation?
**Teaches:** There are three motives. (1) Liquidation: you sell the business's assets piecemeal rather than as a going concern. So you estimate what each asset or asset class fetches on its own. (2) Accounting mission: US and international standards moved to "fair value" accounting. Accountants must restate balance sheets at fair rather than book value. (3) Sum of the parts: value divisions one by one. Potential acquirers do this before restructuring. Investors do it to find businesses selling for less than the sum of their parts ("cheap").
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, liquidation, fair-value, sum-of-the-parts

## p.106 — How do you do asset based valuation?
**Teaches:** There are three mechanics for valuing individual assets. (1) Intrinsic value: estimate each asset's expected cash flows and discount at a risk-adjusted rate. (2) Relative value: find similar assets that sold recently and price each asset off those transactions. (3) Accounting value: use the asset's book value as a proxy for its value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of mechanic); the intrinsic/relative computations that follow are deterministic given inputs.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, methodology, book-value

## p.107 — When is asset-based valuation easiest?
**Teaches:** Asset-based valuation is easiest under three conditions. (1) Assets are separable. Real estate holdings or holding companies of independent businesses work well. Interrelated assets are problematic; brand name cuts across assets and is hard to value separately. (2) Earnings and cash flows can be traced to individual assets. It is much harder when the business earns money but each asset's role cannot be isolated. (3) An active market for similar assets exists, so you can draw transaction prices for the relative-pricing route.
**Rules:** Prefer asset-based valuation when assets are separable, have stand-alone cash flows, and trade in active markets; avoid it for synergistic/intangible cross-cutting assets like brand.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, separability, criteria

## p.108 — I. Liquidation valuation
**Teaches:** Liquidation valuation asks what you would get from selling the business's assets today rather than as a going concern. It therefore makes more sense to price the assets (relative valuation) than to intrinsically value them. For separable, traded assets (e.g., real estate) pricing is easy; otherwise book value is often used as a proxy for, or basis for estimating, liquidation value. If the liquidation is urgent, attach an additional discount to the estimated value.
**Rules:** Use pricing (not DCF) for liquidation; use book value as fallback proxy for non-traded assets; apply a discount for urgency/fire sales.

## p.109 — II. Accounting valuation: glimmers from FAS 157
**Teaches:** FAS 157 fair-value accounting asks accountants to attach the values that "market participants" would pay/receive for assets/liabilities. The definition is an exit price: the price received to sell the asset or paid to transfer the liability, not an entry price. This tilts fair value toward relative valuation. The hierarchy puts market prices at the top and accepts intrinsic value only when market prices aren't accessible. The mission is split: accountants must also back relative valuations with intrinsic valuations, which often leads to reverse engineering — arriving at values first and developing the valuations later.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** fair-value-accounting, fas-157, exit-price, accounting-valuation

## p.110 — III. Sum of the parts valuation: whose motive?
**Teaches:** You can value a company in pieces using either relative or intrinsic valuation; which you use depends on who you are and why. A long-term passive investor looking for market mistakes that will get corrected should do intrinsic valuation of the individual assets. An activist investor planning to acquire or push for change (splitting the company up to capture the value increase) should focus on relative valuation, since the pieces will be sold/spun at market prices.
**Rules:** Passive long-term investor → intrinsic sum-of-the-parts; activist/acquirer → relative (pricing) sum-of-the-parts.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** sum-of-the-parts, activist-investing, intrinsic-vs-relative
