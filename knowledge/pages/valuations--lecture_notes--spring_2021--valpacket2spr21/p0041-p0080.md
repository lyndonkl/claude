## p.41 — PEG Ratios and Fundamentals: Propositions

**Teaches:** Three propositions about PEG ratios. Proposition 1: high-risk companies trade at much lower PEG ratios than low-risk companies with the same expected growth rate; corollary — the firm that looks most undervalued on PEG in a sector may simply be the riskiest firm. Proposition 2: companies that attain growth more efficiently (investing less in higher-return projects) have higher PEG ratios than companies growing at the same rate less efficiently. Corollary — firms that look cheap on PEG may have high reinvestment rates and poor project returns. Proposition 3: companies with very low or very high growth rates tend to have higher PEG ratios than average-growth firms, and the bias is worse for low-growth stocks; corollary — PEG ratios do NOT neutralize the growth effect.
**Rules:** Do not conclude a stock is cheap purely because its PEG is low relative to the sector — check risk, reinvestment efficiency, and whether its growth rate is near the sector average.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** peg-ratio, relative-valuation, growth, risk, multiples

## p.42 — III. Price to Book Ratio: Determinants

**Teaches:** Derives the price-to-book (PBV) ratio from the stable-growth dividend discount model by defining ROE = EPS0 / Book Value of Equity. PBV is driven by ROE, payout ratio, growth, and the cost of equity.
**Formulas:**
- P0 = DPS1 / (r - gn), where P0 = value of equity per share today, DPS1 = expected dividends per share next year, r = cost of equity, gn = stable growth rate.
- P0 = BV0 * ROE * Payout Ratio * (1 + gn) / (r - gn), where BV0 = current book value of equity per share, ROE = EPS0 / BV0.
- P0 / BV0 = PBV = ROE * Payout Ratio * (1 + gn) / (r - gn) (using trailing ROE).
- If ROE is based on expected next-period earnings: PBV = ROE * Payout Ratio / (r - gn).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROE, payout, gn, r → PBV)
**Tags:** price-to-book, pbv, dividend-discount-model, roe, multiples

## p.43 — PBV of a Stable Growth Firm: Simplified

**Teaches:** Substituting the fundamental growth equation g = (1 - Payout ratio) * ROE into the PBV formula gives PBV = (ROE - gn) / (r - gn). The PBV of a stable firm is therefore set by the gap between ROE and the required rate of return. A company expected to earn ROE above its cost of equity should trade at PBV above one; below, below one; equal, at one.
**Formulas:**
- g = (1 - Payout ratio) * ROE
- P0 / BV0 = PBV = (ROE - gn) / (r - gn), where ROE = return on equity, gn = stable growth rate, r = cost of equity.
**Rules:** ROE > cost of equity → PBV should be > 1; ROE < cost of equity → PBV < 1; ROE = cost of equity → PBV = 1.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROE, gn, r → PBV)
**Tags:** price-to-book, stable-growth, roe, excess-returns

## p.44 — EV / Book Capital Determinants

**Teaches:** The enterprise-value analogue of PBV. Start from the stable-growth FCFF model and divide by book capital. Then substitute FCFF = EBIT(1-t) - (g/ROC)*EBIT(1-t). The value-to-book-capital ratio reduces to a function of return on capital, growth, and WACC — the firm-level parallel of PBV = (ROE - g)/(r - g).
**Formulas:**
- V0 = FCFF1 / (WACC - g), where V0 = value of operating assets, FCFF1 = expected free cash flow to the firm next year, WACC = cost of capital, g = stable growth.
- V0 / BV = (FCFF1 / BV) / (WACC - g), where BV = book value of invested capital.
- With FCFF = EBIT(1-t) - (g/ROC)*EBIT(1-t): V0 / BV = (ROC - g) / (WACC - g), where ROC = after-tax return on capital, t = tax rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, g, WACC → EV/Book Capital)
**Tags:** ev-to-book, enterprise-value, return-on-capital, wacc, multiples

## p.45 — IV. EV to EBITDA: Determinants

**Teaches:** Derives EV/EBITDA from the stable-growth FCFF model by expanding FCFF into its components (after-tax EBITDA, depreciation tax benefit, cap ex, working-capital change) and dividing through by EBITDA. The determinants of EV/EBITDA are: the cost of capital, the expected growth rate, the tax rate, and the reinvestment rate (or return on capital).
**Formulas:**
- EV0 = FCFF1 / (WACC - g)
- EV = [EBITDA(1-t) + Depr(t) - CEx - ΔWorking Capital] / (WACC - g), where t = tax rate, Depr(t) = depreciation tax savings (depreciation × tax rate), CEx = capital expenditures, ΔWorking Capital = change in non-cash working capital.
- EV/EBITDA = (1-t)/(WACC-g) + [Depr(t)/EBITDA]/(WACC-g) - [CEx/EBITDA]/(WACC-g) - [ΔWC/EBITDA]/(WACC-g)
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (t, WACC, g, Depr/EBITDA, CEx/EBITDA, ΔWC/EBITDA → EV/EBITDA)
**Tags:** ev-ebitda, enterprise-value, fcff, multiples

## p.46 — EV/EBITDA: A Simple Example

**Teaches:** A worked application of the EV/EBITDA determinants formula for a stable-growth firm.
**Example:** Firm with tax rate = 36%, CapEx/EBITDA = 30%, Depreciation/EBITDA = 20%, cost of capital = 10%, no working-capital requirements, stable growth of 5% forever. Value/EBITDA = (1-0.36)/(0.10-0.05) + (0.2)(0.36)/(0.10-0.05) - 0.3/(0.10-0.05) - 0/(0.10-0.05) = 8.24.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (tax rate, CapEx/EBITDA, Depr/EBITDA, WACC, g, ΔWC/EBITDA → EV/EBITDA multiple)
**Tags:** ev-ebitda, worked-example, stable-growth

## p.48 — V. EV/Sales Ratio

**Teaches:** The EV/Sales (value-to-sales) multiple should be paired with pre-tax or after-tax operating margins because both are firm-level (enterprise) measures. Replacing FCFF with EBIT(1 - tax rate)(1 - Reinvestment Rate) in a two-stage FCFF model yields Value/Sales as a function of the after-tax operating margin, growth, reinvestment, and WACC.
**Formulas:**
- Free Cash Flow to the Firm = EBIT(1 - tax rate)(1 - Reinvestment Rate)
- Value/Sales0 = After-tax Operating Margin * { [(1 - RIR_growth)(1+g) * (1 - (1+g)^n/(1+WACC)^n)] / (WACC - g) + [(1 - RIR_stable)(1+g)^n * (1+gn)] / [(WACC - gn)(1+WACC)^n] }
  Symbols: g = growth rate in after-tax operating income for the first n years. gn = stable growth rate after year n forever. RIR_growth, RIR_stable = reinvestment rates in the high-growth and stable periods. WACC = weighted average cost of capital. n = length of the high-growth period.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (margin, g, gn, n, RIRs, WACC → Value/Sales)
**Tags:** ev-sales, price-to-sales, operating-margin, two-stage-model, multiples

## p.49 — The Value of a Brand Name

**Teaches:** A critique of traditional valuation is that it ignores brand names and intangibles, but ad-hoc analyst approaches to brand value can badly over- or understate it. A well-known brand lets a firm charge higher prices for the same products, producing higher margins and hence higher value/sales ratios and firm value; the larger the price premium, the greater the brand value. Brand value can be quantified by comparing the firm's value/sales ratio with that of a generic-product firm.
**Formulas:** Value of brand name = [(V/S)_b - (V/S)_g] * Sales, where (V/S)_b = Value-of-firm/Sales ratio with the benefit of the brand name, (V/S)_g = Value-of-firm/Sales ratio of a firm with a generic product.
**Compute:** MIXED — the brand-value formula is deterministic given the two V/S ratios and sales; choosing the generic comparable and its margin is judgment.
**Tags:** brand-value, intangibles, ev-sales, valuation

## p.51 — The Determinants of Multiples (Summary Map)

**Teaches:** A single map showing that every equity multiple derives from P = Dividends1/(ke - g) = Net Income1(1 - Payout)/(ke - g), and every enterprise-value multiple derives from EV = FCFF1/(WACC - g) = EBIT1(1-t)(1 - RIR)/(WACC - g). Each multiple is a function of a small set of fundamentals.
**Formulas:**
Equity multiples:
- P/Div1 = 1/(ke - g); Div Yield = f(ke, g)
- P/E1 = (1 - Payout)/(ke - g); PE = f(ke, g, Payout)
- P/Book Equity = ROE(1 - Payout)/(ke - g); PBV = f(ROE, ke, g, Payout)
- P/Sales1 = Net Margin(1 - Payout)/(ke - g); PS = f(ROE, ke, g, Payout, Net Margin)
- Variants of Net Income: Net Income1 = Net Margin × Sales = ROE × Book Equity = Net Income0(1+g)

Enterprise multiples:
- EV/FCFF1 = 1/(WACC - g)
- EV/EBIT1(1-t) = (1 - RIR)/(WACC - g)
- EV/EBIT1 = (1-t)(1 - RIR)/(WACC - g)
- EV/Sales1 = ATOM(1 - RIR)/(WACC - g)
- EV/IC = ROIC(1 - RIR)/(WACC - g)
- Variants of EBIT1(1-t): = ATOM × Sales = ROIC × IC = EBITDA1(1-t) + t×DA

Definitions ("cheat boxes"):
- ROE = Net Income1/Book Equity0
- Net Margin = Net Income/Sales
- Payout = Dividends/Net Income
- IC (invested capital) = Book Equity + Debt - Cash
- ATOM (after-tax operating margin) = EBIT(1-t)/Sales
- RIR (reinvestment rate) = (Cap Ex - DA + Chg WC)/EBIT(1-t)
- ROIC = EBIT1(1-t)/IC
- ke = cost of equity; WACC = cost of capital; g = growth; t = tax rate; DA = depreciation & amortization
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (fundamentals → intrinsic value of each multiple)
**Tags:** multiples, determinants, equity-multiples, enterprise-value-multiples, cheat-sheet

## p.52 — Application Tests

**Teaches:** Two tests when applying multiples. (1) What is a "comparable" firm? Traditional analysis assumes same-sector firms are comparable, but valuation theory says a comparable is any firm similar in fundamentals — risk, growth, and cash flow characteristics — even if it is in a very different business. (2) Given comparables, how do you adjust for differences on fundamentals? It is impossible to find an exactly identical firm, so you must control for cross-firm differences.
**Rules:** Define comparables by fundamentals (risk, growth, cash flows), not merely by sector membership; always control for remaining differences.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** comparable-firms, relative-valuation, methodology

## p.53 — The Sampling Choice

**Teaches:** Ideally you want many publicly traded firms "just like" your firm so no controls are needed; in practice firms sharing the same risk, growth, and cash-flow profile are rare or nonexistent. The trade-off is a spectrum: a small sample of firms that are "just like" your firm versus a large sample of firms similar on some dimensions but different on others.
**Rules:** Choose between small/close-match samples (fewer control problems, more sampling noise) and large/loose samples (more data, more need to control for differences).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** sampling, comparable-firms, relative-valuation

## p.54 — The "Control for Differences" Choices

**Teaches:** Four ways to control for cross-firm differences when comparing multiples: (1) Direct comparisons — if comparables are "just like" your firm, compare multiples directly (higher multiple = expensive, lower = cheap). (2) Story telling — if firms vary on one key dimension, tell a story about how value varies on that dimension. Example: "this company trades at 12x earnings vs. the sector's 10x, but it's cheap because its growth is much higher." (3) Modified multiple — modify the multiple to incorporate the dimension of difference (e.g., PEG). (4) Statistical techniques — if firms vary on more than one dimension, use multiple regressions (or variants) to get a "controlled" estimate.
**Rules:** Match the control technique to the number of differing dimensions: none → direct comparison; one → story or modified multiple; several → regression.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** relative-valuation, controls, regression, methodology

## p.77 — Solution 1: Use Proxies for Survival and Growth — Amazon in Early 2000

**Teaches:** When current earnings are negative, replace them with proxies for survival and future growth: revenue growth and cash holdings (with revenue level controlling for size). The resulting regression can price young money-losing firms relative to each other.
**Formulas:** PS = 30.61 - 2.77 × ln(Rev) + 6.42 × (Rev Growth) + 5.11 × (Cash/Rev); t-statistics: ln(Rev) 0.66, Rev Growth 2.63, Cash/Rev 3.49; R squared = 31.8%. Rev = revenues, Rev Growth = revenue growth rate (decimal), Cash/Rev = cash balance divided by revenues.
**Example:** Amazon in early 2000: ln(Rev) = 7.1039, Rev Growth = 1.9946 (≈199%), Cash/Rev = 0.3069. Predicted PS = 30.61 - 2.77(7.1039) + 6.42(1.9946) + 5.11(0.3069) = 30.42. Actual PS = 25.63 → Amazon is undervalued RELATIVE to other internet stocks (which may all be collectively overpriced).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ln revenues, revenue growth, cash/revenues → predicted PS)
**Tags:** price-to-sales, amazon, internet-stocks, survival-proxies, regression

## p.78 — Solution 2: Use Forward Multiples — Watch Out for Bumps in the Road (Tesla)

**Teaches:** A waterfall showing why forward (future-year) multiples must be haircut back to today: Tesla's estimated value in year 10 of $68,271m shrinks step by step to today's value once each risk is applied. The steps: (1) Estimated value in year 10 = $68,271m. (2) Discount 10 years at the riskfree rate of 2.75% → $52,050m. (3) Use the risk-adjusted cost of capital of 10.03% instead → $27,750m. (4) Adjust for dilution from new equity issues needed to finance growth → $12,814m. (5) Adjust for a 10% failure risk → $12,174m. (6) Adjust for debt and cash → equity value $11,797m. (7) Adjust for the overhang of 25.06m options → common equity value $8,152m.
**Rules:** A forward multiple applied to a future-year metric must be adjusted for time value at a risk-adjusted rate, expected dilution, failure risk, net debt, and option overhang before comparing to today's price.
**Example:** Tesla: $68,271m (year-10 value) → $52,050m (riskfree discounting) → $27,750m (10.03% cost of capital) → $12,814m (dilution) → $12,174m (10% failure risk) → $11,797m (net debt & cash) → $8,152m (option overhang).
**Compute:** MIXED — each discounting/adjustment step is deterministic given the assumptions; the assumptions (cost of capital, failure risk, dilution) are judgment.
**Tags:** forward-multiples, tesla, dilution, failure-risk, young-companies
