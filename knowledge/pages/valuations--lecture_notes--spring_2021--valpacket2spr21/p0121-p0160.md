## p.124 — Process of Valuing Private Companies

**Teaches:** Valuing a private company follows the same process as a public company: estimate cash flows, attach a discount rate reflecting the riskiness of those cash flows, and compute present value — either the entire business (FCFF at the cost of capital) or equity (FCFE at the cost of equity). Two standard problems distinguish private firms: there is no market value for either debt or equity, and financial statements go back fewer years, have less detail, and have more holes.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** private-company, dcf, valuation-process

## p.125 — 1. No Market Value?

**Teaches:** The absence of traded prices for a private firm's debt and equity creates three problems. (1) Market values as inputs are unavailable — you cannot compute debt ratios for levering betas or cost-of-capital weights, nor market prices for valuing employee options/warrants. (2) Market value as output — for public firms the market price serves as a reasonableness check, but a private company's estimated value stands alone. (3) Market-price-based risk measures such as beta and bond ratings are unavailable.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

## p.129 — I. Private-to-Private Transaction: Three Key Issues

**Teaches:** In a private-to-private sale, three issues must be confronted. (1) Neither the buyer nor the seller is diversified, so risk-return models that price only non-diversifiable risk seriously underestimate the discount rate. (2) The investment is illiquid, so the buyer must factor in an "illiquidity discount." (3) Key person value: revenues and operating profit may reflect the presence of the current owner, not just the business's potential.

## p.132 — Step 1: Estimating Discount Rates (why standard models fail)

## p.135 — Estimating a Total Beta

**Teaches:** To go from market beta to total beta you need the proportion of the firm's risk that is market risk. The R-squared of the comparable firms' beta regressions provides it: the average R-squared across high-end retailer regressions is 25%. Because betas are based on standard deviations (not variances), use the correlation coefficient — the square root of R-squared — as the proportion of risk that is market risk.

**Formulas:** Total Unlevered Beta = Market Beta / Correlation with the market, where Correlation = sqrt(R-squared of the comparable regressions). Example: 1.18 / 0.5 = 2.36.

**Example:** High-end retailers: market unlevered beta 1.18, avg R² 25% → correlation 0.5 → total unlevered beta = 1.18/0.5 = 2.36.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs (market beta, R²) → total beta; choosing the comparable set is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** total-beta, r-squared, correlation, private-company

## p.136 — Debt-to-Equity Ratio and Cost of Equity

**Teaches:** Private firms have no market D/E ratio to re-lever the beta. There are two alternatives. Either assume the firm's D/E equals the industry-average market D/E of publicly traded firms in the sector. Or use your own estimates of debt and equity values as weights. The second creates a circular reasoning problem: you need the cost of capital to get values, and values to get the cost of capital. The example assumes the restaurant's D/E equals the average publicly traded restaurant's (14.33%), even though retailers were used for the unlevered beta.

**Formulas:** Levered beta = Unlevered total beta x (1 + (1 − tax rate) x (D/E)) = 2.36 x (1 + (1 − 0.4)(0.1433)) = 2.56. Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Levered beta x Equity risk premium = 4.25% + 2.56 x (4%) = 14.50%. (T-bond rate 4.25% at the time; ERP 4%.)

**Example:** Restaurant: total unlevered beta 2.36, D/E 14.33%, tax 40% → levered beta 2.56; cost of equity 14.50%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — (unlevered beta, D/E, tax rate, riskfree, ERP) → levered beta and cost of equity; D/E assumption is JUDGMENT.

## p.137 — Estimating a Cost of Debt and Cost of Capital

**Teaches:** With no rating or recent bank loans, estimate a synthetic rating from the interest coverage ratio, treating lease expenses as interest. The coverage ratio maps to a rating and default spread, giving a pre-tax and after-tax cost of debt; the cost of capital uses the same industry-average debt ratio used to lever the beta (note: D/E of 14.33% converts to debt-to-capital weights of 14.33/114.33 and 100/114.33).

**Formulas:** Coverage Ratio = Operating Income / Interest (Lease) Expense = 400,000/120,000 = 3.33. After-tax Cost of debt = (Riskfree rate + Default spread) x (1 − tax rate) = (4.25% + 3.25%) x (1 − 0.40) = 4.50%. Cost of capital = Cost of equity x (E/(D+E)) + After-tax cost of debt x (D/(D+E)) = 14.50% x (100/114.33) + 4.50% x (14.33/114.33) = 13.25%.

**Rules:** Coverage ratio 3.33 → synthetic rating BB+ → default spread 3.25% (from Damodaran's synthetic rating lookup).

**Example:** Restaurant: coverage 3.33 → BB+ → spread 3.25%; after-tax cost of debt 4.50%; cost of capital 13.25%.

## p.142 — Step 6: Consider the Effect of Illiquidity

**Teaches:** Practice reduces illiquidity to a rule of thumb: a fixed 20-30% discount for all private firms. But the discount should vary on three dimensions. Companies: healthier, larger firms with more liquid assets deserve smaller discounts than money-losing, smaller firms with illiquid assets. Time: liquidity is worth more in bad economies with tight credit than in booming markets. Buyers: liquidity is worth more to buyers with short horizons and high cash needs than to long-term investors who can hold.

**Rules:** Conventional rule of thumb: illiquidity discount = 20-30% of value, invariant across firms — a rule the slide criticizes.

## p.143 — Standard Approach: Illiquidity Discount from Illiquid Publicly Traded Assets

**Teaches:** The standard way to estimate illiquidity discounts uses two classes of illiquid but observable assets. Restricted stock: shares issued by public companies bypassing SEC registration that cannot be traded for one year after issue. Pre-IPO transactions: trades among equity investors in a private firm prior to its IPO. In both cases the discount is the difference between the market price of the liquid asset and the observed transaction price of the illiquid asset.

**Formulas:** Discount(restricted stock) = Stock price − Price on restricted stock offering. Discount(IPO) = IPO offering price − Price on pre-IPO transaction.

## p.144 — The Restricted Stock Discount (Silber regression)

**Teaches:** Aggregate restricted-stock studies: Maher (four mutual funds, 1969-73) found an average discount of 35.43%; Moroney reported a 35% mean discount across 146 restricted stock issues bought by 10 investment companies (1970 data); Silber (1991), studying 1980s offerings, finds a median discount of 33.75%. Silber then related the discount's size to offering characteristics via a regression.

**Formulas:** LN(RPRS) = 4.33 + 0.036 LN(REV) − 0.142 LN(RBRT) + 0.174 DERN + 0.332 DCUST. Symbols: RPRS = relative price of restricted stock (to publicly traded stock). REV = revenues of the private firm ($ millions). RBRT = restricted block relative to total common stock, in %. DERN = 1 if earnings are positive, 0 if negative. DCUST = 1 if there is a customer relationship with the investor, 0 otherwise.

**Data:** Study results: Maher 35.43% average discount; Moroney 35% mean; Silber (1991) 33.75% median.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — (REV, RBRT, DERN, DCUST) → RPRS → implied discount = 1 − RPRS.

**Tags:** restricted-stock, illiquidity-discount, silber-regression, empirical-studies

## p.147 — The "Sampling" Problem

**Teaches:** Both restricted-stock and pre-IPO studies suffer significant sampling bias: firms making restricted stock offerings tend to be small, troubled firms out of conventional financing options, and IPOs where insiders sell at huge discounts in the five months before listing tend to have significant pricing uncertainty. For restricted stock, comparing discounts on all private placements with discounts on restricted stock offerings suggests illiquidity alone accounts for a discount of less than 10%, leaving the remaining 20-25% explained by sampling problems.

**Rules:** Treat headline 30-35% restricted-stock discounts skeptically; the pure illiquidity component may be under 10%.

## p.148 — Alternative Approach: Bid-Ask Spread Regression (Whole Sample)

**Teaches:** All traded assets are somewhat illiquid; the bid-ask spread (difference between buy and sell price at the same instant) is the illiquidity measure. Regressing the spread (as a percent of price) on variables measurable for both public and private firms — revenues, an earnings dummy, cash as a percent of firm value, trading volume — lets you plug in a private firm's values (with zero trading volume) to estimate its implied spread, i.e., its illiquidity discount.

**Formulas:** Spread = 0.145 − 0.0022 x ln(Annual Revenues) − 0.015 x DERN − 0.016 x (Cash/Firm Value) − 0.11 x ($ Monthly trading volume / Firm Value), where DERN = 1 if earnings positive, 0 if negative; regression estimated on data from end of 2000; revenues in $ millions.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — (revenues, earnings dummy, cash/firm value, trading volume=0) → estimated spread/discount.

**Tags:** bid-ask-spread, illiquidity-discount, regression, private-company

## p.150 — II. Private Company Sold to Publicly Traded Company

**Teaches:** When a private company is sold to a public buyer, the seller is undiversified but the buyer (or its investors) is diversified, so they see different amounts of risk in the same firm — the seller sees more risk than the buyer, implying different discount rates. Cash flows can also differ because public companies' tax rates can diverge from private owners'. And there should be no illiquidity discount for a public buyer, since its investors can sell their holdings in a market.

**Rules:** For a public (diversified) buyer: use market beta not total beta, use the buyer's tax rate, and apply no illiquidity discount.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

## p.157 — A. Use of the Proceeds

**Teaches:** IPO proceeds can be taken out by existing owners, used to pay down debt and other obligations, or held as cash for future reinvestment needs. Treatment depends on the use.
