## p.161 — C. The Investment Banking guarantee

**Teaches:** Almost all IPOs are managed by investment banks and are backed by a pricing guarantee. The investment banker guarantees the offering price to the issuer. If the actual issuance price falls below the guaranteed price, the banker must buy the shares at the guaranteed price and may bear the loss. This guarantee pushes bankers to set the offering price low (below fair value), since underpricing shifts risk away from them.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (institutional/conceptual description, no computation).
**Tags:** ipo, investment-banking, underwriting, pricing-guarantee

## p.164 — An investment opportunity?

**Teaches:** Discussion question: investment banks underprice IPOs by roughly 10-15% on average. What investor strategy would exploit this — say, subscribe to every IPO and flip on day one? And why might it not work? The implicit answer is that share allocation is rationed. You get full allotments of the overpriced IPOs and are rationed on the underpriced ones (winner's curse). So the realized return to a passive IPO-subscription strategy is far lower than the headline underpricing.
**Rules:** Average IPO underpricing assumed at 10-15%; allocation bias (winner's curse) undermines strategies built on it.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (strategy reasoning question).
**Tags:** ipo, underpricing, winners-curse, investment-strategy

## p.165 — D. The offering quantity

**Teaches:** Underpricing costs the owner only on the shares actually sold in the offering. Suppose you offer 100% of Twitter, investors would pay $20 billion, and the issue is underpriced 15%. You lose 15% of $20B = $3 billion. Now suppose you offer only 10% and plan to sell most of your remaining stake over the next two years. The direct underpricing loss applies only to the 10% sold ($300M). The first-day pop may even raise the price at which the later stakes are sold. So owners may rationally tolerate, or even welcome, underpricing on a small initial float.
**Example:** Twitter thought experiment: investors willing to pay $20 billion for the common stock; 15% underpricing. Selling 100%: wealth loss = 0.15 x $20B = $3B. Selling 10%: direct loss = 0.15 x $2B = $0.3B, with possible offsetting gains on the retained 90%.
**Compute:** MIXED — loss = underpricing % x proceeds sold is deterministic; the effect on the retained stake's value is judgment.
**Tags:** ipo, offering-quantity, underpricing-cost, float

## p.166 — Alternatives to IPOs

**Teaches:** The traditional bank-intermediated IPO model has come under assault for two reasons. First, banking failures on the services (pricing and selling) offered in return for their fees. Second, loss of the credibility effect of the banking guarantee, as bankers' public standing has fallen. Two alternatives have emerged. In a direct listing, the company lists its shares directly on the exchange and lets demand and supply set the price — no banker-set offer price. In a SPAC, a publicly traded entity collects money for a future, unspecified IPO. It then uses the funds to buy out a private company, which takes on the public company's standing.
**Rules:** Direct listing = market sets price, no underwriting guarantee; SPAC = blank-check public shell acquires a private company to take it public.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (institutional description).
**Tags:** ipo, direct-listing, spac, going-public

## p.167 — Private to VC to Public: cost of equity by stage

**Teaches:** The cost of equity for the same business changes as ownership shifts from an undiversified private owner, to a partially diversified venture capitalist, to diversified public market investors. The beta that matters is the total beta: market beta divided by the owner's correlation with the market. Setup: sector market beta = 1, average firm-market correlation = 0.25, riskfree rate = 4%, equity risk premium (ERP) = 5%.
**Formulas:** Perceived (total) beta = Market beta / Correlation of owner's portfolio with the market. Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Perceived beta x ERP, where Riskfree rate = 4% and ERP = 5% here.
**Example:** Stage 1 — nascent business, fully invested private owner (correlation 0.25): Perceived beta = 1/0.25 = 4; Cost of equity = 4% + 4(5%) = 24%. Stage 2 — angel/VC financing by a specialized venture capitalist holding multiple high-tech investments (portfolio correlation with market 0.5): Perceived beta = 1/0.5 = 2; Cost of equity = 4% + 2(5%) = 14%. Stage 3 — public offering, diversified retail and institutional investors: Perceived beta = 1; Cost of equity = 4% + 1(5%) = 9%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: market beta, correlation per stage, riskfree rate, ERP → outputs: total beta and cost of equity per stage).
**Tags:** total-beta, cost-of-equity, private-company, venture-capital, diversification

## p.168 — To value this company (stage-varying costs of equity)

**Teaches:** Values the private business from p.167 with staged ownership. The current private owner holds it fully for years 1-2 (cost of equity 24%). A technology VC owns it from the start of year 3 through year 5 (14%). It goes public, or is sold to a public firm, at the end of year 5 (9% thereafter). Cash flows are discounted using cumulated costs of equity that change with the owner at each stage. A single cost of equity throughout is wrong: 24% forever undervalues the firm; 9% forever overvalues it.
**Formulas:** Terminal value at end of year 5 = Terminal-year cash flow / (post-transition cost of equity - stable growth rate) = 175 / (0.09 - 0.02) = $2,500. Cumulated COE(t) = product over years 1..t of (1 + cost of equity in that year); PV of year-t cash flow = CF(t) / Cumulated COE(t). Value of firm = sum of PVs of yearly cash flows plus PV of terminal value.

## p.169 — Implications (of the private-to-public transition)

**Teaches:** Proposition 1: a private business expected to transition to a publicly traded company is worth more than an otherwise similar private business that does not expect this transition. The reason: later cash flows get discounted at the lower, diversified-investor cost of equity. Corollaries: private businesses in sectors that are "hot" for going public (e.g., social media in 2014) are worth more than those in less sexy sectors. As IPOs boom (bust), private company valuations increase (decrease). Private companies in countries with easy access to public markets are worth more than those without such access. Proposition 2: a private business that expects to go public sooner is worth more than an otherwise similar company that will take longer. So private businesses are worth more when companies can go public earlier in their life cycle.
**Rules:** Expected transition to public ownership raises private company value; sooner transition → higher value; IPO market conditions and public-market access feed directly into private company valuations.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative propositions).
**Tags:** private-company, ipo-market, valuation-propositions, market-access

## p.170 — Private company valuation: Closing thoughts

**Teaches:** The value of a private business depends on the potential buyer. A seller maximizes value by selling to a long-term investor who is well diversified (or whose own investors are). The buyer should also not think too highly of the seller personally, so value is not discounted for the owner's departure (minimal key-person attachment). When valuing a private business for legal purposes (tax or divorce court), the assumptions and the resulting value depend on which side of the legal divide you are on. Final proposition: the owner of a private business always has the option of investing wealth in publicly traded stocks. So there has to be a relationship between what those investments can earn and the return demanded on the private business.
**Rules:** Value is buyer-specific; to maximize sale value seek a long-term, well-diversified buyer; legal-context valuations are advocacy-driven; public-market returns anchor the required return on a private business.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (closing principles).
**Tags:** private-company, buyer-specific-value, key-person, closing-thoughts
