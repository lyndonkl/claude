## p.281 — From DCF value to target price and returns (Con Ed exercise)

**Teaches:** A DCF value today can be rolled forward into a target price a year from now: if the value estimate, cost of equity, and next-year dividend are all fair, the expected price in one year is today's intrinsic value grown at the cost of equity minus the dividend paid out. It also shows how to compute the expected return from buying at today's market price if the market corrects its mistake (price converges to value): expected return = (expected price in 1 year + dividend − purchase price) / purchase price. Inputs given: Con Ed DCF value = $42.30 per share, cost of equity = 7.70%, expected dividend next year = 2.32 × 1.021 (current dividend of $2.32 growing at 2.1%), current market price = $40.76.
**Formulas:** Expected price in 1 year = Value today × (1 + cost of equity) − expected dividend = 42.30 × 1.077 − (2.32 × 1.021). Expected annual return if market corrects = (Expected price₁ + Dividend₁ − Price paid today) / Price paid today, with Price paid = $40.76.
**Example:** Con Ed: value $42.30, cost of equity 7.70%, dividend 2.32 × 1.021 = $2.369, bought at $40.76 (left as an in-class exercise; numbers fully specified).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs (value, cost of equity, dividend, market price) → expected target price and expected 1-year return.

## p.286 — Value drivers for the index

**Teaches:** Framework diagram for intrinsic valuation of the S&P 500: base-year collective earnings → expected earnings growth over next 5 years → expected earnings; multiplied by % of earnings returned as dividends & buybacks → expected cash flows E(CF); discounted at required return r = riskfree rate (10-yr T-bond) + equity risk premium (market-set, reflecting collective risk aversion). After year 5, earnings and cash flows grow at the economy's rate forever. Crisis questions attached to each driver: how will 2020–21 earnings be hit, how much dissipates by 2024, how much will dividends/buybacks fall, how will the riskfree rate respond to flight to safety and central banks, how will risk aversion (ERP) change.
**Formulas:** Value of index = E(CF₁)/(1+r) + E(CF₂)/(1+r)² + E(CF₃)/(1+r)³ + E(CF₄)/(1+r)⁴ + E(CF₅)/(1+r)⁵ + E(CF₆)/[(r − 0.0245)(1+r)⁵], where E(CF_t) = expected aggregate dividends + buybacks on the index in year t, r = required return on stocks (riskfree rate + ERP), and 0.0245 (2.45%) is the perpetual growth rate (set to the riskfree rate at the time).
**Compute:** MIXED — the discounting is deterministic (cash flows, r, g → index value); estimating earnings paths, payout, and ERP is judgment.

## p.294 — The fundamental determinants of value

**Teaches:** Every valuation, easy or hard, answers four questions: (1) What are the cash flows from existing assets? (equity: cash flows after debt payments; firm: cash flows before debt payments). (2) What is the value added by growth assets? (equity: growth in equity earnings/cash flows; firm: growth in operating earnings/cash flows). (3) How risky are the cash flows from both existing and growth assets? (equity: risk in the company's equity; firm: risk in the firm's operations). (4) When will the firm become a mature firm, and what are the potential roadblocks? This four-question framework is reused throughout the dark-side sections to diagnose why each company type is hard to value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** valuation-framework, value-drivers, cash-flows, growth, risk

## p.295 — The Dark Side of Valuation: where the challenges lie

**Teaches:** Valuing stable, money-making companies with clean accounting, long histories, and many comparables is easy. The true test of valuation skill is "difficult" companies, and the challenges are greatest when valuing: (1) young companies early in the life cycle in young businesses, (2) companies that don't fit the accounting mold, and (3) companies facing substantial truncation risk (default or nationalization risk).
**Rules:** Difficulty classification: young/early-life-cycle firms, non-standard-accounting firms, and truncation-risk (default/nationalization) firms are the hardest to value.

## p.296 — Difficult-to-value companies: a taxonomy

**Teaches:** Where valuation difficulty comes from. Across the life cycle: young growth firms (limited history, small revenues with big operating losses, high failure propensity); mature companies in transition (history must be abandoned, parameters re-estimated); declining/distressed firms (long but irrelevant history, declining markets, high debt loads, likelihood of distress). Across markets: emerging-market companies (structure, country-risk exposure, poor corporate governance). Across sectors: financial service firms (opaque statements, hard-to-estimate basic inputs — you end up trusting managers); commodity and cyclical firms (valuations hostage to commodity prices / macro cycle); firms with intangible assets (accounting principles break down).
**Rules:** Difficulty taxonomy — life cycle (young growth / mature transition / declining-distressed), markets (emerging), sectors (financial services / commodity-cyclical / intangible-heavy).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** difficult-companies, life-cycle, emerging-markets, financial-firms, intangibles

## p.297 — I. The challenge with young companies

**Teaches:** Applies the four fundamental questions to young companies. Existing assets: cash flows are non-existent or negative. Growth assets: judgments on revenues/profits are difficult because there is no history and possibly no product/service to gauge market potential or profitability — the entire value lies in future growth with little to base estimates on. Risk: limited historical earnings data and no market prices for securities make risk hard to assess. Maturity: whether the firm survives the gauntlet of market demand and competition is uncertain, and even if it does, timing maturity is difficult. Additionally, different claims on cash flows (preferred rounds, etc.) can affect the value of equity at each stage.

## p.298 — Upping the ante: young companies in young businesses

**Teaches:** Valuation normally draws on three information sources: (1) the firm's current financial statements (how much did it sell, earn?); (2) the firm's financial history (revenue/earnings growth over time, cost structure, macro susceptibility); (3) industry and comparable-firm data (what happens to margins, revenue growth, reinvestment needs, and risk as firms mature). Young companies in young businesses lack all three, and that's when analysts are tempted by the dark side — "paradigm shifts" get declared, new metrics get invented, and the story dominates while the numbers lag.
**Rules:** Warning signs of dark-side valuation: claims of paradigm shifts, invented metrics, story dominating the numbers.

## p.300 — Lesson 1: Don't sweat the small stuff (discount rates for young firms)

**Teaches:** For young companies, don't obsess over discount-rate precision — the regression beta is useless (Amazon's 2-year weekly regression beta against the S&P 500 was raw 2.23 / adjusted 1.82 with R² of only 0.17 and standard error 0.50). Instead: (1) spotlight the business the company is in and use the bottom-up beta of that business; (2) don't try to incorporate failure risk into the discount rate; (3) let the cost of capital change over time as the company matures; (4) if desperate, use the cross-section of costs of capital across all companies and take the 90th or 95th percentile as the young firm's cost of capital.
**Rules:** Use bottom-up sector beta, not regression beta; failure risk goes in cash flows/value, not the discount rate; cost of capital should decline toward mature levels over the forecast; fallback = 90th–95th percentile of the cross-sectional distribution of costs of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (rules for setting discount rates).
**Tags:** young-companies, discount-rate, bottom-up-beta, cost-of-capital, regression-beta

## p.304 — Lesson 5: The dilution is taken care of

**Teaches:** Young growth companies' share counts will rise for two reasons: new equity issues to fund growth or acquisitions, and employee options getting exercised. A properly built DCF already captures both effects, even though it divides by the current share count. (1) Future new-equity needs show up as negative FCFF in the early years. The present value of those negative cash flows drags down equity value today — that IS the dilution effect. In the Amazon valuation, the PV of negative FCFF in the first 6 years cut equity value by $3.09 billion, about a 16% reduction. (2) Existing options are valued as options and netted against equity value, handling the overhang. Future option grants are handled by forecasting earnings after stock-based compensation expense — don't fall for the "it's not a cash expense" ploy.
**Rules:** Do not add a separate dilution adjustment on top of a DCF that (a) includes negative early FCFF, (b) nets out the option value of existing options, and (c) treats SBC as a real expense.
**Example:** Amazon: PV of negative FCFF in first 6 years = $3.09B, ~16% reduction in equity value = the dilution effect.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual); the netting itself is deterministic once option values are computed.
**Tags:** dilution, share-count, employee-options, stock-based-compensation, young-companies

## p.314 — The perils of valuing mature companies

## p.315 — Hormel Foods: the value of control changing

**Teaches:** Values Hormel Foods (2008 after-tax operating income $315M, 5% compounded growth over prior 5 years) two ways to quantify the value of control. Status quo (existing management, conservative policies): reinvestment rate 19.14%, ROC 14.34% → expected growth = 14.34% × 19.14% = 2.75% for 3 years; debt ratio 10.4% → cost of capital 6.79% (= 7.33% × (1−0.104) + 3.60% × (1−0.40) × 0.104); beyond year 3: growth 2.35%, ROC 7.23%, reinvestment 32.52%, cost of capital 7.23%. Value of operating assets $4,682; +cash 155 −debt 491 −management options 53 → equity $4,293; value per share $31.91. Optimal (new and better management): (1) Operating restructuring — reinvestment rate raised to 40%, ROC 14.00%, growth = 14.00% × 40% = 5.60% for 5 years; (2) Financial restructuring — debt ratio raised to 20%: cost of capital = 7.75% × (1−0.20) + 3.60% × (1−0.40) × 0.20 = 6.63% (cost of equity rises but cost of capital drops). Beyond year 5: growth 2.35%, ROC 6.74%, reinvestment 34.87%, cost of capital 6.74%. Value of operating assets $5,475 → equity $5,085; value per share $37.80. ## p.319 — III. Dealing with decline and distress

**Teaches:** Applies the four fundamental questions to declining/distressed firms. Existing assets: historical data often shows flat or declining revenues, falling margins, and investments earning less than the cost of capital. Growth assets: growth can be negative as the firm sheds assets and shrinks; as less-profitable assets are shed, the remaining assets may improve in quality. ## p.320 — a. Dealing with decline

**Teaches:** In decline, firms see declining revenues and lower margins, translating into negative expected growth over time. If run by good managers, they will not fight the decline: they adapt — shutting down or selling investments that do not earn the cost of capital — which can mean negative net capital expenditures (depreciation exceeding capex), declining working capital, and an overall negative reinvestment rate. The best case: the firm sheds bad assets, becomes a much smaller and healthier firm, and settles into long-term stable growth. The investor's worst case: managers in denial who keep expanding via bad investments (returns below cost of capital) — such firms may grow revenues and operating income but destroy value along the way.
**Rules:** Good management in decline = divest sub-cost-of-capital assets, accept negative reinvestment; growing revenues/earnings while earning below the cost of capital destroys value.
