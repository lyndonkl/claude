## p.321 — JC Penney valuation: a declining business with distress risk

**Teaches:** Figure 14.5 values JC Penney as a declining business: revenues are expected to drop 3% a year for the next 5 years, then the decline moderates (-2%, -1%, 0%, +1%, +2% in years 6-10). Operating margins improve gradually from 1.32% (base year) to the US retail-sector median of 6.25% by year 10. As stores shut down, cash is released from real estate, which shows up as negative reinvestment in early years. The cost of capital is 9% (high because of a high cost of debt) declining to 8% by year 10. Because the high debt load and poor earnings put survival at risk, the DCF value of operating assets is blended with a distress scenario: based on the bond rating, a 20% chance of failure is assumed, with liquidation bringing in 50% of book value.
**Formulas:** Value of operating assets = (Sum of PV of FCFF over 10 years + PV of terminal value) x (1 - Probability of failure) + Proceeds if firm fails x Probability of failure. Here: $4,841 x 0.80 + $2,421 x 0.20 = $4,357 million.

## p.322 — Dealing with the downside of distress

**Teaches:** A DCF valuation values a firm as a going concern. If there is a significant likelihood of the firm failing before it reaches stable growth, and its assets would then be sold for less than the present value of expected cash flows (a distress sale value), a DCF valuation will overstate firm value. The fix is to compute an expected value across the going-concern and distress scenarios. The distress sale value of equity is usually best estimated as a percent of book value, and that percent will be lower if the economy is doing badly and other firms in the same business are also in distress.
**Formulas:** Value of Equity = DCF value of equity x (1 - Probability of distress) + Distress sale value of equity x (Probability of distress).
**Rules:** Three ways to estimate the probability of distress: (1) use the bond rating to estimate the cumulative probability of distress over 10 years; (2) estimate it with a probit; (3) back it out of the market value of the firm's bonds.
**Compute:** MIXED — the expected-value blend is deterministic given probability and distress proceeds; estimating those inputs is judgment.
**Tags:** distress, probability-of-default, dcf, equity-value

## p.324 — Adjusting the value of LVS for distress

**Teaches:** LVS was rated B+ by S&P in February 2009; historically 28.25% of B+ rated bonds default within 10 years. A better market-based estimate backs the probability of distress out of a traded bond price: LVS had a 6.375% coupon bond maturing in 7 years trading at $529 (per $1000 face). Discounting the survival-weighted expected cash flows at the riskfree rate and solving gives an annual distress probability of 13.54%, implying a cumulative 10-year probability of distress of 76.66%. In distress, expected sale proceeds ($2,769m) are less than the face value of debt, so equity gets zero. Blending gives an expected value per share of $1.92 — much closer to the $4.25 market price than the going-concern $8.12.
**Formulas:** Bond-price equation: 529 = Sum over t=1..7 of [63.75 x (1 - Pi_distress)^t / (1.03)^t] + 1000 x (1 - Pi_distress)^7 / (1.03)^7, where 63.75 is the annual coupon (6.375% of 1000), 3% is the riskfree rate, and Pi_distress is the annual probability of distress. Solution: Pi_distress = 13.54%/year. Cumulative probability of surviving 10 years = (1 - .1354)^10 = 23.34%; cumulative probability of distress over 10 years = 1 - .2334 = 76.66%. Expected value per share = DCF value x (1 - cumulative distress prob) + distress equity value x cumulative distress prob = $8.12 x (1 - .7666) + $0.00 x .7666 = $1.92.
**Example:** LVS Feb 2009 as above; expected distress sale proceeds $2,769m < face value of debt, so equity value per share in distress = $0.00; blended value $1.92.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (bond price, coupon, maturity, riskfree rate → distress probability; then DCF value + distress value → expected value per share).
**Tags:** distress, bond-implied-default-probability, las-vegas-sands, expected-value

## p.327 — Lesson 1: Incorporate country risk with a scalpel, not a bludgeon

**Teaches:** Emerging market companies are exposed to additional country risk because they are incorporated in countries with more political and economic risk — but not all emerging-market companies are equally exposed, and many developed-market companies have emerging-market exposure through their operations. So country risk should be assigned by exposure, not by country of incorporation.
**Rules:** Two ways to capture country risk exposure: (1) the "weighted country risk premium," with weights reflecting the countries revenues come from; (2) the lambda approach, which may incorporate more than revenues (e.g. production locations, risk-management).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** country-risk, emerging-markets, lambda, risk-premium

## p.328 — Lesson 2: Currency should not matter

**Teaches:** Any company can be valued in any currency (a Brazilian company in nominal reais, US dollars, or Swiss francs). For the valuation to stay invariant and consistent, cash flows and discount rates must be in the same currency: a high-inflation currency raises both growth rates and discount rates. For cash flows to be consistent, expected exchange rates must reflect purchasing power parity — the higher-inflation currency has to depreciate by the inflation differential each year.
**Rules:** Match cash-flow currency to discount-rate currency; convert with PPP-consistent expected exchange rates (annual depreciation = inflation differential).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (consistency principle), with the PPP exchange-rate adjustment itself DETERMINISTIC given inflation rates.
**Tags:** currency, purchasing-power-parity, inflation, discount-rate-consistency

## p.340 — Aramco: adjusting for regime change

**Teaches:** Political/truncation risk applied to Aramco as probability-weighted scenarios. If there is no chance of regime change, expected equity value stays $1.65 trillion. ## p.341 — Section: Valuing financial service companies — estimation issues

## p.345 — Lesson 2: For financial service companies, book value matters

**Teaches:** Book value of assets and equity is mostly irrelevant for non-financial companies — it is a historical figure and can be nonsensical (book equity is negative for more than 1,000 publicly traded US companies). For financial service firms it is relevant for two reasons: (1) because they mark to market, book value more likely reflects what the firm owns right now rather than a historical value; (2) regulatory capital ratios are based on book equity, so a bank with negative or even low book equity will be shut down by regulators. Consequently, reinvestment for a bank can be defined as the amount it must add to book equity to sustain its growth ambitions and safety requirements.
**Formulas:** FCFE (bank) = Net Income - Reinvestment in regulatory capital (book equity).
**Rules:** For banks, track book equity and regulatory capital ratios; a bank with negative/low book equity gets shut down by regulators.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework); the FCFE identity is deterministic given net income and required capital additions.
**Tags:** banks, book-value, regulatory-capital, fcfe

## p.348 — Lesson: Accounting rules are cluttered with inconsistencies

**Teaches:** By accounting first principles, capital expenditures create benefits over many periods, so they should not reduce operating income in the period made but be depreciated/amortized over their life and appear as balance-sheet assets. Accounting is consistent for manufacturing firms but inconsistent for firms that do not fit the mold: for pharmaceutical and technology firms, R&D is the ultimate capex but is expensed; for consulting and other human-capital firms, recruiting and training expenses are the long-term investments but are expensed; for brand-name consumer product companies, a portion of advertising builds the brand and is the real capital expenditure but is expensed.
**Rules:** Reclassify as capex: R&D (pharma/tech), recruiting & training (human-capital firms), brand-building portion of advertising (consumer products).

## p.353 — Lesson 1: Don't get lost in macro assumptions

**Teaches:** With cyclical and commodity ("macro") companies, the value you arrive at is unavoidably affected by your views on the economy or the commodity price. Building your own macro forecasts into the valuation creates a value jointly driven by your macro views and your company views, which are then impossible to separate. The best (though not easiest) practice: separate macro from micro — use current market-based numbers (e.g. today's commodity price or futures) in the valuation, and provide a separate assessment of what you think about those market numbers.
**Rules:** Use current market-based macro inputs in the valuation; state macro disagreements separately rather than embedding them.

## p.354 — Lesson 2: Use probabilistic tools for macro-driven value

**Teaches:** If a key macro variable affecting your company's value is uncertain (and it always is), quantify the uncertainty as a distribution rather than a single price and use that distribution in the valuation. A simulation gives you everything a standard valuation gives (an estimated value) plus additional output — the variation in that value and the likelihood that the firm is under- or over-valued.

## p.359 — Are you valuing or pricing?

**Teaches:** Value and price are different processes with different tools and drivers. Intrinsic value is the value of cash flows adjusted for time and risk; its drivers are cash flows from existing assets, growth in cash flows, and quality of growth; its tools are DCF valuation, intrinsic multiples, book-value-based approaches, and excess-return models. Price is set by demand and supply; its drivers are market moods & momentum and surface stories about fundamentals; its tools are multiples and comparables, charting and technical indicators, and "pseudo DCF." Between them sits THE GAP — is there one, and will it close? — driven by information, liquidity, and corporate governance, and analyzed with behavioral finance and price catalysts.
**Rules:** Classify any analysis: valuing (intrinsic tools/drivers) vs pricing (relative tools/drivers); an investment thesis based on the gap requires a reason the gap will close (catalyst).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** value-vs-price, intrinsic-value, pricing, behavioral-finance
