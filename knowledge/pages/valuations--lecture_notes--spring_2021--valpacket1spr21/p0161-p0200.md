## p.162 — Issues in estimating historical growth

**Teaches:** Historical growth rates can be estimated in several ways — arithmetic versus geometric averages, and simple averages versus regression models — and the resulting numbers can differ substantially depending on the method. Historical growth estimates are sensitive to the estimation period chosen (starting and ending points) and to the metric in which growth is measured (revenues vs EBITDA vs EBIT vs EPS). Two practical problems must be dealt with when using historical growth: how to handle negative earnings (growth rates become meaningless), and the effects of scaling up (growth rates decline as firms become larger).

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing method, period, and metric requires reasoning; the averages themselves are deterministic once chosen)
**Tags:** historical-growth, growth-estimation, arithmetic-vs-geometric, negative-earnings

## p.163 — Motorola: arithmetic vs geometric growth rates

**Teaches:** Using Motorola 1994–1999 data, the page shows that arithmetic and geometric average growth rates can diverge dramatically, and the divergence widens with volatility of the series. For revenues (low volatility) arithmetic (7.08%) and geometric (6.82%) averages are close; for EBIT (highly volatile, standard deviation 141.78%) the arithmetic average is 42.45% while the geometric average is only 4.31%. The more volatile the earnings measure, the less meaningful the arithmetic average; the geometric average better captures compounded actual growth.

**Formulas:** Arithmetic average growth = simple mean of the yearly % changes. Geometric average growth = (Ending value / Beginning value)^(1/n) − 1, where n = number of years of growth (here 5).

**Data:**

| Year | Revenues ($) | % Change | EBITDA ($) | % Change | EBIT ($) | % Change |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1994 | 22,245 | — | 4,151 | — | 2,604 | — |
| 1995 | 27,037 | 21.54% | 4,850 | 16.84% | 2,931 | 12.56% |
| 1996 | 27,973 | 3.46% | 4,268 | -12.00% | 1,960 | -33.13% |
| 1997 | 29,794 | 6.51% | 4,276 | 0.19% | 1,947 | -0.66% |
| 1998 | 29,398 | -1.33% | 3,019 | -29.40% | 822 | -57.78% |
| 1999 | 30,931 | 5.21% | 5,398 | 78.80% | 3,216 | 291.24% |

## p.164 — A Test: growth rate with negative starting earnings

**Teaches:** A multiple-choice quiz: Time Warner EPS was −$0.05 in 1996 and expected +$0.25 in 1997; what is the growth rate? Options: (a) −600%, (b) +600%, (c) +120%, (d) cannot be estimated. The point (answered on the next page) is that when the base-year earnings are negative, a conventionally computed growth rate is meaningless — the mechanical calculation (0.30/−0.05) gives −600%, an absurd sign for an improvement.

**Example:** Time Warner: EPS 1996 = −$0.05, expected EPS 1997 = $0.25. Change = $0.30. Conventional growth = 0.30/(−0.05) = −600%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC arithmetic, but JUDGMENT that the result is meaningless (MIXED)
**Tags:** negative-earnings, growth-estimation, quiz, time-warner

## p.165 — Dealing with negative earnings

**Teaches:** When starting-period earnings are negative, the standard growth rate cannot be meaningfully estimated (change/base = 0.30/−0.05 = −600%). Three workarounds exist, but the deeper lesson is that when earnings are negative the growth rate is meaningless no matter how you compute it — it can be calculated but tells you little about the future. (For such firms, use the revenue/margin approach instead — see p.193.)

**Rules:** Three solutions when base earnings are negative.
- Use the higher of the two earnings numbers as the denominator (0.30/0.25 = 120%).
- Use the absolute value of the starting earnings as the denominator (0.30/0.05 = 600%).
- Run a linear regression of earnings over time. Divide the regression coefficient by the average earnings.
Overriding rule: a growth rate off a negative base is meaningless. Do not rely on it.

**Example:** Time Warner: change in EPS = 0.25 − (−0.05) = $0.30. Method 1: 0.30/0.25 = 120%. Method 2: 0.30/0.05 = 600%. Naive: 0.30/−0.05 = −600%.

**Compute:** MIXED (each workaround is deterministic arithmetic; deciding that none is informative is judgment)
**Tags:** negative-earnings, growth-estimation, heuristics

## p.166 — The effect of size on growth: Callaway Golf

**Teaches:** Growth rates decline as firms scale up. Callaway Golf's net profit grew from 1.80 in 1990 to 122.30 in 1996 — a geometric average growth rate of 102% — but the year-by-year growth rates fell steadily from 255.56% to 25.18% as the base grew. A historical average computed over a scaling-up period will vastly overstate sustainable future growth.

**Data:**

| Year | Net Profit | Growth Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 1.80 | — |
| 1991 | 6.40 | 255.56% |
| 1992 | 19.30 | 201.56% |
| 1993 | 41.20 | 113.47% |
| 1994 | 78.00 | 89.32% |
| 1995 | 97.70 | 25.26% |
| 1996 | 122.30 | 25.18% |

Geometric average growth rate 1990–1996 = 102%.

**Example:** Callaway Golf: (122.30/1.80)^(1/6) − 1 ≈ 102% geometric average, even though the most recent years' growth is only ~25%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (profits by year → growth rates and geometric average); JUDGMENT in interpreting the average as unsustainable
**Tags:** historical-growth, scaling-effect, callaway-golf, size-and-growth

## p.167 — Extrapolation and its dangers

**Teaches:** Mechanically extrapolating a high historical growth rate produces absurd results. If Callaway's net profit ($122.30 in 1996) kept growing at its past-6-years rate (~102%/year), expected net income in 5 years (2001) would be $4.113 billion — an implausible number for a golf-equipment company. Illustrates why historical growth from a small base must not be projected forward.

**Data (illustrative extrapolation):** 1996: $122.30; 1997: $247.05; 1998: $499.03; 1999: $1,008.05; 2000: $2,036.25; 2001: $4,113.23 (each year ≈ prior year × 2.02).

**Example:** 122.30 × (2.02)^5 ≈ 4,113.23, i.e. $4.113 billion projected net income by 2001.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (base profit + growth rate → projected profits); the lesson (don't do it) is JUDGMENT
**Tags:** extrapolation, historical-growth, callaway-golf, growth-dangers

## p.169 — Analyst forecasts of growth

**Teaches:** An analyst's job is nominally to find under- and over-valued stocks in the sectors they cover. Yet most of their time (outside selling) goes to forecasting earnings per share — mostly the EPS in the next earnings report, not long-term growth. Many analysts do publish 5-year expected EPS growth. The analysis and information behind those long-term estimates is far more limited than behind next-quarter forecasts. Analyst EPS and growth forecasts are widely disseminated by services such as Zacks and IBES, at least for U.S. companies.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** analyst-estimates, eps-forecasts, growth-estimation

## p.170 — How good are analysts at forecasting growth?

**Teaches:** Analyst EPS forecasts tend to beat simple time-series models, but only by small margins. The analyst advantage decreases with forecast horizon (next quarter vs 5 years), is greater for larger firms than smaller firms, and is greater at the industry level than the company level. Growth forecasts and revisions are highly correlated across analysts (herding).

**Data:**

| Study | Group tested | Analyst Error | Time Series Model Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Collins & Hopwood | Value Line Forecasts | 31.7% | 34.1% |
| Brown & Rozeff | Value Line Forecasts | 28.4% | 32.2% |
| Fried & Givoly | Earnings Forecaster | 16.4% | 19.8% |

**Rules:** Trust analyst forecasts more for short horizons, large firms, and industry-level estimates; discount long-horizon (5-year) analyst growth estimates.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** analyst-estimates, forecast-accuracy, empirical-evidence

## p.171 — Are some analysts more equal than others?

**Teaches:** A study of All-America Analysts (chosen by Institutional Investor) found four things. (1) There is no evidence they were chosen for forecasting skill: their median forecast error in the quarter before selection was 30%, versus 28% for other analysts. (2) In the calendar year after being chosen, they become slightly better forecasters; their median forecast error is 2% lower than other analysts'. (3) Their earnings revisions move stock prices much more than other analysts' revisions. (4) Their recommendations move prices more (3% on buys, 4.7% on sells); the price changes are sustained and keep rising in the following period (2.4% for buys, 13.8% for sells). Implication: star status confers influence and better company access, rather than reflecting superior prior skill.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** analyst-estimates, all-america-analysts, market-impact, empirical-evidence

## p.172 — The five deadly sins of an analyst

**Teaches:** Five behavioral failings of sell-side analysts.
- Tunnel Vision: so focused on the sector and within-sector valuations that the bigger picture is lost.
- Lemmingitis: strong urge to change recommendations and revise earnings estimates when other analysts do.
- Stockholm Syndrome: analysts start identifying with the managers of the firms they cover.
- Factophobia (often coupled with delusions of being a famous storyteller): basing recommendations on a "story" while refusing to face the facts.
- Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde: analysts who see their primary job as bringing investment-banking business to the firm.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** analyst-estimates, behavioral-biases, analyst-incentives

## p.173 — Propositions about analyst growth rates

**Teaches:** Proposition 1: analyst forecasts contain far less private information and far more public information than generally claimed. Proposition 2: the biggest source of private information for analysts is the company itself. That explains three things: why there are more buy than sell recommendations (information bias and the need to preserve sources); why forecasts and revisions are highly correlated across analysts; and why All-America analysts become better forecasters after being chosen. Proposition 3: there is value in knowing what analysts forecast for a firm's earnings growth, but danger both when they agree too much (lemmingitis/herding) and when they agree too little (their information is so noisy as to be useless).

**Rules:** Use analyst consensus as one input; distrust it when dispersion is extremely low (herding) or extremely high (noise).

**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** analyst-estimates, private-information, herding

## p.175 — Fundamental growth rates (mechanics)

**Teaches:** Earnings growth comes mechanically from how much a firm reinvests and what return it earns. Current earnings = investment in existing projects × current return on those projects. Next period's earnings = existing investment × next period's ROI + new investment × ROI on new projects. The change in earnings therefore has two sources: any change in ROI on existing investment, plus new investment times its return.

**Formulas:** Current Earnings = Investment in Existing Projects × Current Return on Investment. Next Period's Earnings = Investment in Existing Projects × Next Period's ROI + Investment in New Projects × Return on New Projects. Change in Earnings = Investment in Existing Projects × (Change in ROI) + Investment in New Projects × Return on New Projects.

**Example:** Existing investment $1,000 at 12% ROI → current earnings $120. Add $100 new investment at 12%, ROI on existing unchanged (change 0%) → next period's earnings = 1000×12% + 100×12% = $132; change in earnings = 1000×0% + 100×12% = $12.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (investment amounts and ROIs → earnings and change in earnings)
**Tags:** fundamental-growth, reinvestment, return-on-investment

## p.176 — Growth rate derivations

**Teaches:** Derives the fundamental growth equation. In the special case where ROI on existing projects is unchanged and equals ROI on new projects, growth in earnings = reinvestment rate × return on investment. In the general case where ROI can change period to period, growth also includes an efficiency term from the ROI change on existing investment.

**Formulas:** Special case: (Investment in New Projects / Current Earnings) × Return on Investment = Change in Earnings / Current Earnings, i.e. Growth Rate in Earnings = Reinvestment Rate × Return on Investment, where Reinvestment Rate = new investment / current earnings and ROI = return earned on that investment. General case: [Investment in Existing Projects × (Change in ROI) + New Projects × ROI] / [Investment in Existing Projects × Current ROI] = Change in Earnings / Current Earnings.

**Example:** Reinvestment rate = 100/120 = 83.33%; ROI = 12% → growth = 83.33% × 12% = 10% ($12/$120). If ROI rises from 12% to 13%: [$1,000 × (0.13 − 0.12) + 100 × 0.13] / [$1,000 × 0.12] = $23/$120 = 19.17%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (existing investment, new investment, current and next ROI → growth rate)
**Tags:** fundamental-growth, reinvestment-rate, roi-change, derivation

## p.177 — Fundamental growth: three variations by earnings measure

**Teaches:** The fundamental growth equation (growth = reinvestment measure × return measure) comes in three consistent variations depending on the earnings measure: EPS, net income from non-cash assets, or operating income. Each pairs a matching reinvestment measure and return measure — mismatching them gives wrong answers.

**Data:**

| Earnings Measure | Reinvestment Measure | Return Measure |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share | Retention Ratio = % of net income retained = 1 − Payout ratio | Return on Equity = Net Income / Book Value of Equity |
| Net Income from non-cash assets | Equity reinvestment Rate = (Net Cap Ex + Change in non-cash WC − Change in Debt) / Net Income | Non-cash ROE = Net Income from non-cash assets / (Book value of equity − Cash) |
| Operating Income | Reinvestment Rate = (Net Cap Ex + Change in non-cash WC) / After-tax Operating Income | Return on Capital (ROIC) = After-tax Operating Income / (Book value of equity + Book value of debt − Cash) |

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC once the accounting inputs are given (each row: reinvestment measure × return measure → growth); choosing the right variation is JUDGMENT
**Tags:** fundamental-growth, retention-ratio, reinvestment-rate, roe, roic

## p.178 — I. Expected long-term growth in EPS

**Teaches:** For EPS growth, reinvestment rate = retained earnings / current earnings = retention ratio, and return on investment = ROE = net income / book value of equity. In the special case where the current ROE stays unchanged, expected EPS growth equals retention ratio times ROE. Proposition 1: a company's expected long-term earnings growth cannot exceed its return on equity (since retention ratio ≤ 100%).

**Formulas:** g_EPS = Retained Earnings_(t−1) / NI_(t−1) × ROE = Retention Ratio × ROE = b × ROE, where b = retention ratio (fraction of net income retained), ROE = Net Income / Book Value of Equity, NI = net income.

**Rules:** Long-term expected earnings growth ≤ ROE.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (retention ratio and ROE → g_EPS)
**Tags:** eps-growth, retention-ratio, roe, fundamental-growth

## p.180 — Regulatory effects on expected EPS growth

**Teaches:** An exercise extending the Wells Fargo example: assume the 2008 banking crisis forces banks to hold 30% more book equity to do the same business. With the same net income spread over 30% more equity, ROE falls (new ROE = 17.56%/1.30 = 13.51%), and with the same retention ratio expected growth drops (0.4537 × 13.51% ≈ 6.13%). Demonstrates how regulation that raises capital requirements mechanically lowers ROE and hence sustainable EPS growth. (The slide leaves the blanks "New Return on Equity =" and "Expected growth rate =" for students to fill.)

**Formulas:** New ROE = Old ROE / (1 + % increase in required book equity); Expected growth = Retention Ratio × New ROE.

**Example:** Wells Fargo: book equity up 30% → new ROE = 17.56%/1.3 = 13.51%; expected growth = 0.4537 × 13.51% ≈ 6.13%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (old ROE, capital increase %, retention ratio → new ROE and growth)
**Tags:** eps-growth, regulation, banks, roe, wells-fargo

## p.181 — Pumping up ROE with debt

**Teaches:** ROE can be raised by using more financial leverage: as long as the after-tax return on capital exceeds the after-tax cost of debt, adding debt increases ROE (and vice versa). This makes leverage a way to raise fundamental EPS growth — with an obvious risk downside.

**Formulas:** ROE = ROC + D/E × (ROC − i(1−t)).
- ROC = EBIT_t (1 − tax rate) / Book value of Capital_(t−1)
- D/E = book value of debt / book value of equity
- i = interest expense on debt / book value of debt
- t = tax rate on ordinary income
- Book value of capital = Book Value of Debt + Book Value of Equity − Cash

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, D/E, i, t → ROE)
**Tags:** roe, leverage, roc, fundamental-growth

## p.182 — Decomposing ROE: Brahma 1998

**Teaches:** Brahma (now Ambev) had extremely high ROE partly because it borrowed at a rate well below its return on capital — leverage magnified a healthy ROC into a much higher ROE. The slide asks: this seems an easy way to deliver higher EPS growth — what is the downside? (Answer: added financial risk; leverage cuts both ways.)

**Example:** Brahma 1998: ROC = 19.91%; Debt/Equity = 77%; after-tax cost of debt = 5.61%. ROE = ROC + D/E(ROC − i(1−t)) = 19.91% + 0.77 × (19.91% − 5.61%) = 30.92%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, D/E, after-tax cost of debt → ROE)

## p.184 — II. Expected growth in net income from non-cash assets

**Teaches:** The EPS fundamental growth equation assumes retained earnings are reinvested at the ROE, but firms that pile up cash mute the effect on net income. A more general version substitutes actual equity reinvestment in real assets (net cap ex and working capital, net of debt funding) and strips cash out of both income and equity.

**Formulas:** Net Income from non-cash assets = Net income − Interest income from cash × (1 − t), where t = tax rate. Equity Reinvestment Rate = (Net Capital Expenditures + Change in Working Capital) × (1 − Debt Ratio) / Net Income from non-cash assets. Non-cash ROE = Net Income from non-cash assets / (BV of Equity − Cash). Expected Growth in Net Income = Equity Reinvestment Rate × Non-cash ROE.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, interest income, tax rate, cap ex, depreciation, WC change, debt ratio, book equity, cash → expected growth)
**Tags:** net-income-growth, equity-reinvestment, non-cash-roe, fundamental-growth

## p.186 — III. Expected growth in EBIT: stable ROC and reinvestment rate

**Teaches:** For operating income (EBIT) growth with stable fundamentals, the reinvestment rate is measured against after-tax operating income and the return measure is return on capital. Expected growth in operating income = reinvestment rate × ROC. Proposition: for a given growth rate, a firm's net capital expenditure needs are inversely proportional to the quality of its investments (higher ROC firms need to reinvest less for the same growth).

**Formulas:** Reinvestment Rate = (Net Capital Expenditures + Change in WC) / EBIT(1−t). Return on Capital ROC = EBIT(1−t) / (BV of Debt + BV of Equity − Cash). Expected Growth in Operating Income = Reinvestment Rate × ROC. (t = tax rate; WC = working capital; BV = book value.)

**Rules:** For a given growth target, required reinvestment is inversely proportional to ROC.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cap ex, depreciation, WC change, EBIT, tax rate, book debt/equity, cash → expected EBIT growth)
**Tags:** ebit-growth, reinvestment-rate, roc, fundamental-growth

## p.188 — ROIC and its limits

**Teaches:** ROIC is the "magical number" proxy for returns on existing assets and for continuing returns from them. It has serious limitations. (1) Abnormal earnings: the last 12 months may be unusually good or bad. (2) Accounting issues: operating income is skewed by misclassification (leases, R&D) and unusual items. (3) Life-cycle effect: current earnings do not reflect long-term potential for young and infrastructure firms. (4) Accounting write-offs: writing off mistakes shrinks invested capital and flatters the ratio. (5) Inflation: unadjusted book values understate capital invested in older assets. (6) Accounting misclassification: capital expenses (R&D) and financial expenses (leases) booked as operating expenses understate invested capital. Operating income is measured as trailing-12-month operating income, net of the effective tax rate paid over those 12 months.

**Formulas:** ROIC = After-tax Operating Income / Capital Invested in existing assets; Invested Capital = Book value of equity + Book value of debt − Cash & Cross holdings.

**Compute:** MIXED (ROIC computation is deterministic from financials; correcting for the six distortions is judgment)
**Tags:** roic, accounting-limitations, invested-capital, measurement

## p.189 — IV. Operating income growth when ROC is changing

**Teaches:** When return on capital changes over time, growth has a second component beyond reinvestment: an efficiency term, positive if ROC is rising and negative if falling. If the change occurs over multiple periods, the efficiency component should be spread out over each period.

**Formulas:** Expected Growth Rate = ROC_(t+1) × Reinvestment Rate + (ROC_(t+1) − ROC_t) / ROC_t, where ROC_t = return on capital in period t and ROC_(t+1) = return on capital in period t+1.

**Rules:** Spread the efficiency-growth component ((ROC_(t+1) − ROC_t)/ROC_t) evenly over the periods during which the ROC change occurs.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC_t, ROC_(t+1), reinvestment rate → expected growth)
**Tags:** ebit-growth, changing-roc, efficiency-growth, fundamental-growth

## p.190 — Motorola's growth rate (changing ROC)

**Teaches:** Worked application of the changing-ROC growth formula. Motorola's current ROC is 12.18% with a 52.99% reinvestment rate; ROC is expected to rise to 17.22% over 5 years (halfway to the industry average). Combining new-investment growth (at the new ROC) with annualized efficiency growth from existing assets gives 16.29% expected growth, decomposable into 9.12% from new investments and 7.17% from using existing investments more efficiently. Assumption noted: new investments earn 17.22% immediately, while existing assets improve gradually over 5 years.

**Formulas:** Expected Growth Rate = ROC_NewInvestments × Reinvestment Rate_Current + {[1 + (ROC_In5years − ROC_Current)/ROC_Current]^(1/5) − 1}, where the exponent 1/5 annualizes the 5-year efficiency improvement.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (current ROC, target ROC, horizon, reinvestment rate → expected growth and decomposition); choosing the target ROC is JUDGMENT
**Tags:** motorola, changing-roc, efficiency-growth, worked-example

## p.191 — The value of growth

**Teaches:** Five firms all show the same 10% expected growth rate, but they achieve it very differently: high-return new investment, massive reinvestment at low returns, or pure efficiency gains. With a 10% cost of capital, growth only creates value when the return on capital exceeds the cost of capital. Firm 1 (ROIC 50% > 10%) creates value. Firms 2 and 4 (ROIC = 10% = cost of capital) create none from new investment. Firm 3 (ROIC 5% < 10%) destroys value despite growing 10%. Firms 4 and 5 get some or all growth from efficiency improvements on existing assets, which does add value. Same growth rate, very different value effects.

**Formulas:** Expected growth = Growth from new investments + Efficiency growth = Reinvestment Rate × ROC + (ROC_t − ROC_(t−1)) / ROC_(t−1).

**Data:**

| | Firm 1 | Firm 2 | Firm 3 | Firm 4 | Firm 5 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Reinvestment Rate | 20.00% | 100.00% | 200.00% | 20.00% | 0.00% |
| ROIC on new investment | 50.00% | 10.00% | 5.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% |
| ROIC on existing investments before | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% |
| ROIC on existing investments after | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.80% | 11.00% |
| Expected growth rate | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% | 10.00% |

**Rules:** Growth adds value only if ROIC on new investments > cost of capital; growth from efficiency improvements on existing assets adds value; growth at ROIC = cost of capital is value-neutral; growth at ROIC < cost of capital destroys value.

## p.193 — Growth when operating income is negative or margins are changing

**Teaches:** The fundamental growth equations assume a sustainable long-term ROE or ROC. They break down when operating income is negative or margins are expected to change. In those cases, use a three-step top-down process. Step 1: estimate revenue growth over time. Size the total market for the business model, estimate attainable market share, decrease the growth rate as the firm gets larger, and sanity-check absolute revenues for feasibility. Step 2: estimate expected operating margins each year. Set a target margin the firm will converge to, and adjust the current margin toward it. Step 3: estimate the capital needed to deliver that growth. Pick a sales-to-capital ratio and use it to generate each year's reinvestment needs.

**Rules:** Use the top-down (revenue → margin → reinvestment) approach whenever operating income is negative or margins are changing; taper revenue growth with scale; verify absolute revenue levels are feasible against total market size.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (market size, share, target margin, sales-to-capital are all estimates; the year-by-year arithmetic that follows is deterministic)
**Tags:** top-down-growth, negative-earnings, revenue-growth, target-margin, sales-to-capital

## p.196 — Expected growth rate: decision tree summary

**Teaches:** Summary flowchart of all growth-estimation approaches. Expected growth rate splits into Equity Earnings vs Operating Income. Equity earnings growth can come from analysts, fundamentals, or historical data; fundamentals split into EPS and Net Income measures, each with stable-return and changing-return formulas. Operating income growth comes from fundamentals or history; fundamentals split into stable ROC, changing ROC, and negative earnings (which requires the top-down route: revenue growth, operating margins, reinvestment needs).

**Formulas:** EPS, stable ROE: g = ROE × Retention Ratio. EPS, changing ROE: g = ROE_(t+1) × Retention Ratio + (ROE_(t+1) − ROE_t)/ROE_t. Net Income, stable ROE: g = ROE × Equity Reinvestment Ratio. Net Income, changing ROE: g = ROE_(t+1) × Equity Reinvestment Ratio + (ROE_(t+1) − ROE_t)/ROE_t. Operating income, stable ROC: g = ROC × Reinvestment Rate. Operating income, changing ROC: g = ROC_(t+1) × Reinvestment Rate + (ROC_(t+1) − ROC_t)/ROC_t. Negative earnings: forecast via (1) revenue growth, (2) operating margins, (3) reinvestment needs.

**Rules:** Pick the growth model by (a) earnings measure (equity earnings vs operating income), (b) whether returns are stable or changing, and (c) whether earnings are negative (use top-down).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC per formula once inputs chosen; model selection is JUDGMENT (MIXED)

## p.198 — Getting closure in valuation

**Teaches:** A publicly traded firm potentially has infinite life, so its value is the present value of cash flows forever. Since cash flows cannot be estimated forever, we estimate cash flows for a finite "growth period" and then a terminal value that captures all value at the end of that period.

**Formulas:** Value = Σ_{t=1}^{∞} CF_t / (1+r)^t (infinite-horizon version), where CF_t = expected cash flow in year t and r = discount rate. Practical version: Value = Σ_{t=1}^{N} CF_t / (1+r)^t + Terminal Value / (1+r)^N, where N = length of the explicit growth period and Terminal Value = value of all cash flows beyond year N as of year N.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cash flows, discount rate, terminal value, N → value)
**Tags:** terminal-value, dcf, present-value, closure

## p.199 — Ways of estimating terminal value

**Teaches:** There are three ways to estimate terminal value.
- Liquidation value: most useful when assets are separable and marketable.
- Multiple approach: easiest, but it turns the whole valuation into a relative valuation. It imports market pricing into an intrinsic valuation.
- Stable growth model: technically soundest. It requires judgments about when the firm will reach a stable growth rate it can sustain forever, and what excess returns (if any) it will earn during that period.

**Rules:** Use liquidation value when assets are separable and marketable. Avoid the multiple approach in intrinsic valuation; it makes the result a relative valuation. Prefer the stable growth model for intrinsic DCF, accepting the required judgments on timing and excess returns.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of approach and stable-growth assumptions)
**Tags:** terminal-value, liquidation-value, exit-multiple, stable-growth-model

## p.200 — 1. Obey the growth cap

**Teaches:** When cash flows grow at a constant rate forever, value is the growing-perpetuity formula. The first rule of terminal value is the growth cap: the stable growth rate cannot exceed the growth rate of the economy. It can be set lower. If the economy contains both high-growth and stable-growth firms, stable firms likely grow below the economy's rate. The stable growth rate can even be negative; the firm then gradually disappears, lowering terminal value. Growth must be consistent with the currency. With nominal cash flows and discount rates, use a nominal growth rate in the valuation currency. A simple proxy for the economy's nominal growth rate is the riskfree rate.

**Formulas:** Value = Expected Cash Flow Next Period / (r − g), where r = discount rate (cost of equity or cost of capital, matching the cash flow) and g = expected constant growth rate forever.

**Rules:** Stable growth rate ≤ growth rate of the economy (can be lower or negative); nominal cash flows require nominal growth in the valuation currency; proxy for the economy's nominal growth rate = the riskfree rate.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (next-period cash flow, r, g → value); choosing g subject to the cap is JUDGMENT (MIXED)
**Tags:** terminal-value, stable-growth, growth-cap, perpetuity, riskfree-rate
