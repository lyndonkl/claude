## p.241 — Other potential liabilities on the way to equity value

**Teaches:** Beyond ordinary debt, two other claims should be netted out when moving from firm value to equity value. First, underfunded pension or health-care plans: subtract the underfunding at this stage. Do not double count by also including a cash-flow line item for cash set aside to meet the unfunded obligation. Do not count these items as debt in the cost-of-capital calculation either. Second, contingent liabilities (e.g., an undecided lawsuit): subtract their expected value.

**Formulas:**
- Value of contingent liability = Probability that the liability will occur * Expected value of liability, where probability is the estimated chance the liability materializes and expected value is the magnitude if it does.

**Rules:**
- Subtract pension/health-care underfunding from firm value to get equity value, but never simultaneously as a cash-flow item or as debt in the cost of capital (avoid double counting).
- Contingent liabilities enter at expected value (probability-weighted), not at face value.

**Compute:** MIXED — the expected-liability multiplication is deterministic (probability, liability size → expected value); estimating the probability and magnitude is judgment.
**Tags:** equity-value, contingent-liabilities, pension-underfunding, dcf-adjustments

## p.242 — Equity to employees: effect on value (intro)

**Teaches:** Firms now often pay employees (especially top managers) with equity options or restricted stock. Options granted are usually long-term and on volatile stocks. ## p.243 — The easier problem: restricted stock grants

**Teaches:** Restricted stock is the simpler case. For past grants: count already-granted restricted stock in shares outstanding today. That lowers value per share. For expected future grants: estimate their value as a percent of revenue. Forecast that as part of compensation expense, which lowers future income and cash flows.

**Rules:**
- Past restricted stock grants → include in the share count (denominator).
- Future restricted stock grants → forecast as an operating/compensation expense (numerator, via lower cash flows).

**Compute:** MIXED — including shares in the count is deterministic; forecasting future grants as % of revenue is judgment.

## p.244 — The bigger challenge: employee options

**Teaches:** Options can increase shares outstanding, but dilution per se is not the problem. Options affect equity value at exercise because shares are issued below the prevailing market price (options are exercised only when in the money). Equivalently, the company uses cash flows that would otherwise go to equity investors to buy back shares to meet exercise. Either way, equity value falls. Options affect equity value BEFORE exercise because valuation must build in the probability of and cost of future exercise.

## p.245 — A simple example: XYZ option grant setup

**Teaches:** Sets up a base-case firm to test option-accounting approaches. It then asks what a 10-million at-the-money option grant does to value per share. The correct answer (developed on later slides) is (c): value drops by less than 10%. Exercise brings cash into the firm, but the options have time value, so the effect is not zero.

**Formulas:**
- Value of firm = FCFF / (Cost of capital − growth rate), the growing-perpetuity (stable growth) formula, where FCFF = free cash flow to the firm.

**Example:** XYZ company: FCFF = $100 million growing 3%/yr in perpetuity, cost of capital 8%, 100 million shares, $1 billion debt. Value of firm = 100/(.08−.03) = 2000; − Debt 1000 = Equity 1000; Value per share = 1000/100 = $10. Then XYZ grants its CEO 10 million at-the-money options (strike $10). Choices: (a) no effect since not in the money; (b) −10% from potential 10m new shares; (c) decrease by less than 10% since options bring in cash but have time value.

## p.246 — I. The diluted share count approach

**Teaches:** The simplest fix divides equity value by fully diluted shares (adding shares that would exist if options were exercised). Its flaw: it ignores the cash that exercise brings into the firm. So it overstates the impact of options and understates value per share.

**Formulas:**
- Value per share (diluted approach) = (Firm value − Debt) / (Existing shares + shares from option exercise).

**Example:** Same XYZ: Value of firm = 100/(.08−.03) = 2000; Debt = 1000; Equity = 1000; diluted shares = 110 (100 + 10 option shares); Value per share = 1000/110 = $9.09.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — equity value, diluted share count → value per share.
**Tags:** employee-options, diluted-shares, per-share-value, worked-example

## p.247 — II. The treasury stock approach

**Teaches:** The treasury stock approach adds expected option-exercise proceeds to equity value before dividing by diluted shares. Its flaws: it ignores the time premium on the options. It also mishandles out-of-the-money options. If counted, they can (wrongly) raise value per share. If ignored, they are treated as non-existent even though they have value.

**Formulas:**
- Value per share (treasury stock approach) = (Equity value + Option exercise proceeds) / Diluted shares, where proceeds = number of options * strike price.

**Example:** Same XYZ: Equity = 1000; diluted shares = 110; proceeds from exercise = 10 options * $10 strike = 100; Value per share = (1000 + 100)/110 = $10 — identical to the no-option value, which is wrong because the time premium is ignored.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — equity value, options, strike, diluted shares → value per share.
**Tags:** employee-options, treasury-stock-approach, per-share-value, worked-example

## p.248 — III. The option value drag approach (steps)

**Teaches:** The preferred method values the options directly and subtracts them from equity as a separate claim. Step 1: value the firm (DCF or other). Step 2: subtract outstanding debt to get equity (or estimate equity directly). Step 3: subtract the market value (or estimated value) of other equity claims: warrants, conversion options, and employee options. Step 4: divide the remaining equity by ACTUAL (not diluted) shares outstanding to get value per share.

**Formulas:**
- Value of Warrants = Market Price per Warrant * Number of Warrants (or estimate via an option pricing model).
- Value of Conversion Option = Market Value of Convertible Bonds − Value of Straight Debt Portion of Convertible Bonds.
- Value of employee options: value using the average exercise price and average maturity in an option pricing model.
- Value per share = (Equity value − value of all option claims) / Actual shares outstanding.

**Compute:** MIXED — the subtraction/division is deterministic given the option values; valuing the options (model choice, inputs) is judgment.
**Tags:** employee-options, option-value-drag, warrants, convertibles, per-share-value

## p.249 — The dilution problem in valuing firm-issued options

**Teaches:** Standard option pricing models can value employee options, subject to four caveats. (1) Employee options are long term, making constant-variance and constant-dividend-yield assumptions shakier. (2) They cause stock dilution. (3) They are often exercised early, making European models dangerous. (4) They cannot be exercised until the employee vests. Partial fixes: use a model allowing variance shifts and early exercise, factor in dilution, and adjust the resulting value for the probability that the employee does not vest.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — model-selection caveats, no computation.
**Tags:** employee-options, option-pricing, dilution, vesting

## p.250 — Valuing employee options with dilution-adjusted Black-Scholes

**Teaches:** Applies a dilution-adjusted Black-Scholes model to the XYZ example. Note the stock price input is itself adjusted downward for dilution ($10 → $9.58) before being fed into the model.

**Formulas:**
- Value per call = S_adj * N(d1) − K * e^(−r*t) * N(d2). Here S_adj = dilution-adjusted stock price; K = strike price; r = riskless rate; t = maturity in years. N(d1) and N(d2) are cumulative standard normal probabilities from the Black-Scholes d1, d2 terms.

**Example:** Inputs: Stock price $10, adjusted for dilution = $9.58; Strike = $10; Maturity = 10 years (can be reduced to reflect early exercise); standard deviation of stock price = 40%; riskless rate = 4%. Model outputs: N(d1) = 0.8199, N(d2) = 0.3624. Value per call = 9.58(0.8199) − 10 * e^(−0.04*10) * (0.3624) = $5.42.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — S, K, t, sigma, r, dilution adjustment → option value ($5.42 per call).
**Tags:** black-scholes, employee-options, option-valuation, worked-example

## p.251 — From value of equity to value per share (option drag result)

**Teaches:** Completes the XYZ example using the $5.42 per-call value: subtract total option value from equity, then divide by ACTUAL shares. The resulting $9.46 is higher than the diluted share count answer ($9.09, which ignores exercise proceeds). It is lower than the treasury stock answer ($10.00, which ignores the options' time premium). This bracketing confirms the option-drag approach is the consistent middle ground.

**Formulas:**

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — equity value, option count, per-option value, shares → value per share.

## p.252 — To tax adjust or not to tax adjust

**Teaches:** The example assumed no tax advantages from options. If option exercise creates tax deductions for the firm, the true cost of the options is lower by the tax savings. Simple adjustment: multiply option value by (1 − tax rate) to get an after-tax option cost.

**Formulas:**
- After-tax option cost = Value of options * (1 − tax rate), where tax rate = the firm's marginal tax rate.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — option value, tax rate → after-tax cost.

## p.253 — Option grants in the future

**Teaches:** If the firm will keep granting options each year, expected future grants also reduce value today. Simplest mechanism: (1) estimate the value of options granted each year over the last few years as a percent of revenues. (2) Forecast that percentage into future years, letting it shrink as revenues grow. (3) Treat this line item as part of operating expenses each year, which reduces operating margin and cash flow.

**Rules:**
- Past/outstanding grants → subtract value from equity (stock/option drag). Future grants → forecast as an annual operating expense (% of revenues, declining as the firm scales).

**Compute:** MIXED — expensing the forecast line item is deterministic once the % path is set; choosing the % of revenues path is judgment.
**Tags:** employee-options, future-grants, operating-expenses, forecasting

## p.254 — When options affect equity value per share the most

**Teaches:** Option grants hurt per-share value more (a) the lower the strike price is next to the stock price, (b) the longer the option maturity, and (c) the more volatile the stock. The effect grows even larger when companies are allowed to reset (reprice) exercise prices downward after the stock price falls.

**Rules:** Bigger option drag when: strike << stock price; long maturity; high volatility; repricing/reset provisions exist.

## p.261 — Step 3: Check the narrative — possible, plausible, probable

**Teaches:** Narratives fall on a probability-of-occurrence spectrum and each grade gets a different valuation treatment. "IT IS POSSIBLE" (probability cannot be assessed): it could happen but you don't know what, when, or what it will look like — valuation response: value as an option, with option value increasing in the size of the possible market and the exclusivity of the firm's access to it. "IT IS PLAUSIBLE" (low probability): you can make a reasoned argument it could happen though there is no tangible evidence yet — response: show as expected growth, adjusting for risk in the expected return; value rises with market size and the firm's competitive advantages. "IT IS PROBABLE" (increasing probability): you expect it to happen with some basis/evidence, though substantial uncertainty remains — response: show in base-year numbers and expected cash flows, adjusting for risk in the expected return. Moving from possible to plausible requires gauging market potential and testing products; from plausible to probable requires product success and financial results.

**Rules:**
- Possible (cannot assess probability) → value as a real option.
- Plausible (low probability, reasoned argument, no tangible evidence) → build into expected growth, risk-adjust the return.
- Probable (evidence-backed expectation) → build into base-year numbers and expected cash flows, risk-adjust the return.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — classification framework.
**Tags:** narrative-and-numbers, possible-plausible-probable, real-options, growth

## p.262 — The impossible, the implausible and the improbable

**Teaches:** A checklist of narrative failures. THE IMPOSSIBLE (never allow):
- Bigger than the economy: perpetuity growth rate > economy growth rate.
- Bigger than the total market: revenues that imply more than 100% market share.
- Profit margin > 100%: earnings growth exceeds revenue growth long enough to push margins above 100%.
- Depreciation without cap ex: depreciation exceeds cap ex in perpetuity.

THE IMPLAUSIBLE (requires extraordinary justification):
- Growth without reinvestment: growth forever with no reinvestment.
- Profits without competition: high growth and higher profits with no competitive response.
- Returns without risk: high returns in a business with no risk.

THE IMPROBABLE (internally inconsistent combinations, drawn as the "Value Narrative" triangle of Growth–Risk–Reinvestment): high growth and low risk; high growth and low reinvestment; low risk and high reinvestment.

**Rules:**
- Perpetual growth rate must be ≤ economy growth rate.
- Implied market share must be ≤ 100% of the business's total market.
- Margins must stay below 100%; earnings growth cannot exceed revenue growth indefinitely.
- Depreciation cannot exceed cap ex in perpetuity.
- Growth requires reinvestment; excess profits invite competition; high returns come with risk.
- Flag improbable pairings: high growth + low risk, high growth + low reinvestment, low risk + high reinvestment.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — consistency checks (some are mechanically checkable given a model's inputs).
**Tags:** narrative-checks, consistency, terminal-growth, reinvestment, margins

## p.274 — Step 6: Be ready to modify narrative as events unfold

**Teaches:** Narratives change in three ways, each with a valuation response and a tool. Narrative Break/End: external events (legal, political, economic) or internal ones (management, competitive, default) can end the narrative; valuation estimates (cash flows, risk, growth, value) are no longer operative; tool: estimate a probability the break occurs and its consequences. Narrative Shift: improvement or deterioration in the initial business model — changing market size, market share and/or profitability; valuation estimates must be modified to reflect the new data; tool: Monte Carlo simulations or scenario analysis. Narrative Change (expansion or contraction): unexpected entry/success in a new market or unexpected exit/failure in an existing one; valuation must be redone with new overall market potential and characteristics; tool: real options.

**Rules:**
- Narrative break/end → probability-weight the break and its consequences.
- Narrative shift → Monte Carlo simulation or scenario analysis.
- Narrative expansion/contraction → real options.

## p.279 — Training wheels valuation: Con Ed in August 2008

**Teaches:** A stable-growth dividend discount model (DDM) valuation of Con Ed (August 2008), with three consistency tests. Why a stable-growth DDM:
1. Why stable growth: Con Ed is a regulated utility, restricted from new growth markets. New York's population and power needs grow very slowly (growth rate forever ≈ 2%).
2. Why equity: the debt ratio has been stable (~70% equity, 30% debt) for decades.
3. Why dividends: the company has paid out about 97% of its FCFE as dividends over the last five years. Model value $42.30 vs. an August 12, 2008 market price of $40.76.

**Formulas:**
- Value per share today = Expected dividends per share next year / (Cost of equity − Growth rate) — the Gordon growth model; expected DPS next year = current DPS × (1 + g).
- Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Beta × Equity risk premium = 4.1% + 0.8 × (4.5%) = 7.70%.
- Expected growth (fundamental) = Retention ratio × ROE = 27% × 7.7% ≈ 2.1% (using ROE = cost of equity = 7.7%).

**Rules:**
- Test 1 (dividends like a stable growth firm?): dividend payout ratio 73% — high payout consistent with stable growth. Trailing 12 months through June 2008: EPS $3.17, DPS $2.32.
- Test 2 (stable growth rate consistent with fundamentals?): retention ratio 27%, ROE = cost of equity = 7.7% → expected growth 2.1%; growth forever must be sustainable.
- Test 3 (risk consistent with a stable-growth firm?): beta of 0.80 is at the lower end of the stable-company beta range of 0.8–1.2.
- Use a stable-growth DDM when: growth is constrained (regulated utility, slow-growing service area), leverage is stable, and dividends ≈ FCFE (here ~97%).

**Example:** Con Ed, August 2008. Inputs: DPS $2.32, growth forever 2.1%, riskfree rate 4.10% (10-year T-bond), beta 0.80 (regulated power utilities), ERP 4.5% (implied US ERP, 8/2008). Cost of equity = 4.1% + 0.8(4.5%) = 7.70%. Value per share = 2.32(1.021)/(.077 − .021) = $42.30. Market price on August 12, 2008: $40.76 — stock roughly fairly valued to slightly cheap.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — DPS, g, riskfree rate, beta, ERP → cost of equity, value per share (choosing g, beta, ERP is judgment upstream).
