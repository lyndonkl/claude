## p.201 — Risk free Rates and Nominal GDP Growth

**Teaches:** Both the risk free rate and nominal GDP growth decompose into expected inflation plus a real component (real interest rate vs. real growth), which motivates using nominal GDP growth as a proxy/cap for the risk free rate. The argument for setting Risk free rate = Nominal GDP growth has two parts. (1) In the long term the real growth rate cannot be lower than the real interest rate, since growth in goods/services must cover the promised rate. (2) In the long term real growth can exceed the real interest rate to compensate risk-taking. But as economies mature the difference shrinks, and it is prudent to assume the extra growth comes from the economy's growth companies. Historical US data show the 10-year T.Bond rate tracking nominal GDP growth reasonably closely over long periods.
**Formulas:**
- Risk free Rate = Expected Inflation + Expected Real Interest Rate
- Nominal GDP Growth = Expected Inflation + Expected Real Growth
**Rules:** As a long-term prudent assumption, cap the perpetual growth rate at (or set the risk free rate equal to) nominal GDP growth.
**Data:**
| Period | 10-Year T.Bond Rate | Inflation Rate | Real GDP Growth | Nominal GDP growth rate | Nominal GDP - T.Bond Rate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1954-2015 | 5.93% | 3.61% | 3.06% | 6.67% | 0.74% |
| 1954-1980 | 5.83% | 4.49% | 3.50% | 7.98% | 2.15% |
| 1981-2008 | 6.88% | 3.26% | 3.04% | 6.30% | -0.58% |
| 2009-2015 | 2.57% | 1.66% | 1.47% | 3.14% | 0.57% |
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given expected inflation and real component → risk free rate or nominal GDP growth by addition); choosing the components is JUDGMENT.

## p.202 — Practical Reason for the Risk Free Rate Cap: Consistency

**Teaches:** Your risk free rate implicitly embeds an assumption about nominal growth in the economy: a low risk free rate implies low expected inflation and low real growth; a high risk free rate implies high nominal growth. If your explicit nominal growth assumption in the cash flows conflicts with the implicit growth assumption in the discount rate denominator, you bias the valuation. Assuming high nominal economic growth with a low risk free rate overvalues businesses. Assuming low nominal growth with a high risk free rate undervalues them.
**Rules:** Keep the growth assumption in the cash flows consistent with the nominal growth implied by the risk free rate; high growth + low risk free rate → overvaluation bias; low growth + high risk free rate → undervaluation bias.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** risk-free-rate, consistency, valuation-bias, terminal-value

## p.205 — Tie Growth Period to Competitive Advantages

**Teaches:** Growth per se does not create value — only growth with excess returns does. For growth firms to keep generating value-creating growth, they must keep competition at bay. Proposition 1: the stronger and more sustainable the competitive advantages, the longer a growth company can sustain value-creating growth. Proposition 2: growth companies with strong and sustainable competitive advantages are rare.
**Rules:** Length of the value-creating growth period should be tied to the strength and sustainability of competitive advantages (moats).

## p.206 — Growth Has to Be Earned (Stable-Period Reinvestment)

**Teaches:** The fundamental growth equation says growth comes from reinvestment times return on invested capital plus efficiency gains. In stable growth you cannot count on efficiency delivering growth, so you must reinvest to deliver the forecast growth rate. The stable-period reinvestment rate is therefore determined by the stable growth rate and the perpetual return on capital. The terminal value equation embeds this.
**Formulas:**
- Growth rate = Reinvestment Rate x Return on invested capital + Growth rate from improved efficiency
- Reinvestment Rate (stable) = Stable growth rate / Stable period ROC = g / ROC
- Terminal Value in year n = EBIT_{n+1} x (1 - t) x (1 - g/ROC) / (Cost of Capital - g), where EBIT_{n+1} = operating income in year n+1, t = tax rate, g = stable growth rate, ROC = stable-period return on capital.
**Rules:** In stable growth, always tie the reinvestment rate to g/ROC — never assume growth is free.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs EBIT_{n+1}, t, g, ROC, cost of capital → terminal value); choosing g and ROC is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** terminal-value, reinvestment-rate, stable-growth, roc

## p.208 — Excess Returns to Zero?

**Teaches:** Some (e.g., McKinsey) argue return on capital should always equal cost of capital in stable growth (zero excess returns). But empirical evidence (McKinsey chart of median ROIC for top 500 US public companies by revenues in 1965, 1975, 1985, 1995, tracked 1969–2004) shows ROIC is sustainable over long time periods (roughly stable around 8–12%), while real revenue growth inevitably declines toward GDP growth. So it can be defensible to allow modest excess returns in perpetuity, but not high growth.
**Rules:** Growth fades toward economy growth; excess returns can persist — mean-revert growth faster than excess returns.

## p.209 — Don't Fall for Sleight of Hand (Cap Ex = Depreciation)

**Teaches:** A common DCF trick assumes that in stable growth capital expenditures merely offset depreciation and there are no working capital needs (maintenance cap ex only). Poses the question: if you make that assumption, what expected growth rate can you legitimately use in the terminal value? (Implied answer: zero real growth — no net reinvestment means no real growth.) Follow-up: is it acceptable if the stable growth rate equals the inflation rate? (Even then it is questionable, since inflation-driven nominal growth usually still requires some reinvestment in working capital and assets at inflated prices.)
**Rules:** If net cap ex and working capital investment are zero in the terminal period, the growth rate used must be consistent (effectively zero real growth); do not pair zero reinvestment with positive real growth.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

## p.210 — Be Internally Consistent (Stable Period Characteristics)

**Teaches:** Stable growth firms should look mature on every dimension: betas closer to one; debt ratios closer to industry or mature-company averages; country risk premiums (especially emerging markets) evolving over time; excess returns approaching or becoming zero (ROC → cost of capital, ROE → cost of equity); and reinvestment/payout reflecting lower growth and excess returns.
**Formulas:**
- Stable period payout ratio = 1 - g/ROE (g = stable growth rate, ROE = stable-period return on equity)
- Stable period reinvestment rate = g/ROC (ROC = stable-period return on capital)
**Rules:** In stable phase set beta ≈ 1, debt ratio ≈ industry/mature average, fading country risk premium, excess returns ≈ 0.

## p.213 — Which Cash Flow Should I Discount? (Equity vs. Firm Valuation)

**Teaches:** Use equity valuation (a) for firms with stable leverage, whether high or not, and (b) when equity (stock) is being valued. ## p.214 — Dividends or FCFE?

**Teaches:** Choosing between the dividend discount model and the FCFE model. DDM: (a) firms whose dividends (plus stock buybacks) are close to FCFE over an extended period; (b) firms where FCFE is hard to estimate (e.g., banks and financial service companies). ## p.215 — What Discount Rate Should I Use?

**Teaches:** Match the discount rate to the cash flow: cash flows to equity → cost of equity; cash flows to the firm → cost of capital. ## p.216 — Which Growth Pattern Should I Use?

**Teaches:** Match the growth model to firm characteristics. Stable growth model: firm is large and growing at or below the economy's growth rate, or constrained by regulation from growing faster, or has the characteristics of a stable firm (average risk and reinvestment rates). 2-stage model: large and growing at a moderate rate (≤ overall growth rate + 10%), or a single product with barriers to entry of finite life (e.g., patents). 3-stage or n-stage model: small and growing at a very high rate (> overall growth rate + 10%), or significant barriers to entry, or characteristics very different from the norm.
**Rules:** Growth ≤ economy growth (or regulated, or stable-firm traits) → stable growth model; growth ≤ economy growth + 10% (or finite-life single-product moat) → 2-stage; growth > economy growth + 10% (or strong barriers, or atypical firm) → 3-stage/n-stage.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC threshold screen given firm growth vs. economy growth; assessing barriers/characteristics is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** growth-pattern, stable-growth, two-stage-model, three-stage-model, model-choice

## p.217 — The Building Blocks of Valuation

**Teaches:** Every DCF is assembled from three choices. (1) A cash flow: expected dividends to stockholders; or FCFE = Net Income - (1-δ)(Capital Exp. - Depreciation) - (1-δ)(Change in Working Capital), where δ = debt ratio; or FCFF = EBIT(1 - tax rate) - (Capital Exp. - Depreciation) - Change in Working Capital. (2) A discount rate. For equity cash flows, use the cost of equity — the riskier the investment, the greater the cost of equity; models are CAPM (Riskfree Rate + Beta x Risk Premium) and APM (Riskfree Rate + Σ Beta_j x Risk Premium_j over n factors). For firm cash flows, use the cost of capital: WACC = k_e x E/(D+E) + k_d x D/(D+E), with k_d = current borrowing rate x (1-t) and E, D at market values. (3) A growth pattern: stable growth (constant g), two-stage (high growth then stable), or three-stage (high growth, transition, stable).
**Formulas:**
- FCFE = Net Income - (1 - δ)(Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - (1 - δ)(Change in Working Capital), δ = debt ratio
- FCFF = EBIT(1 - tax rate) - (Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - Change in Working Capital
- CAPM cost of equity = Riskfree Rate + Beta x Risk Premium
- APM cost of equity = Riskfree Rate + Σ_{j=1..n} Beta_j x Risk Premium_j
- WACC = k_e x E/(D+E) + k_d x D/(D+E); k_d = current borrowing rate x (1 - t); E, D = market values of equity and debt
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC formulas given inputs; picking model/pattern is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf-building-blocks, fcfe, fcff, wacc, capm, growth-pattern

## p.220 — 1. The Value of Cash

**Teaches:** The cleanest treatment of cash and marketable securities is to keep them out of the operating valuation. Cash flows should be before interest income from cash, and the discount rate should be uncontaminated by cash — use betas of the operating assets alone for cost of equity. Then add back the value of cash after valuing operating assets. In many equity valuations interest income from cash is instead included in cash flows, which requires adjusting the discount rate (beta weighted down by cash holdings). Unless cash stays a fixed percentage of value over time, such valuations tend to break down.
**Rules:** Value operating assets excluding cash and its interest income, then add cash back; avoid the blended approach unless cash is a constant fraction of value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (methodological rule).
**Tags:** cash, non-operating-assets, valuation-mechanics

## p.222 — Should You Discount Cash for Its Low Returns?

**Teaches:** Some analysts argue that excess cash (cash beyond operating needs) earning a low return (below what the firm earns on non-cash investments) should be discounted. That is the wrong reason: cash invested in riskless securities should earn a low rate; as long as the return is fair given its riskless nature, cash does not destroy value. The right reason to discount cash, applicable to some companies: managers can do stupid things with cash (overpriced acquisitions, pie-in-the-sky projects), and you should discount for that possibility.
**Rules:** Do not discount cash merely for earning a low riskless return; discount cash only for the risk of poor managerial deployment.

## p.229 — Valuing Cross Holdings Right

**Teaches:** The correct three-step procedure: Step 1 — value the parent company without any cross holdings, which requires unconsolidated financial statements. Step 2 — value each cross holding individually (using market values of the cross holdings imports the market's valuation errors into your valuation). Step 3 — parent equity value = value of un-consolidated parent - debt of un-consolidated parent + sum over holdings of the parent's proportional share of each holding's equity value.
**Formulas:** Value of equity in parent with N cross holdings = Value of un-consolidated parent company - Debt of un-consolidated parent company + Σ_{j=1}^{N} (% owned of Company j) x (Value of Company j - Debt of Company j).
**Rules:** Use unconsolidated statements for the parent; value each holding intrinsically rather than at market value.

## p.231 — Approximating Cross Holding Value (Price-to-Book Shortcut)

**Teaches:** When you cannot do the full intrinsic sum-of-parts, approximate. For majority holdings (full consolidation): convert the balance-sheet minority interest from book to market value using the subsidiary's sector-average price-to-book ratio, and subtract that from the consolidated firm's estimated value to get parent equity. For minority holdings: convert the book value of the holdings reported on the balance sheet into market value by multiplying by the sector price-to-book ratio(s), and add that to the value of operating assets to get total firm value.
**Formulas:** Estimated market value of minority interest = Minority interest on balance sheet x Price-to-Book ratio for sector (of subsidiary). Similarly, market value of minority holdings = Book value of holdings x sector Price-to-Book.
**Rules:** Majority/consolidated → subtract P/B-adjusted minority interest; minority holdings → add P/B-adjusted book value of holdings.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given book values and sector P/B ratios.
**Tags:** cross-holdings, price-to-book, approximation, minority-interest

## p.233 — 3. Other Assets Not Counted Yet

**Teaches:** Which non-operating assets may be added to a DCF value. Do NOT add assets already contributing to cash flows: real estate housing your offices, PP&E (factories/productive assets), brand names or customer lists (their value is in the cash flows), and definitely not goodwill (a non-asset). CAN add two kinds of assets. First, overfunded pension plans: if a defined benefit plan's assets exceed expected liabilities, the overfunding may be counted, with two caveats — collective bargaining agreements may block claiming the excess, and withdrawals from pension plans are often taxed at much higher rates. Second, unutilized assets: assets or property not generating cash flows (e.g., vacant land) are not yet in your valuation, so assess a market value and add it to firm value.
**Rules:** Add only assets whose earnings are NOT in the forecast cash flows; never double-count operating assets or brand value; never add goodwill.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** non-operating-assets, double-counting, pension-plans, unutilized-assets, goodwill

## p.238 — Dealing with Complexity

**Teaches:** In DCF valuation there are three stances toward complexity. The aggressive analyst trusts the firm's statements about its value. The conservative analyst refuses to value what cannot be seen. The compromise adjusts the valuation for complexity — via cash flows, the discount rate, the expected growth rate/length of growth period, or by valuing the firm and then discounting the value for complexity. In relative valuation you can estimate the price the market charges for complexity. A regression across the hundred largest market-cap firms gives: PBV = 0.65 + 15.31 x ROE - 0.55 x Beta + 3.04 x Expected growth rate - 0.003 x (# pages in 10K). Each extra 10K page costs about 0.003 in price-to-book.
**Formulas:** PBV = 0.65 + 15.31(ROE) - 0.55(Beta) + 3.04(Expected growth rate) - 0.003(# Pages in 10K), where PBV = price-to-book ratio, ROE = return on equity, Beta = market beta; estimated on the 100 largest market-cap firms.
**Rules:** Adjust for complexity in cash flows, discount rate, growth assumptions, or as a final value discount; do not ignore it.
**Compute:** MIXED — the PBV regression prediction is DETERMINISTIC given ROE, beta, growth, and 10K pages; choosing how to adjust a DCF for complexity is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** complexity-discount, regression, price-to-book, relative-valuation

## p.239 — 5. Defining Debt for Cost of Capital Purposes

**Teaches:** For cost of capital purposes, debt is defined by three characteristics. There is a commitment to make fixed payments in the future. The fixed payments are tax deductible. Failure to make the payments can lead to default or loss of control of the firm to the party owed. So defined, debt should include all interest-bearing liabilities (short and long term) and all leases (operating as well as capital). Debt should NOT include accounts payable or supplier credit. Beware conservative impulses to count everything as debt: that inflates the debt ratio and understates the cost of capital.
**Rules:** Include: all interest-bearing liabilities (short + long term), all leases (operating + capital). Exclude: accounts payable, supplier credit. Test: fixed commitment + tax deductibility + default/control consequences.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC classification given the liability's characteristics.
**Tags:** debt-definition, cost-of-capital, leases, accounts-payable

## p.240 — Book Value or Market Value of Debt?

**Teaches:** For a distressed telecom with DCF enterprise value of $1 billion and $1 billion face value of debt trading at 50% of face: is equity worth nothing (EV minus face value) or $500 million (EV minus market value of debt)? The market-value answer ($500m) is right for a going concern, since the debt could effectively be retired at its market price. But if the liquidation value of the assets today is $1.2 billion and you planned to liquidate today, the answer changes: in liquidation, debt must be paid at face value ($1 billion), leaving equity $200 million — the face-value treatment applies.
**Rules:** Going concern → subtract market value of debt from enterprise value; liquidation → debt is owed at face value, so subtract face value from liquidation proceeds.
**Example:** Distressed telecom: EV (DCF) = $1b; debt face $1b trading at 50% → market value $0.5b. Going concern equity = $1b - $0.5b = $500m. Liquidation: assets $1.2b - face debt $1b = $200m equity.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC arithmetic given EV/liquidation value and debt values; choosing going-concern vs. liquidation frame is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** market-value-of-debt, distressed-firms, liquidation, equity-value
