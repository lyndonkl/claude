## p.82 — The CAPM Beta: The Most Used (and Misused) Risk Measure

**Teaches:** The standard way of estimating a stock's beta is to run a regression of the stock's returns (Rj) against market returns (Rm); the regression slope is the beta and measures the stock's riskiness. This regression beta has three problems: (1) it has a high standard error; (2) it reflects the firm's business mix over the regression period, not its current mix; and (3) it reflects the firm's average financial leverage over the period rather than its current leverage.

**Formulas:** Rj = a + b*Rm, where Rj = returns on the stock, Rm = returns on the market index, a = intercept of the regression, b = slope of the regression = beta of the stock.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given time series of stock returns and market returns → OLS slope = beta and intercept a).
**Tags:** capm, beta, regression-beta, risk-measurement

## p.87 — Decision tree of relative risk measures beyond beta

**Teaches:** If you reject betas or modern portfolio theory, there is still a menu of relative risk measures, organized by two questions. Question 1: Do you believe the marginal investors who price risk are diversified? Question 2: Do you believe in price-based risk measures? The choices map as follows.

**Rules:**
- Diversified marginal investor + price-based measures → The CAPM, the APM (arbitrage pricing model), or multi-factor models.
- Diversified marginal investor + NOT price-based → Accounting betas, or cost-of-debt-based models.
- NOT diversified + price-based → Relative price volatility, proxy models, "CAPM Plus" models, or implied cost of capital.
- NOT diversified + NOT price-based → Relative earnings volatility, or accounting-ratio-based models.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing a risk model based on beliefs about investors and measures).
**Tags:** risk-measures, capm-alternatives, decision-tree, cost-of-equity

## p.88 — Price-based measures without the diversified-investor focus

**Teaches:** Three price-based relative risk measures for those who reject the diversified-investor assumption. (1) Relative standard deviation: relative volatility = std dev of the stock / average std dev across all stocks; it captures all risk, not just market risk. (2) Proxy models: look at historical returns on all stocks and find variables (fundamentals) that explain return differences — effectively multiple regressions with individual stock returns as the dependent variable and fundamentals as independent variables; the approach started with market cap (the small-cap effect) and has since added momentum, liquidity, etc. (3) CAPM Plus models: start with the traditional CAPM (Rf + Beta*ERP) and add other premiums for proxies (e.g., a small-cap premium).

**Formulas:** Relative Volatility = Std dev of Stock / Average Std dev across all stocks. CAPM Plus: Cost of equity = Rf + Beta*(ERP) + additional proxy premiums, where Rf = riskfree rate and ERP = equity risk premium.

**Compute:** MIXED — relative volatility is DETERMINISTIC (stock std dev and market-average std dev → ratio); proxy/CAPM-plus models require JUDGMENT in choosing variables and premiums.
**Tags:** relative-volatility, proxy-models, capm-plus, risk-measures

## p.89 — Non-price-based risk measures

**Teaches:** For those who distrust market-price-based risk measures: (1) Accounting risk measures — compute an accounting beta or relative volatility from accounting earnings volatility, or compute a risk score from balance-sheet ratios like debt ratios or cash holdings (akin to default-risk scores such as the Z-score). (2) Qualitative risk models — risk assessments based at least partially on qualitative factors such as quality of management. (3) Debt-based measures — estimate a cost of equity from the company's observable cost of debt by scaling it up: Cost of equity = Cost of debt * Scaling factor, where the scaling factor can be computed from implied volatilities.

**Formulas:** Cost of equity = Cost of debt * Scaling factor (scaling factor derivable from implied volatilities).

**Compute:** MIXED — accounting beta/relative earnings volatility is DETERMINISTIC from earnings series; scaling factors and qualitative assessments require JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** accounting-beta, qualitative-risk, cost-of-debt, risk-measures

## p.90 — Determinants of Betas and Relative Risk

**Teaches:** The equity (levered) beta is driven by the firm's unlevered beta plus its financial leverage. The unlevered (firm) beta has two determinants. (1) Nature of the product/service: the more discretionary the product or service, the higher the beta — so cyclical companies, luxury-goods firms, high-priced goods/services firms, and growth firms should all have higher betas than their opposites. (2) Operating leverage (fixed costs as a percent of total costs): the greater the proportion of fixed costs, the higher the beta — so firms with high infrastructure needs and rigid cost structures, smaller firms, and young firms should have higher betas. (3) Financial leverage: other things equal, the greater the proportion of capital raised from debt, the higher the equity beta — highly levered firms should have higher betas than firms with less debt.

**Formulas:** Equity Beta (Levered beta) = Unlevered Beta * (1 + (1 - t)*(Debt/Equity ratio)), where t = tax rate, D/E = debt-to-equity ratio in market values.

**Rules:** Discretionary product → higher beta; cyclical > non-cyclical; luxury > basic goods; high-priced > low-priced; growth > mature; high fixed costs > flexible costs; smaller > larger; young > mature; high leverage > low leverage.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC for the lever formula (unlevered beta, tax rate, D/E → levered beta); JUDGMENT for assessing product discretion and operating leverage.
**Tags:** beta-determinants, operating-leverage, financial-leverage, levered-beta

## p.91 — Ideal beta estimation process

**Teaches:** In a perfect world you would estimate a firm's beta in three steps. (1) Start with the beta of the business the firm is in. (2) Adjust that business beta for the firm's operating leverage to arrive at the firm's unlevered beta. (3) Use the firm's financial leverage to convert the unlevered beta into the equity (levered) beta.

**Formulas:** Levered Beta = Unlevered Beta * (1 + (1 - tax rate)*(Debt/Equity)).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC for step 3 (unlevered beta, tax rate, D/E → levered beta); JUDGMENT for steps 1-2.
**Tags:** beta-estimation, unlevered-beta, levered-beta, process

## p.92 — Adjusting for operating leverage

**Teaches:** Within any business, firms with lower fixed costs (as a percentage of total costs) should have lower unlevered betas. If you could compute fixed and variable costs for each firm in a sector, you could break the unlevered beta into a pure business component and an operating-leverage component. The biggest problem is informational: it is difficult to get fixed vs. variable cost data for individual firms, so in practice we assume that firms within a business have similar operating leverage and use the same unlevered beta for every firm in that business.

**Formulas:** Unlevered beta = Pure business beta * (1 + (Fixed costs / Variable costs)).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given pure business beta and fixed/variable cost split → unlevered beta; JUDGMENT in practice because the cost split is rarely observable.
**Tags:** operating-leverage, unlevered-beta, fixed-costs, beta-adjustment

## p.93 — Adjusting for financial leverage: two lever formulas

**Teaches:** Two ways to lever a beta. Conventional approach: assume debt carries no market risk (debt beta = 0); then the equity beta is a function of the unlevered beta and the debt-equity ratio (some versions ignore the tax effect and drop the (1-t) term). Debt-adjusted approach: if debt carries market risk and you can estimate a debt beta, subtract its levered contribution — this is more realistic but debt betas are difficult to estimate.

**Formulas:**
- Conventional: BL = Bu * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)), where BL = levered (equity) beta, Bu = unlevered beta, t = tax rate, D/E = market debt-to-equity ratio. (Some versions omit the (1-t).)
- Debt-adjusted: BL = Bu * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)) - Bdebt * (1-t) * (D/E), where Bdebt = beta of the firm's debt.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (Bu, t, D/E, and optionally Bdebt → BL).
**Tags:** levered-beta, financial-leverage, debt-beta, beta-formulas

## p.94 — Bottom-up Betas: five-step procedure

**Teaches:** The bottom-up beta procedure. Step 1: identify the business(es) the firm operates in. Step 2: find publicly traded firms in each business, average their regression betas, and unlever that average using the average D/E ratio of those firms: Unlevered beta for business = Average beta across publicly traded firms / (1 + (1-t)*(Average D/E ratio across firms)). Refinement: adjust for differences between your firm and the comparables in operating leverage and product characteristics. Step 3: estimate how much value your firm derives from each business (revenues or operating income are often used as weights, but estimating the value of each business is better). Step 4: compute the weighted average of the businesses' unlevered betas using the step-3 weights — this is the firm's bottom-up unlevered beta; if the business mix will change over time, weights can change year to year. Step 5: lever using the firm's own market D/E ratio: Levered bottom-up beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(Debt/Equity)); if the D/E ratio is expected to change over time, the levered beta will change over time.

**Formulas:** Unlevered beta for business = Average regression beta of comparable firms / (1 + (1-t)*(Average D/E of comparable firms)); Bottom-up unlevered beta of firm = sum over businesses of (weight_i * unlevered beta_i); Levered bottom-up beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E of the firm)).

**Compute:** MIXED — unlevering, weighting, and relevering are DETERMINISTIC (comparable betas, D/E ratios, weights, tax rate → firm levered beta); choosing comparables and business-value weights is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** bottom-up-beta, comparable-firms, unlevered-beta, beta-estimation

## p.95 — Why bottom-up betas?

**Teaches:** Three reasons to prefer bottom-up betas over regression betas. (1) Lower estimation error: averaging over many firms reduces standard error by roughly the square root of the sample size. (2) The bottom-up beta can be adjusted to reflect the firm's current or expected business mix and financial leverage, while regression betas reflect only the past. (3) Bottom-up betas can be estimated even without historical stock prices — for IPOs, private businesses, or divisions of companies.

**Formulas:** Std error of bottom-up beta = (Average std error across comparable-firm betas) / sqrt(Number of firms in sample).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (average standard error and sample size → bottom-up standard error).
**Tags:** bottom-up-beta, standard-error, private-firms, beta-estimation

## p.98 — Gross debt versus net debt approaches

**Teaches:** Analysts in Europe and Latin America often use net debt (debt minus cash) instead of gross debt when computing debt ratios, which yields very different levered betas. For Embraer, the gross D/E ratio is 1953/11,042 = 18.95%, giving a levered beta of 1.07; the net debt ratio is (1953 - 2320)/11,042 = -3.32% (cash exceeds debt), giving a levered beta of 0.95*(1+(1-0.34)*(-0.0332)) = 0.93. The net-debt cost of equity is much lower than the gross-debt one, but the cost of capital evens out because the debt ratio used in the cost-of-capital equation must then also be the net debt ratio.

**Formulas:** Net Debt Ratio = (Debt - Cash) / Market value of Equity; Levered beta (net) = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(Net D/E)).

**Example:** Embraer: gross D/E = 1953/11,042 = 18.95% → levered beta 1.07; net D/E = (1953-2320)/11,042 = -3.32% → levered beta = 0.95*(1 + 0.66*(-0.0332)) = 0.93. (Amounts in millions of BR reais; 1953 = debt, 2320 = cash, 11,042 = market value of equity.)

**Rules:** If you lever beta with a net debt ratio, you must also use the net debt ratio for the weights in the cost of capital — be internally consistent.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (debt, cash, equity market value, unlevered beta, tax rate → net debt ratio and levered beta).

## p.99 — The Cost of Equity: A Recap

**Teaches:** Summary map of cost-of-equity estimation: Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Risk Premium). The riskfree rate must be in the same currency as the cash flows and defined in the same terms (real or nominal). The beta should preferably be a bottom-up beta, based on other firms in the same business and the firm's own financial leverage. The risk premium can be either a historical premium — the mature-market premium (average premium earned by stocks over T-bonds in the US) plus a country risk premium = Country Default Spread * (sigma_Equity / sigma_CountryBond) — or an implied premium based on how the equity market is priced today and a simple valuation model.

**Formulas:** Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Risk Premium); Country risk premium = Country Default Spread * (sigma_Equity / sigma_CountryBond), where sigma_Equity = standard deviation of the country's equity market and sigma_CountryBond = standard deviation of the country's bond.

**Rules:** Riskfree rate must match cash flows in currency and real/nominal terms; prefer bottom-up beta over regression beta.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC once inputs are chosen (Rf, beta, ERP → cost of equity); JUDGMENT in choosing historical vs implied premium and estimating the components.
**Tags:** cost-of-equity, recap, risk-premium, bottom-up-beta

## p.101 — Estimating the Cost of Debt

**Teaches:** The cost of debt is the rate at which the firm can borrow currently; it reflects both the firm's default risk and the level of market interest rates. There are two widely used estimation approaches. (1) Look up the yield to maturity on a straight bond outstanding from the firm. This is limited because very few firms have long-term straight bonds that are liquid and widely traded. (2) Look up the firm's rating and estimate a default spread from that rating. This is more robust, but different bonds from the same firm can carry different ratings, so use a median rating. When in trouble (no rating, or multiple ratings), estimate a synthetic rating for the firm and base the cost of debt on that.

**Rules:** If liquid straight bonds exist → use YTM; else if rated → use median rating + default spread; else (no or conflicting ratings) → estimate a synthetic rating.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT in choosing the approach; the spread lookup itself is deterministic once the rating is known.
**Tags:** cost-of-debt, ratings, default-spread, synthetic-rating

## p.104 — Cost of debt computation for Embraer

**Teaches:** Applying the synthetic rating: Embraer's interest coverage ratio of 3.56 maps to a synthetic rating of A-, giving a company default spread of 1.00%. Companies in countries with low bond ratings and high default risk may also bear country default risk, especially if they are smaller or earn all revenues domestically. If Embraer bore all of Brazil's country risk, the full 2004 Brazil country default spread of 6.01% would be added. But larger companies with significant global revenues are less exposed. So only two-thirds of the Brazilian country risk is added, based on traded bond spreads of other large Brazilian companies in 2004.

**Formulas:** Cost of debt = Riskfree rate + (fraction of country risk borne) * (Country default spread) + Company default spread.

**Example:** Embraer 2004: Cost of debt = 4.29% + (2/3)*(6.01%) + 1.00% = 9.29%.

**Rules:** Firms with all revenues domestic and/or small → add full country default spread; large global-revenue firms → add a fraction (here 2/3) of it.

**Compute:** MIXED — the addition is DETERMINISTIC (Rf, country spread, company spread, fraction → cost of debt); choosing the country-risk fraction is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** cost-of-debt, country-risk, embraer, synthetic-rating

## p.105 — Synthetic ratings: some caveats

**Teaches:** The interest-coverage-to-rating relationship was developed using US companies and travels well as long as you analyze large manufacturing firms in markets with interest rates close to US rates. It is more problematic for smaller companies in markets with higher interest rates than the US. One adjustment: modify the interest coverage ratio for interest-rate differences — for instance, if interest rates in an emerging market are twice as high as in the US, halve the interest coverage ratio before using the table.

**Rules:** If local interest rates are k times US rates, divide the interest coverage ratio by k before looking up the synthetic rating (example: rates twice as high → halve the coverage ratio).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC adjustment once the rate ratio is known; JUDGMENT about when the table applies.

## p.111 — Worked example: Estimating cost of capital — Embraer in 2004

**Teaches:** Full cost-of-capital computation for Embraer (2004). Equity side: Cost of equity = 4.29% + 1.07*(4%) + 0.27*(7.89%) = 10.70%. The inputs are a riskfree rate of 4.29%, a levered beta of 1.07 applied to the mature-market premium of 4%, and a lambda of 0.27 applied to Brazil's country risk premium of 7.89%. Market value of equity = 11,042 million BR ($3,781 million). Debt side: cost of debt = 4.29% + 4.00% + 1.00% = 9.29%. That is the riskfree rate, plus 2/3 of Brazil's country default spread (≈4.00%), plus the company default spread of 1.00%. Market value of debt = 2,083 million BR ($713 million). It is estimated by treating the interest expense of 222 million BR as a 4-year annuity at 9.29% and discounting the book value of debt (1,953 million BR) over 4 years: MV debt = 222*(PV of annuity, 4 years, 9.29%) + 1953/1.0929^4 = 2,083 million BR. Book value of equity is 3,350 million BR; average debt maturity is 4 years. Cost of capital = 10.70%*(0.84) + 9.29%*(1-0.34)*(0.16) = 9.97%, using market-value weights E/(D+E) = 84%, D/(D+E) = 16%, and a marginal tax rate of 34%.

**Formulas:** Estimated market value of debt = Interest expense * (PV of annuity, average maturity, cost of debt) + Book value of debt / (1 + cost of debt)^average maturity; Cost of capital = Cost of equity * (E/(D+E)) + Cost of debt * (1-t) * (D/(D+E)).

**Example:** Embraer 2004: cost of equity 10.70%, MV equity 11,042m BR; cost of debt 9.29%, MV debt = 222*(PV annuity, 4 yrs, 9.29%) + 1953/1.0929^4 = 2,083m BR; cost of capital = 10.70%*0.84 + 9.29%*0.66*0.16 = 9.97%.

## p.112 — Converting a dollar cost of capital to a nominal-BR cost of capital

**Teaches:** Two ways to get a cost of capital in local (Brazilian real, BR) nominal terms instead of US dollars. Approach 1: redo all calculations with a BR riskfree rate. For example, if the BR riskfree rate were 12%: Cost of equity = 12% + 1.07*(4%) + 0.27*(7.89%) = 18.41%, and Cost of debt = 12% + 1% = 13%. This assumes the BR riskfree rate has no country risk premium embedded in it. Approach 2: convert the dollar cost of capital using the differential inflation rate.

**Formulas:** Cost of capital (BR) = (1 + Cost of Capital in $) * [(1 + Inflation_BR)/(1 + Inflation_$)] - 1, where Inflation_BR = expected inflation in Brazil and Inflation_$ = expected US inflation.

**Example:** With dollar cost of capital 9.97%, Brazilian inflation 8%, US inflation 2%: 1.0997 * (1.08/1.02) - 1 = 0.1644 = 16.44%.

## p.113 — Dealing with hybrids and preferred stock

**Teaches:** For hybrid securities such as convertible bonds, break the security into its debt and equity components and allocate the amounts accordingly — e.g., $125 million of convertible debt splits into a straight-debt component (debt) and a conversion-option component (equity). For preferred stock, keep it as a separate third component of capital; the cost of preferred stock is the preferred dividend yield. Rule of thumb: if preferred stock is less than 5% of the firm's outstanding market value, lumping it in with debt will make no significant difference to the valuation.

**Formulas:** Cost of preferred stock = Preferred dividend yield (annual preferred dividend / market price of preferred).

**Rules:** Convertibles → decompose into straight debt + conversion option (option counts as equity); preferred stock → separate component, unless < 5% of firm market value, in which case lumping with debt is acceptable.

## p.114 — Decomposing a convertible bond (worked example)

**Teaches:** How to split a convertible bond into debt and equity. The straight-debt value is what the bond would be worth as a plain bond: discount its coupons and face value at the rate on straight bonds of the same rating. The difference between the market value of the convertible and this straight-debt value is the conversion option, which counts as equity. The debt portion is added to debt and the option portion to market capitalization when computing cost-of-capital weights.

**Formulas:** Straight debt value = Coupon * (PV of annuity, maturity, straight-bond rate for the rating) + Face value / (1 + straight-bond rate)^maturity; Equity (option) portion = Market value of convertible - Straight debt value.

**Example:** Convertible with $125 million face value, 4% stated interest rate, 10-year maturity, market value $140 million; firm rated A with A-rated straight-bond rate of 8%. Straight debt = (4% of $125m)*(PV of annuity, 10 years, 8%) + 125/1.08^10 = $91.45 million. Equity portion = $140m - $91.45m = $48.55 million. The $91.45m goes to debt and $48.55m to market capitalization in the cost-of-capital weights.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (face value, coupon rate, maturity, market value, straight-bond rate → debt and equity portions).
**Tags:** convertible-bonds, decomposition, worked-example, capital-structure

## p.115 — Recapping the Cost of Capital

**Teaches:** Summary map of the cost-of-capital formula. Cost of equity should be based on a bottom-up beta. Cost of borrowing should be based on (1) a synthetic or actual bond rating and (2) a default spread: Cost of Borrowing = Riskfree rate + Default spread. The tax rate is the marginal tax rate, reflecting the tax benefits of debt. The debt and equity weights should be market value weights.

**Formulas:** Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity * (Equity/(Debt + Equity)) + Cost of Borrowing * (1 - t) * (Debt/(Debt + Equity)); Cost of Borrowing = Riskfree rate + Default spread; t = marginal tax rate.

**Rules:** Bottom-up beta for cost of equity; rating-based default spread for cost of debt; marginal tax rate; market value weights.

## p.117 — Steps in cash flow estimation

**Teaches:** Three steps in estimating cash flows. (1) Estimate the firm's current earnings: for cash flows to equity, use earnings after interest expenses (net income); for cash flows to the firm, use operating earnings after taxes. (2) Consider how much the firm invested to create future growth: investment not expensed shows up as capital expenditures (depreciation provides a cash flow that covers some of these), and increasing working capital needs are also investments for future growth. (3) If looking at cash flows to equity, also consider cash flows from net debt issues (debt issued minus debt repaid).

## p.118 — Measuring cash flows: FCFF vs FCFE vs dividends

**Teaches:** Cash flows can be measured either to all claimholders in the firm (FCFF) or just to equity investors (FCFE, or more narrowly actual dividends plus stock buybacks). FCFF starts from after-tax operating income; FCFE starts from net income and also nets out debt cash flows and preferred dividends.

**Formulas:**
- FCFF = EBIT*(1 - tax rate) - (Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash working capital.
- FCFE = Net Income - (Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash Working Capital - (Principal Repaid - New Debt Issues) - Preferred Dividend.
- Cash to equity investors (narrow measure) = Dividends + Stock Buybacks.

## p.119 — Three pathways to FCFF

**Teaches:** There are three equivalent ways to compute free cash flow to the firm, all starting from after-tax operating income. (1) FCFF = EBIT*(1 - tax rate) minus [(Capex - Depreciation) + Change in non-cash working capital]. (2) FCFF = EBIT*(1 - tax rate) minus Reinvestment, where Reinvestment is the bracketed sum above. (3) FCFF = EBIT*(1 - tax rate) * (1 - Reinvestment Rate), where Reinvestment Rate = Reinvestment / (EBIT*(1 - tax rate)). Closing question posed: where are the tax savings from interest expenses? (Answer developed in the course: not in the FCFF — they are captured in the cost of capital through the after-tax cost of debt.)

**Formulas:** FCFF = EBIT*(1-t) - [(Capex - Depreciation) + Change in non-cash WC] = EBIT*(1-t) - Reinvestment = EBIT*(1-t)*(1 - Reinvestment Rate), where Reinvestment = (Capex - Depreciation) + Change in non-cash WC and Reinvestment Rate = Reinvestment / (EBIT*(1-t)).

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (EBIT, tax rate, capex, depreciation, change in WC → FCFF by any of the three pathways).
