## p.362 — The drivers of value

**Teaches:** Intrinsic value is driven by four questions, each with an equity and a firm variant. (1) What are the cashflows from existing assets? — for equity, cashflows after debt payments; for the firm, cashflows before debt payments. (2) What is the value added by growth assets? — for equity, growth in equity earnings/cashflows; for the firm, growth in operating earnings/cashflows. (3) How risky are the cash flows from both existing assets and growth assets? — for equity, the risk in equity in the company; for the firm, the risk in the firm's operations. (4) When will the firm become a mature firm, and what are the potential roadblocks? These four drivers (existing-asset cashflows, growth, risk, and the path to maturity) are the entire fundamental basis of value.

## p.363 — The determinants of price

**Teaches:** In contrast to value, the market price is determined by four forces. (1) Mood and momentum: price is determined in large part by mood and momentum, which are driven by behavioral factors (panic, fear, greed). (2) Liquidity and trading ease: even when an asset's value does not change much from period to period, liquidity and ease of trading can change, and as they do, so will the price. (3) Incremental information: since traders make money on price changes, not price levels, the focus is on incremental information (news stories, rumors, gossip) and how it measures up relative to expectations. (4) Group think: to the extent that pricing is about gauging what other investors will do, the price can be determined by the "herd." None of these determinants is a fundamental driver of intrinsic value.

**Tags:** price-determinants, momentum, liquidity, behavioral-finance, herd-behavior

## p.364 — Three views of "the gap" between price and value

**Teaches:** Investors split into three camps on the gap between price and value. Each camp's belief dictates its investment strategy. Efficient marketers think gaps are random, so they index. Value extremists think price eventually converges to value, so they buy and hold undervalued stocks. Pricing extremists think value is illusory and convergence may never happen, so they trade mispricing and momentum.
**Data:**

| Camp | View of the gap | Investment strategies |
|---|---|---|
| The Efficient Marketer | The gaps between price and value, if they do occur, are random. | Index funds |
| The "value" extremist | You view pricers as dilettantes who will move on from fad to fad. Eventually, the price will converge on value. | Buy and hold stocks where value < price [i.e., where price < value — buy undervalued stocks] |
| The pricing extremist | Value is only in the heads of the "eggheads." Even if it exists (and it is questionable), price may never converge on value. | (1) Look for mispriced securities. (2) Get ahead of shifts in demand/momentum. |

## p.365 — The "pricers" dilemma

**Teaches:** Three problems face investors who play only the pricing game. (1) No anchor: if you do not believe in intrinsic value and never estimate it, you have no moorings when you invest. You get pushed back and forth as price moves from high to low. Everything becomes relative, and you can lose perspective. (2) Reactive: without a core measure of value, your investment strategy will often be reactive rather than proactive. (3) Crowds are fickle and tough to get a read on. Success as a pricer requires reading the crowd mood and detecting shifts in that mood early. By their nature, crowds are tough to read and almost impossible to model systematically.

## p.366 — The valuer's dilemma and ways of dealing with it

**Teaches:** Valuers face two distinct uncertainties about the price-value gap. The first is uncertainty about the magnitude of the gap. Four coping mechanisms apply. First, use a margin of safety. Many value investors demand a buffer between price and estimated value as protection against risk/uncertainty. Second, collect more information about the company to make the investment less risky. Third, ask what-if questions. Scenario analysis gives a sense of whether you should invest. Fourth, confront uncertainty directly. Bring it into the analysis and deal with the consequences. The second is uncertainty about whether the gap will ever close. That one is tougher. You can reduce exposure to it by lengthening your time horizon, and by providing or looking for a catalyst that will cause the gap to close.
**Rules:** If uncertain about gap magnitude → use margin of safety, more information, scenario analysis, or explicit uncertainty modeling. If uncertain about gap closing → lengthen time horizon or find/provide a catalyst.

## p.367 — Strategies for managing the risk in the "closing" of the gap

**Teaches:** Two strategies for the risk that price never converges to value. (1) The "karmic" approach: buy (or sell short) undervalued (overvalued) companies and sit back and wait for the gap to close, implicitly assuming that given time the market will see the error of its ways and fix it. (2) The catalyst approach: for the gap to close, price has to converge on value, and convergence usually requires a catalyst. If you are an activist investor, you may be the catalyst yourself — your act of buying the stock may be a sufficient signal for the market to reassess the price. If you are not an activist, look for other catalysts: a new CEO or management team, a "blockbuster" new product, or an acquisition bid where the firm is targeted.
**Rules:** Catalysts to watch for gap-closing: new CEO/management team, blockbuster new product, acquisition bid targeting the firm, or activist buying itself.

**Tags:** value-investing, catalysts, activist-investing, price-convergence
