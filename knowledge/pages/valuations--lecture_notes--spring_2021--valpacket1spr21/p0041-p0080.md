## p.41 — Risk free rate when you don't have/trust the government bond rate

**Teaches:** When a currency lacks a trustworthy government bond rate, there are two ways to construct a risk free rate. (1) Build-up approach: the risk free rate in any currency is the sum of expected inflation in that currency and the expected real interest rate. (2) Differential-inflation approach: scale up the US $ risk free rate by the ratio of expected inflation in the foreign currency to expected inflation in US dollars.

**Formulas:**
- Risk free rate = Expected Inflation in currency + Expected real interest rate
- Risk free rate_Currency = (1 + Riskfree rate_US$) * (1 + Expected Inflation_Foreign Currency) / (1 + Expected Inflation_US$) − 1
  - Riskfree rate_US$ = US dollar risk free rate; Expected Inflation_Foreign Currency = expected inflation rate in the foreign currency; Expected Inflation_US$ = expected US inflation rate.

**Example:**
- Build-up: expected inflation in a country = 15%, TIPs (real) rate = 1% → risk free rate = 16%.
- Differential inflation: US $ risk free rate = 2.00%, foreign inflation = 15%, US inflation = 1.5% → Risk free rate = (1.02)*(1.15)/(1.015) − 1 = 15.57%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: US$ risk free rate, foreign and US expected inflation, or inflation + real rate → output: foreign-currency risk free rate); choosing the inflation estimates is JUDGMENT.

## p.47 — The perils of trusting the past

**Teaches:** Two problems with historical risk premiums. (1) Noisy estimates: even with ~90 years of data (back to 1928) and an assumed 20% annual standard deviation of stock returns, the standard error of the premium exceeds 2% — large relative to the premium itself. (2) Survivorship bias: US 20th-century data oversamples one of the most successful economies/equity markets, biasing the premium upward relative to what an investor could have expected ex ante.

**Formulas:** Standard Error in Premium = Annualized standard deviation of stock returns / sqrt(Number of years of data). Example: 20% / sqrt(90) = 2.1%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (std dev of returns, number of years → standard error of premium).

## p.49 — Country risk premium via the country default spread

**Teaches:** Simplest additional-country-risk-premium approach: set the country risk premium (CRP) equal to the country's default spread, estimated one of three ways: (1) the default spread on a dollar-denominated bond issued by the country (Brazil $ bond spread in Jan 2021 = 2.05%); (2) the sovereign CDS spread adjusted for the US CDS (Brazil 10-yr CDS in Jan 2021 = 1.92%); (3) the default spread implied by the country's local-currency sovereign rating (Brazil rated Ba2; Ba2 sovereign default spread ≈ 2.65% in Jan 2021). The default spread is then added to a mature-market premium to get the total equity risk premium.

**Formulas:** Total ERP_country = Mature market premium + Country default spread. With mature market premium = 4.72% and Brazil CRP = 2.65%: Total ERP for Brazil = 4.72% + 2.65% = 7.37%.

**Rules:** Three acceptable proxies for the country default spread: sovereign US$ bond spread, sovereign CDS spread (net of US CDS), or rating-based default spread.

**Example:** Brazil, January 2021: rating Ba2 → default spread 2.65%; CRP = 2.65%; Total ERP = 4.72% + 2.65% = 7.37%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (mature market premium + chosen default spread → total ERP); choosing among the three spread measures is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, default-spread, sovereign-rating, equity-risk-premium

## p.50 — Equity-volatility approach to country total ERP

**Teaches:** Scale the US risk premium by the relative volatility of the emerging equity market versus the US equity market to get the emerging market's total equity risk premium; the country risk premium is the excess over the US premium.

**Formulas:** Total equity risk premium = Risk Premium_US * (sigma_Country Equity / sigma_US Equity), where sigma = annualized standard deviation of the equity market.

**Example:** US ERP = 4.72%; std dev of Bovespa (Brazil equity) = 30%; std dev of S&P 500 = 18%. Total ERP Brazil = 4.72% * (30%/18%) = 7.89%. Country ERP for Brazil = 7.89% − 4.72% = 3.17%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (US premium, two equity std devs → total ERP and CRP).

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, relative-volatility, equity-risk-premium, emerging-markets

## p.51 — Melded approach: default spread scaled by equity/bond volatility

**Teaches:** Country ratings measure default risk; equity risk premiums should exceed debt spreads even though the two are highly correlated. The melded approach multiplies the country bond default spread by the ratio of equity-market to government-bond volatility in that country, then adds the result to the mature market premium.

**Formulas:** Country Equity risk premium = Default spread on country bond * (sigma_Country Equity / sigma_Country Bond).

**Example:** Brazil, January 2021: std dev Bovespa (equity) = 30%; std dev Brazil government bond = 20%; default spread = 2.65%. Brazil CRP = 2.65% * (30%/20%) = 3.98%. Brazil Total ERP = 4.72% + 3.98% = 8.70%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (default spread, equity std dev, bond std dev, mature premium → CRP and total ERP).

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, melded-approach, default-spread, relative-volatility

## p.52 — Template for estimating a country ERP (Jan 1, 2021 procedure)

**Teaches:** Four-step flowchart for estimating any country's ERP. Step 1: estimate the mature market premium as the implied ERP for the S&P 500 (on Jan 1, 2021 ≈ 4.72%, updated monthly). Step 2: assess country risk via Moody's sovereign local-currency rating (if unavailable, use S&P and convert to Moody's equivalent). Step 3: convert country risk into an additional CRP. Step 4: ERP for the country.

**Rules:**
- If sovereign rating is AAA (Aaa): ERP for country = US ERP.
- If rating below Aaa: get a default spread (from sovereign US$ bond spread, CDS spread, or ratings table), then ERP for country = US ERP + Default Spread * Relative Equity Market Volatility, where Relative Equity Market Volatility = std dev of emerging-market equity index / std dev of emerging-market bond index (in Jan 2021 = 1.10).
- If no sovereign rating: get a country risk score from PRS (Political Risk Services) and estimate an ERP from the PRS score.

**Compute:** MIXED — the arithmetic per branch is DETERMINISTIC (rating → spread → ERP); rating conversion and PRS mapping involve JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, erp-estimation, sovereign-rating, methodology

## p.54 — From country ERPs to corporate equity risk premiums

**Teaches:** Three ways to build a company's expected return from a country risk premium. Approach 1 assumes every company in the country is equally exposed to country risk (CRP added outside beta). Approach 2 assumes exposure to country risk mirrors exposure to other market risk (CRP scaled by beta). Approach 3 treats country risk as a separate factor with its own exposure lambda (e.g. based on the share of non-domestic revenues).

**Formulas:**
- Approach 1: E(Return) = Riskfree Rate + CRP + Beta * (Mature ERP)
- Approach 2: E(Return) = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Mature ERP + CRP)
- Approach 3: E(Return) = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Mature ERP) + lambda * (CRP)
  where Mature ERP = mature market equity risk premium, CRP = additional country risk premium, Beta = market beta, lambda = firm-specific exposure to country risk.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given riskfree rate, beta, ERP, CRP, lambda → expected return; choosing the approach and lambda is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** cost-of-equity, country-risk-premium, capm, lambda

## p.55 — Approaches 1 & 2: location-based vs operation-based CRP

**Teaches:** Location-based CRP (the standard practice) attaches a country risk premium based on the country of incorporation — an Indian company gets the Indian CRP; developed-market companies are assumed unexposed to emerging market risk. Operation-based CRP is the more reasonable modification: compute the company's CRP as a weighted average of the CRPs of the countries it does business in, weighting by revenues or operating income; if exposure spans dozens of countries, weight regional risk premiums instead.

**Rules:** Prefer operation-based (revenue/operating-income weighted) CRP over location-based (country-of-incorporation) CRP.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC weighted average once weights and CRPs are chosen; choice of weights is JUDGMENT.

## p.60 — Approach 3: estimate a lambda for country risk

## p.61 — A revenue-based lambda

**Teaches:** Lambda measures a firm's relative exposure to country risk. A simple revenue-based version divides the firm's domestic revenue share by the average firm's domestic revenue share. Two implications: a company's risk exposure is determined by where it does business, not where it is incorporated; and firms can actively manage their country risk exposures.

**Formulas:** lambda = (% of revenues domestically for the firm) / (% of revenues domestically for the average firm in that market).

**Example:** Tata Motors (2008-09) got 91.37% of revenues in India; TCS got 7.62%; average Indian firm gets ~80% domestically. lambda_TataMotors = 91%/80% = 1.14; lambda_TCS = 7.62%/80% = 0.09.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (firm domestic revenue %, average-firm domestic revenue % → lambda).

**Tags:** lambda, country-risk-exposure, revenue-based, tata

## p.65 — Implied equity premiums: the concept

**Teaches:** If you know the price paid for an asset and expected cash flows, the IRR of those cash flows is your expected return. Assuming stocks are correctly priced in aggregate, solve for the discount rate that sets the present value of expected market cash flows equal to the current index level — that is the expected return on stocks. Subtracting the riskfree rate yields the implied equity risk premium: a forward-looking number that can be updated as often as desired.

**Formulas:** Implied ERP = IRR of expected market cash flows given current index level − riskfree rate.

## p.66 — Implied equity premium: January 2008 worked example

**Teaches:** Worked implied-ERP computation for the S&P 500 on January 1, 2008. Between 2001 and 2007 dividends plus buybacks averaged 4.02% of the index; base cash flow = 4.02% of 1468.36 = 59.03. Analysts expected 5%/yr earnings growth for 5 years (dividends & buybacks keep pace); after year 5, growth = 4.02% (the riskfree/T.Bond rate). Solving for r gives 8.39% expected return; implied ERP = 8.39% − 4.02% = 4.37%.

## p.77 — Which equity risk premium should you use?

**Teaches:** The right ERP depends on your assumption about markets: if premiums revert to historical norms and your time period yields those norms, use the historical risk premium; if the market is correct in the aggregate (or you want a market-neutral valuation), use the current implied ERP; if the market makes mistakes even in aggregate but is correct over time, use the average implied ERP over time. Predictive evidence favors implied premiums: the current implied premium correlates positively with future implied premiums and actual future returns, while the historical premium correlates negatively.

**Rules:**

| If you assume this | Premium to use |
|---|---|
| Premiums revert back to historical norms and your period yields those norms | Historical risk premium |
| Market is correct in the aggregate, or your valuation should be market neutral | Current implied equity risk premium |
| Market makes mistakes even in the aggregate but is correct over time | Average implied equity risk premium over time |

**Data:** Predictive power of ERP measures:

| Predictor | Correlation with implied premium next year | Correlation with actual return next 5 years | Correlation with actual return next 10 years |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current implied premium | 0.763 | 0.427 | 0.500 |
| Average implied premium: last 5 years | 0.718 | 0.326 | 0.450 |
| Historical premium | -0.497 | -0.437 | -0.454 |
| Default spread based premium | 0.047 | 0.143 | 0.160 |

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of premium based on market assumption); the correlations are data.
