## p.2 — The essence of intrinsic value
**Teaches:** In intrinsic valuation you value an asset based on its fundamentals (intrinsic characteristics). For cash-flow-generating assets, intrinsic value is a function of (1) the magnitude of expected cash flows over the asset's lifetime and (2) the uncertainty about receiving those cash flows. Discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation is the tool for estimating intrinsic value. The expected value of an asset is written as the present value of expected cash flows. Either the cash flows or the discount rate is adjusted to reflect risk.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual definition)
**Tags:** intrinsic-value, dcf, valuation-fundamentals

## p.3 — The two faces of discounted cash flow valuation
**Teaches:** There are two equivalent ways to do DCF. (1) Risk-adjusted discount rate approach: discount expected cash flows at a rate reflecting the riskiness of the cash flows. (2) Certainty equivalent approach: replace expected cash flows with the guaranteed cash flows you would accept instead (certainty equivalents) and discount them at the riskfree rate.
**Formulas:**
- Value of asset = E(CF_1)/(1+r) + E(CF_2)/(1+r)^2 + E(CF_3)/(1+r)^3 + ... + E(CF_n)/(1+r)^n. Here the asset has an n-year life. E(CF_t) = expected cash flow in period t. r = risk-adjusted discount rate reflecting the risk of the cash flows.
- Value of asset = CE(CF_1)/(1+r_f) + CE(CF_2)/(1+r_f)^2 + CE(CF_3)/(1+r_f)^3 + ... + CE(CF_n)/(1+r_f)^n. Here CE(CF_t) = certainty equivalent of E(CF_t). r_f = riskfree rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (cash flows, r or r_f → asset value); estimating the inputs is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, present-value, certainty-equivalent, risk-adjusted-discount-rate

## p.4 — Risk-adjusted value: two basic propositions (actually three)
**Teaches:** Restates both DCF value equations (risk-adjusted expected cash flows; certainty equivalents at the riskfree rate) and draws three propositions. (1) The "IT" proposition: if IT (any factor) does not affect the expected cash flows or the riskiness of the cash flows, IT cannot affect value. (2) The "DUH" proposition: for an asset to have value, expected cash flows must be positive at some time over the asset's life. (3) The "DON'T FREAK OUT" proposition: assets that generate cash flows early in their life are worth more than assets that generate cash flows later; the latter may compensate through greater growth and higher later cash flows.
**Formulas:** Same two value equations as p.3 (expected CF at rate r; certainty equivalents at r_f).
**Rules:** A factor affects value only through expected cash flows or their riskiness; positive expected cash flows are required for value; earlier cash flows are worth more than later ones, all else equal.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** dcf, valuation-principles, risk-adjusted-value

## p.5 — DCF choices: equity valuation versus firm valuation
**Teaches:** A financial balance sheet frames two DCF choices. Assets side: Assets in Place (existing investments generating cash flows today, long-lived fixed and short-lived working-capital assets) and Growth Assets (expected value from future investments). Liabilities side: Debt (fixed claim on cash flows, little/no management role, fixed maturity, tax deductible) and Equity (residual claim on cash flows, significant management role, perpetual life). Firm valuation values the entire business (all assets); equity valuation values just the equity claim.

## p.6 — Equity valuation
**Teaches:** In equity valuation the cash flows are cash flows from assets AFTER debt payments, and after reinvestment needed for future growth. The discount rate reflects only the cost of raising equity financing. The present value is the value of just the equity claims on the firm.
**Rules:** Equity cash flows (post-debt) must be discounted at the cost of equity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework)
**Tags:** equity-valuation, fcfe, cost-of-equity

## p.7 — Firm valuation
**Teaches:** In firm valuation the cash flows are cash flows from assets PRIOR to any debt payments, but after the firm has reinvested to create growth assets. The discount rate is the cost of capital: the cost of raising both debt and equity financing, in proportion to their use. The present value is the value of the entire firm, and it reflects all claims on the firm.
**Rules:** Pre-debt (firm) cash flows must be discounted at the cost of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework)
**Tags:** firm-valuation, fcff, cost-of-capital

## p.10 — Equity versus firm valuation: the two correct methods
**Teaches:** Both consistent methods give the same equity value. Method 1: discount CF to Equity at cost of equity → value of equity directly. Method 2: discount CF to Firm at cost of capital → firm value; subtract market value of debt → equity value.
**Formulas:**
- After-tax cost of debt = Pre-tax rate × (1 − tax rate) = 10% × (1 − 0.5) = 5%.
- Cost of capital = Cost of equity × (E/(D+E)) + After-tax cost of debt × (D/(D+E)) = 13.625% × (1073/1873) + 5% × (800/1873) = 9.94%.
- Value of Equity = Value of Firm − Market Value of Debt.

## p.11 — First principle of valuation: discounting consistency
**Teaches:** Discounting Consistency Principle: never mix and match cash flows and discount rates. Mismatching is deadly. Discounting equity (post-debt) cash flows at the weighted average cost of capital gives an upwardly biased estimate of equity value. Discounting pre-debt (firm) cash flows at the cost of equity gives a downwardly biased estimate of firm value.
**Rules:** Equity cash flows ↔ cost of equity; firm cash flows ↔ cost of capital. Equity CF at WACC → equity overvalued; firm CF at cost of equity → firm undervalued.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (principle)
**Tags:** discounting-consistency, valuation-errors, dcf

## p.12 — The effects of mismatching cash flows and discount rates

## p.14 — Discounted cash flow valuation: the steps
**Teaches:** The five steps of DCF valuation. (1) Estimate the discount rate(s): cost of equity for equity valuation or cost of capital for firm valuation; nominal or real depending on whether cash flows are nominal or real; the rate can vary across time. (2) Estimate current earnings and cash flows on the asset, to equity investors (CF to Equity) or to all claimholders (CF to Firm). (3) Estimate future earnings and cash flows, generally via an expected earnings growth rate. (4) Estimate when the firm will reach "stable growth" and its risk and cash-flow characteristics at that point. (5) Choose the right DCF model for the asset and value it.
**Rules:** Match discount rate type (equity vs capital) and units (nominal vs real) to the cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (process framework)
**Tags:** dcf-steps, valuation-process, stable-growth

## p.15 — Generic DCF valuation model
**Teaches:** Diagram of the generic DCF structure: cash flows (firm = pre-debt cash flow; equity = after-debt cash flows) grow during a high-growth period at an expected growth rate (firm: growth in operating earnings; equity: growth in net income/EPS), producing CF_1 ... CF_n; after year n the firm is in stable growth, growing at a constant rate forever, which produces a terminal value. The cash flows are discounted at the matching discount rate (firm: cost of capital; equity: cost of equity) to give value (of firm or of equity).

## p.17 — The dividend discount model (diagram)
**Teaches:** Diagram of the DDM. Dividends = Net Income × Payout ratio. Expected dividends = Expected net income × (1 − Retention ratio), where the retention ratio is what is needed to sustain the expected growth in net income. Value of equity = PV of dividends during the high-growth period, plus value in stable growth. Discount at the cost of equity, the rate of return demanded by equity investors. Stable growth begins when net income and dividends grow at a constant rate forever.
**Formulas:** Dividends = Net Income × Payout ratio; Expected dividends = Expected net income × (1 − Retention ratio).

## p.18 — The "potential dividends" or FCFE model (diagram)
**Teaches:** Diagram of the FCFE model. FCFE (potential dividends) = Non-cash Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash working capital − (Debt repaid − Debt issued). Expected FCFE = Expected net income × (1 − Equity reinvestment rate), where equity reinvestment is what is needed to sustain growth in net income. Value of equity = PV of FCFE during high growth at the cost of equity, plus stable-growth value (when net income and FCFE grow at a constant rate forever); Value of equity in non-cash assets + Cash = Value of equity.
**Formulas:**
- FCFE = Non-cash Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash WC − (Debt repaid − Debt issued).
- Expected FCFE = Expected net income × (1 − Equity reinvestment rate).
- Value of equity = Value of equity in non-cash assets + Cash.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (net income, capex, depreciation, WC change, net debt flows, cost of equity → equity value); inputs are JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** fcfe, potential-dividends, equity-reinvestment

## p.19 — The FCFF model: valuing the entire business (diagram)
**Teaches:** Diagram of the FCFF model. FCFF = After-tax Operating Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash working capital. Expected FCFF = Expected operating income × (1 − Reinvestment rate). Value of operating assets = PV of FCFF during high growth discounted at the cost of capital (weighted average of costs of equity and debt), plus stable-growth value (when operating income and FCFF grow at constant rate forever). Then: Value of operating assets + Cash & non-operating assets − Debt = Value of equity.
**Formulas:**
- FCFF = After-tax Operating Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash WC.
- Expected FCFF = Expected operating income × (1 − Reinvestment rate).
- Value of equity = Value of operating assets + Cash & non-operating assets − Debt.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (operating income, reinvestment, cost of capital, cash, debt → equity value); inputs are JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** fcff, firm-valuation, reinvestment-rate, cost-of-capital

## p.21 — Estimating inputs: discount rates
**Teaches:** Discount rates matter in DCF but not as much as most analysts think. The discount rate should be consistent with both the riskiness and the type of cash flow being discounted. Three consistency dimensions: (1) Equity vs Firm — cash flows to equity are discounted at the cost of equity; cash flows to the firm at the cost of capital. (2) Currency — the currency of the cash flows must match the currency of the discount rate. (3) Nominal vs Real — nominal cash flows (reflecting expected inflation) require a nominal discount rate.
**Rules:** Match discount rate to cash flow on three dimensions: claim type (equity/firm), currency, and nominal/real.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** discount-rates, consistency, currency, nominal-vs-real

## p.22 — Risk in the DCF model
**Teaches:** In the DCF the numerator is the expectation of cash flows across all scenarios, good and bad. It incorporates all risks affecting the asset/business. The denominator is the risk-adjusted discount rate. That rate should reflect the risk perceived by the marginal investor in the company. The risk-adjusted cost of equity is built from a riskfree rate, relative risk, and an equity risk premium.
**Formulas:** Risk-Adjusted Cost of Equity = Riskfree rate in the currency of analysis + Relative risk of company/equity in question × Equity Risk Premium required for average-risk equity.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (riskfree rate, relative risk/beta, ERP → cost of equity); estimating each input is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** cost-of-equity, risk, marginal-investor, equity-risk-premium

## p.23 — Not all risk is created equal
**Teaches:** Three ways to classify risk. (1) Estimation vs Economic uncertainty: estimation uncertainty is the possibility of using the wrong model or estimating inputs incorrectly; economic uncertainty is that markets and economies change over time and even the best models miss unexpected changes. (2) Micro vs Macro uncertainty: micro is uncertainty about a firm's product market, competition, and management quality; macro is that the firm's fortunes are affected by the macroeconomic environment. (3) Discrete vs continuous uncertainty: discrete risks lie dormant and show up at points in time (e.g., a drug failing in the FDA approval pipeline, or nationalization of a Venezuelan company). Continuous risks, such as interest rate or economic growth changes, occur continuously and affect value as they happen.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (taxonomy)
**Tags:** risk-taxonomy, estimation-risk, macro-risk, discrete-risk

## p.24 — Risk and cost of equity: the role of the marginal investor
**Teaches:** Not all risk counts in the cost of equity. Although risk is usually defined as variance of actual returns around expected returns, risk-and-return models in finance assume the risk that should be rewarded (built into the discount rate) is the risk perceived by the marginal investor in the investment. The diversification effect: models assume the marginal investor is well diversified, so the only risk perceived is risk that cannot be diversified away (market/non-diversifiable risk). In effect it is primarily economic, macro, continuous risk that belongs in the cost of equity.
**Rules:** Only non-diversifiable (market) risk — primarily economic, macro, continuous risk — goes into the cost of equity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** marginal-investor, diversification, market-risk, cost-of-equity

## p.25 — The cost of equity: competing market-risk models
**Teaches:** Four competing models for expected return / cost of equity and the inputs each requires.
**Formulas:**
- CAPM: E(R) = R_f + beta × (R_m − R_f); inputs: riskfree rate, beta relative to market portfolio, market risk premium. (R_m = expected market return.)
- APM (arbitrage pricing): E(R) = R_f + sum over j of beta_j × (R_j − R_f); inputs: riskfree rate, number of factors, betas relative to each factor, factor risk premiums.
- Multi-factor: E(R) = R_f + sum over j of beta_j × (R_j − R_f) with specified macro factors; inputs: riskfree rate, macro factors, betas relative to macro factors, macroeconomic risk premiums.
- Proxy models: E(R) = a + sum over j of b_j × Y_j, where Y_j are proxies (e.g., size, book-to-market) and b_j regression coefficients; inputs: proxies and regression coefficients.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs for each model; choosing model and estimating inputs is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** capm, apm, multi-factor-models, proxy-models, cost-of-equity

## p.26 — Classic risk & return: cost of equity
**Teaches:** In the CAPM, Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate + Equity Beta × (Equity Risk Premium). **Formulas:** Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate + Equity Beta × (Equity Risk Premium).

## p.28 — The risk free rate: laying the foundations
**Teaches:** On a riskfree investment the actual return equals the expected return — there is no variance around the expected return. For an investment to be riskfree it must have no default risk and no reinvestment risk. Three consequences follow for estimating a riskfree rate. (1) Time horizon matters: the riskfree rate depends on when the cash flow is expected to occur and can vary across time. (2) Currencies matter: a riskfree rate is currency-specific and differs across currencies. (3) Not all government securities are riskfree: some governments face default risk, so their bond rates are not riskfree.
**Rules:** Riskfree requires no default risk AND no reinvestment risk; match the riskfree rate's horizon to the cash flow's timing and its currency to the cash flow's currency; do not treat all sovereign bonds as riskfree.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, default-risk, reinvestment-risk

## p.32 — Sovereign default spread: three paths to the same destination
**Teaches:** Three ways to estimate a sovereign default spread. (1) Sovereign dollar/euro-denominated bonds: for an emerging sovereign issuing US-dollar bonds, Default spread = Emerging government bond rate (in US$) − US Treasury bond rate of the same maturity. (2) CDS spreads: use the traded sovereign Credit Default Swap spread, perhaps adjusted for CDS market frictions (e.g., net of the US CDS spread). (3) Sovereign-rating-based spread: for countries with no dollar bonds and no CDS, use the average spread of other countries with the same sovereign rating.
**Formulas:** Default spread = Emerging Govt $-Bond Rate − US Treasury Bond rate (same maturity); Default spread = Sovereign CDS spread (adjusted for frictions).
**Rules:** Prefer market measures ($-bond spread or CDS) when available; fall back to rating-based average spreads otherwise.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given market data; choice among approaches is JUDGMENT.

## p.37 — Getting to a riskfree rate in a currency: Brazil example
**Teaches:** Worked example converting Brazil's nominal-reais government bond rate (7.02% on January 1, 2021) into a reais riskfree rate by subtracting a default spread estimated three ways, giving a range of answers.
**Formulas:** Riskfree rate in currency = Local-currency government bond rate − sovereign default spread.
**Example:** Brazil, Jan 1 2021, 10-yr nominal-reais bond rate 7.02%. Approach 1 (government $-bond spread): 2030 Brazil US$-bond spread over US Treasury = 2.05% → riskfree rate in R$ = 7.02% − 2.05% = 4.97%. Approach 2 (CDS): Brazil CDS spread net of US CDS = 1.92% → 7.02% − 1.92% = 5.10%. Approach 3 (rating-based): Moody's Ba2 local-currency rating → spread 2.65% → 7.02% − 2.65% = 4.47%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (bond rate, chosen spread → riskfree rate); choosing among the three spreads is JUDGMENT.

## p.39 — No default-free entity: choices with riskfree rates
**Teaches:** When no default-free entity exists in a currency, you have three choices. (1) Estimate a range for the local riskfree rate. Sub-approach 1: local-currency government bond rate − default spread for the government in local currency. Sub-approach 2: use forward exchange rates plus the riskless rate in an index currency (Euros or dollars) to back out the riskless rate in the local currency. (2) Do the analysis in real terms with a real riskfree rate. Get that rate from an inflation-indexed government bond if one exists; otherwise set it approximately equal to the long-term real growth rate of the economy where the valuation is done. (3) Do the analysis in a currency where a riskfree rate is available, such as US dollars or Euros.
**Formulas:** Local riskfree rate = Government bond rate in local currency − Default spread for government in local currency; Real riskfree rate ≈ long-term real growth rate of the economy (approximation when no indexed bond exists).
**Rules:** Options ranked: subtract default spread / use forward rates; or switch to real terms; or switch valuation currency to one with an observable riskfree rate.
**Compute:** MIXED — spread subtraction and forward-rate arithmetic are DETERMINISTIC; choosing the approach and the real-growth approximation is JUDGMENT.
