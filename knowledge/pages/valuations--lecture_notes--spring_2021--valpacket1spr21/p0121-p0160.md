## p.122 — I. Update Earnings

**Teaches:** Annual reports are often outdated when valuing companies; earnings and asset inputs should be updated using trailing 12-month data constructed from quarterly earnings reports, or informal/unofficial news reports if quarterly reports are unavailable. Updating matters most for smaller and more volatile firms and for firms that have undergone significant restructuring. Time saver: to build a trailing 12-month number you only need one 10K and one 10Q — use the year-to-date numbers from the 10Q.
**Formulas:** Trailing 12-month Revenue = Revenues (in last 10K) − Revenues from first 3 quarters of last year + Revenues from first 3 quarters of this year (example for a third-quarter 10Q; same construction works for any earnings line item).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (10K annual figure + prior-year YTD + current-year YTD → trailing 12-month figure).

## p.123 — II. Correcting Accounting Earnings

**Teaches:** Two corrections to accounting earnings. First, remove financial expenses mixed into operating expenses: a financial expense is any tax-deductible commitment that must be met regardless of operating results, where failure to meet it means losing control of the business — operating leases are the classic example; accounting treats them as operating expenses but they are really financial expenses. Reclassifying them has no effect on equity earnings but changes operating earnings. Second, remove capital expenses mixed into operating expenses: a capital expense is any expense expected to generate benefits over multiple periods — R&D is a capital expenditure, so operating income must be adjusted for it.
**Rules:** Classify an expense as financial if it is a tax-deductible commitment payable regardless of operating results whose non-payment causes loss of control of the business. Classify an expense as capital if it is expected to generate benefits over multiple periods.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of expenses).
**Tags:** operating-income, operating-leases, r-and-d, expense-classification

## p.125 — Dealing with Operating Lease Expenses

**Teaches:** Operating lease expenses are treated as operating expenses in computing operating income, but should be treated as financing expenses. The fix: compute the debt value of operating leases as the present value of future operating lease commitments discounted at the pre-tax cost of debt; when converting leases to debt you also create a counter asset of exactly the same value; and adjust operating earnings by adding back the lease expense and subtracting depreciation on the leased asset.
**Formulas:** Debt Value of Operating Leases = PV of future operating lease commitments discounted at the pre-tax cost of debt. Adjusted Operating Earnings = Operating Earnings + Operating Lease Expense − Depreciation on Leased Asset. Approximation: Adjusted Operating Earnings = Operating Earnings + Pre-tax Cost of Debt × PV of Operating Leases.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (lease commitment schedule + pre-tax cost of debt → lease debt value; operating earnings + lease expense + depreciation → adjusted operating earnings); choosing asset life/depreciation is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** operating-leases, lease-capitalization, adjusted-operating-income, cost-of-debt

## p.130 — R&D Expenses: Operating or Capital Expenses

**Teaches:** Accounting standards require treating R&D as an operating expense even though it is designed to generate future growth; it is more logical to treat it as a capital expenditure. To capitalize R&D: (1) specify an amortizable life for R&D (2–10 years); (2) collect past R&D expenses for as long as the amortizable life; (3) sum the unamortized portion of each year's R&D. With a 5-year life, the research asset = 1/5 of the R&D expense from five years ago + 2/5 of the expense from four years ago + ... + the full current-year expense.
**Formulas:** With straight-line amortization over life n: Research Asset = Σ over k = 0..n of R&D(−k) × (n − k)/n, where R&D(−k) is R&D expense k years ago (current year k=0 counts fully).
**Rules:** Amortizable life for R&D is chosen between 2 and 10 years depending on how long research takes to pay off in the business.
**Compute:** MIXED — choosing the amortizable life is JUDGMENT; given the life and past R&D expenses, the research asset and amortization are DETERMINISTIC.
**Tags:** r-and-d, capitalization, research-asset, amortization

## p.134 — IV. Accounting Malfeasance

**Teaches:** Even under the same accounting standards, firms differ in fidelity to those standards; aggressive firms show higher earnings than conservative firms. You cannot catch outright fraud. ## p.135 — V. Dealing with Negative or Abnormally Low Earnings (framework)

**Teaches:** Decision-tree framework: first ask why earnings are negative or abnormally low. Five causes: (1) temporary problems, (2) cyclicality (e.g., auto firm in recession), (3) life-cycle reasons (young firms, infrastructure firms), (4) leverage problems (otherwise healthy firm with too much debt), (5) long-term operating problems (significant production or cost problems). For temporary/cyclical causes: normalize earnings — if firm size has not changed significantly, use average dollar earnings over time (average net income if valuing equity, average EBIT if valuing the firm); if size has changed, apply the firm's average ROE to current book value of equity (equity valuation) or average ROC to current book value of capital (firm valuation). For life-cycle, leverage, and operating causes: value the firm with detailed cash flow forecasts starting from revenues, reducing or eliminating the problem over time — (a) structural problem: target stable-firm sector operating margins; (b) leverage problem: target a comfortable debt ratio by end of period (own optimal or industry average); (c) operating problem: target an industry-average operating margin.
**Rules:** If cause is temporary/cyclical → normalize (dollar averaging if size unchanged; average ROE×current BV of equity or average ROC×current BV of capital if size changed). If cause is life-cycle, leverage, or long-term operating → forecast from revenues and phase the problem out toward sector-margin / target-debt-ratio benchmarks.
**Formulas:** Normalized net income = Average ROE × Current book value of equity; Normalized EBIT (pre-tax operating income) = Average ROC × Current book value of capital.
**Compute:** MIXED — diagnosing the cause is JUDGMENT; once cause and averages are chosen, normalization arithmetic is DETERMINISTIC.
**Tags:** negative-earnings, normalization, cyclical-firms, young-firms, framework

## p.137 — What Tax Rate? (quiz)

**Teaches:** Quiz: which tax rate should be used to compute after-tax operating income? Options: (a) effective tax rate in the financial statements (taxes paid/taxable income); (b) taxes paid/EBIT; (c) marginal tax rate of the country in which the company operates; (d) weighted-average marginal tax rate across countries of operation; (e) none of the above; (f) any of the above, as long as you compute your after-tax cost of debt using the same tax rate. **Rules:** Consistency requirement: the tax rate used for after-tax operating income must match the one used for the after-tax cost of debt.

## p.138 — The Right Tax Rate to Use

**Teaches:** The real choice is between the effective and the marginal tax rate. In projections it is far safer to use the marginal tax rate, since the effective rate mostly reflects differences between accounting books and tax books. Using the marginal rate tends to understate after-tax operating income in earlier years, but the after-tax operating income is more accurate in later years. If you choose the effective rate, adjust it toward the marginal rate over time. While a weighted-average marginal rate across countries can be argued for, it is safest to use the marginal tax rate of the country (of domicile).
**Rules:** Default to the marginal tax rate for projections; if starting from the effective rate, converge it to the marginal rate over the forecast; prefer the domestic-country marginal rate over a weighted-average across countries.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (rate choice and convergence path).
**Tags:** tax-rate, marginal-tax-rate, effective-tax-rate, forecasting

## p.140 — Net Capital Expenditures

**Teaches:** Net capital expenditures are the difference between capital expenditures and depreciation; depreciation is a cash inflow that pays for some, much, or occasionally all of the capital expenditure. Net cap ex is a function of how fast the firm is growing or expects to grow — high-growth firms have much higher net cap ex than low-growth firms — so assumptions about net capital expenditures can never be made independently of assumptions about future growth.
**Formulas:** Net Capital Expenditures = Capital Expenditures − Depreciation.
**Rules:** Net cap ex assumptions must be tied to growth assumptions; do not set them independently.
**Compute:** MIXED — the subtraction is DETERMINISTIC; linking the level to growth is JUDGMENT.

## p.141 — Capital Expenditures Should Include

**Teaches:** Two items belong in capital expenditures beyond the reported number. ## p.144 — Working Capital Investments

**Teaches:** In accounting terms, working capital = current assets (inventory, cash, accounts receivable) − current liabilities (accounts payable, short-term debt, debt due within the year). A cleaner cash-flow definition — non-cash working capital — is non-cash current assets (inventory and accounts receivable) minus non-debt current liabilities (accounts payable): cash and debt are excluded because they are financing/valuation items, not operating ones. In some sectors, working capital investment is the bigger part of reinvestment.
**Formulas:** Accounting Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities. Non-cash Working Capital = (Inventory + Accounts Receivable) − Accounts Payable, i.e., non-cash current assets − non-debt current liabilities.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (balance sheet items → non-cash working capital).
**Tags:** working-capital, non-cash-working-capital, reinvestment

## p.145 — Working Capital: General Propositions

**Teaches:** Three propositions. (1) Detail: breaking working capital into components (inventory, deferred taxes, payables, etc.) is pointless unless you have specific information that lets you forecast the details. (2) Volatility: year-to-year changes in non-cash working capital are volatile, so building in only the most recent year's change is dangerous — better to estimate the change from working capital as a percent of sales, keeping an eye on industry averages. (3) Negative working capital: some firms have negative non-cash working capital; assuming it persists generates positive cash flows that grow more positive as growth increases (a questionable assumption to lock in forever).
**Rules:** Forecast working capital changes as a percent of sales rather than extrapolating the latest year's change; benchmark against industry averages; be cautious assuming negative working capital persists indefinitely.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing the WC-to-sales assumption).
**Tags:** working-capital, forecasting, negative-working-capital

## p.148 — Dividends and Cash Flows to Equity

**Teaches:** In the strictest sense, the only cash flow an investor receives from equity in a publicly traded firm is the dividend. But actual dividends are set by managers and may be much lower than potential dividends (what could have been paid out), because managers are conservative and smooth dividends, and like holding cash for unforeseen contingencies and investment opportunities. When actual dividends are less than potential dividends, a dividend-only model understates the true value of equity.
**Rules:** If actual dividends < potential dividends, a dividend discount model understates equity value — use potential dividends (FCFE) instead.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dividends, fcfe, potential-dividends, equity-valuation

## p.149 — Measuring Potential Dividends

**Teaches:** Earnings are not potential dividends, for three reasons: earnings are not cash flows (non-cash revenues and expenses are in the calculation); even if they were, a firm paying out all earnings could not invest in new assets and could not grow; and discounting earnings as if they were cash flows overestimates equity value. Potential dividends are the cash flows left over after the firm makes the investments needed to create future growth and after net debt repayments (debt repayments − new debt issues). The common split of capital expenditures into discretionary and non-discretionary loses its basis once future growth is built into the valuation — reinvestment supporting forecast growth is not discretionary.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual).
**Tags:** potential-dividends, fcfe, earnings-vs-cash-flows

## p.150 — Estimating Cash Flows: FCFE

**Teaches:** The definition of free cash flow to equity for a levered firm, built down from net income. Preferred dividends are ignored here; if preferred stock exists, preferred dividends must also be netted out.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income − (Capital Expenditures − Depreciation) − Changes in non-cash Working Capital − (Principal Repayments − New Debt Issues). If preferred stock exists: also subtract preferred dividends.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, cap ex, depreciation, ΔWC, debt repayments/issues → FCFE).
**Tags:** fcfe, cash-flow-estimation, net-income

## p.151 — Estimating FCFE When Leverage Is Stable

**Teaches:** Shortcut FCFE formula assuming the firm finances a constant fraction DR (debt-to-capital ratio) of its reinvestment with debt: equity bears only (1−DR) of net cap ex and working capital needs. This is equivalent to assuming proceeds from new debt issues = principal repayments + DR×(net reinvestment). When computing FCFE, use the book-value debt-to-capital ratio when looking back in time, but it can be replaced with the market-value debt-to-capital ratio looking forward.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income − (1 − DR) × (Capital Expenditures − Depreciation) − (1 − DR) × Working Capital Needs, where DR = Debt/Capital ratio. Implied: New debt issues = Principal Repayments + DR × (Capital Expenditures − Depreciation + Working Capital Needs).
**Rules:** Use book-value DR for historical FCFE; market-value DR is acceptable for forward-looking FCFE.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, net cap ex, ΔWC, DR → FCFE).
**Tags:** fcfe, stable-leverage, debt-ratio

## p.152 — FCFE from the Statement of Cash Flows

**Teaches:** The statement of cash flows can be used to back into FCFE if you navigate it carefully rather than trusting it fully. Two routes: build it from cash flow from operations, or reconstruct it from what the firm did with the cash.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Cash Flow from Operations − Capital Expenditures (from investing section) − Cash Acquisitions (from investing section) − (Debt Repaid − Debt Issued) (from financing section). Alternatively: FCFE = Dividends + Stock Buybacks − Stock Issuances + Change in Cash Balance.

## p.154 — Estimating FCFE: Disney (example)

**Teaches:** Worked FCFE example for Disney (1997) using the stable-leverage shortcut, showing FCFE ($704m) was about double the dividends actually paid ($345m) — Disney could have paid out far more.
**Example:** Inputs: Net income $1,533m; capital spending $1,746m; depreciation $1,134m; increase in non-cash working capital $477m; debt-to-capital ratio DR = 23.83%. Computation: FCFE = 1,533 − (1,746 − 1,134)×(1 − 0.2383) − 477×(1 − 0.2383) = 1,533 − 465.90 − 363.33 = $704 million. Dividends paid = $345 million.

## p.159 — The Value of Growth

**Teaches:** When valuing a company it is easy to treat growth as an unambiguous good — higher growth means higher value. But growth is a double-edged sword: the good side is that it pushes up revenues and operating income (perhaps at different rates depending on how margins evolve); the bad side is that money must be set aside as reinvestment to create that growth. The net effect of growth on value is whether the good outweighs the bad — growth can be good, bad, or neutral for value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual).
**Tags:** growth, reinvestment, value-creation

## p.160 — Ways of Estimating Growth in Earnings

**Teaches:** Three ways to estimate earnings growth. (1) Look at the past: historical growth in earnings per share is usually a good starting point. (2) Look at what others are estimating: analysts estimate EPS growth for many firms, and it is useful to know their estimates. (3) Look at fundamentals: ultimately all earnings growth traces to two fundamentals — how much the firm is investing in new projects, and what returns those projects are making for the firm (reinvestment × return on investment).
