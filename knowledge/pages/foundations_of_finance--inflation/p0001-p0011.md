## p.2 — What is inflation?
**Teaches:** Inflation is a general rise in prices. Deflation is a general fall in the price level. Some prices can rise with no overall inflation, if the rise is offset by falling prices elsewhere. These relative price changes do not show up as inflation. But they still affect people who buy more or less of the goods whose prices moved. Finance cares about inflation because it changes how we value a cash flow today versus an equal cash flow later.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (definitional)
**Tags:** inflation, definitions, price-level, time-value

## p.3 — How is inflation measured?
**Teaches:** To measure inflation, first pick a perspective: consumers or producers. Then pick a basket of goods and services to track price-level changes. In most of the world, a government or its agency estimates inflation. Each one often uses a different approach. The US government measures inflation in multiple ways. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures change over time in prices paid for a market basket of consumer goods/services. The Producer Price Index (PPI) measures change in prices received by producers of goods and services. The GDP deflator is calculated from nominal and real GDP growth numbers, and it covers price changes in all goods and services.

## p.4 — Inflation measurement: errors and biases
**Teaches:** Measured inflation can differ from actual inflation for three reasons. (1) Basket of goods/services: every measure tracks prices on a basket meant to reflect actual usage. If the basket is mis-specified or mis-measured, measured inflation drifts from actual inflation. (2) Sources for prices: how and when prices are observed affects the measured number. (3) Other biases: most inflation rates are measured by governments or their agencies. Governments care about the reported number, and most prefer lower rates. That creates pressure on those measuring inflation to skew the numbers.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative assessment of measurement bias)
**Tags:** inflation, measurement-bias, index-construction, government-statistics

## p.6 — What causes inflation?
**Teaches:** Three drivers of inflation. (1) Government spending: when governments must spend money they do not have in large amounts — to fend off a crisis or fund a war — inflation can surge. (2) Real economic growth: high growth brings more price pressure. Consumers have more spendable income, and production may not keep up. (3) Monetary policy: inflation is at root a monetary phenomenon. Governments that print money more freely tend to see more inflation, at least in the long term.

## p.7 — Real versus nominal
**Teaches:** We live in a nominal world. Wages, taxes, and returns are all computed in nominal terms. But inflation over the period affects what those wages, taxes, and returns are really worth. The same wages, taxes, and returns are worth a lot less over time under high inflation than under low inflation or deflation. That is why inflation acts as a hidden tax.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual)
**Tags:** inflation, real-vs-nominal, purchasing-power

## p.8 — Converting nominal to real returns
**Teaches:** The nominal return on an investment includes inflation. In the simple form, nominal return equals real return plus inflation. When compounding matters, the precise form multiplies (1 + real return) by (1 + inflation). Given a nominal return, converting to a real return is a simple division.
**Formulas:**
- Approximation: Nominal Return = Real Return + Inflation. (Nominal Return = return in current dollars; Real Return = return in purchasing-power terms; Inflation = inflation rate over the period.)
- Exact (compounding): (1 + Nominal Return) = (1 + Real Return) * (1 + Inflation Rate).
- Real Return = (1 + Nominal Return) / (1 + Inflation Rate) - 1.

## p.10 — US equities: quantifying the inflation effect ($100 invested in 1928)
**Teaches:** Compounding makes the inflation drag on long-horizon equity wealth enormous. $100 invested in US stocks in 1928 grows to $502,417.21 by 2019 in nominal terms. In real terms — adjusted for inflation, in 1928 purchasing power — it grows to only $33,953.22, roughly 15x less. The chart shows the nominal line exploding upward after the 1980s. The real line stays almost flat. This is inflation acting as a "hidden tax" on returns.
**Example:** Invest $100 in US stocks in 1928; compound annually through 2019. Nominal ending value = $502,417.21; real ending value = $33,953.22.

## p.11 — Nominal to real cash flows and growth rates
**Teaches:** Converting nominal to real works the same way for growth rates and cash flows as for returns. For growth rates, use the shortcut (subtract inflation) or the precise version (divide by 1 + inflation). For a nominal cash flow in future year t, divide by the inflation rate compounded over t years to get the real cash flow.
**Formulas:**
- Shortcut: Real Growth Rate = Nominal Growth Rate - Inflation Rate.
- Exact: Real Growth Rate = (1 + Nominal Growth Rate) / (1 + Inflation Rate) - 1.
- Real Cash Flow_t = Nominal Cash Flow_t / (1 + Inflation Rate)^t, where t = number of years in the future the cash flow occurs and Inflation Rate = expected annual inflation rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: nominal growth rate or nominal cash flow, inflation rate, year t → real growth rate or real cash flow)
**Tags:** inflation, real-cash-flows, growth-rates, valuation
