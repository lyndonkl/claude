## p.2 — It's always about money

**Teaches:** In business, the old saying "it's always about the money" is always true, however crass it sounds. No matter what aspect of business or investing is being discussed, and no matter how fuzzy or qualitative that aspect is, its ultimate effect shows up in earnings and cash flows. Finance is therefore not just another function of business (like marketing or operations) — it is the measuring rod for all of business, because every business decision is ultimately judged by its effect on earnings and cash flows.

## p.4 — 1. The Concept of a Business

**Teaches:** Understanding how a business is structured in finance is central to recognizing the challenges you will face in running and valuing a business. On the asset side: finance, like accounting, breaks a business down into its assets, but the focus in finance is always on the future. The value of assets in finance is not what you invested in them (historical cost). It is what they will generate as earnings/cash flows in the future. On the financing side, finance keeps it simple: there are only two ways to fund a business, debt and equity.
**Rules:** Value assets on expected future earnings/cash flows, not on what was invested in them. All financing is classified as either debt or equity — there is no third funding category.

## p.5 — 2. The Importance of Cash Flows

**Teaches:** The single defining feature of finance is its dependence on cash flows, rather than the more widely used accounting measures of performance such as earnings. Earnings vs cash flows: understanding how to measure cash flows and why they can differ from earnings is the first step toward financial literacy. Forms of cash flows: the next step toward valuing cash flows is recognizing the different forms they take. These forms are contractually set cash flows (e.g., debt payments), residual cash flows (e.g., equity claims — whatever is left over), and contingent claim cash flows (option-like payoffs that occur only if some event happens).
**Rules:** Cash flow classification taxonomy: (a) contractually set, (b) residual, (c) contingent claim.

## p.6 — 3. The Definition and Measurement of Risk

**Teaches:** The notion that human beings are risk averse is central to how cash flows are valued in finance. Adjusting value for risk requires three steps. First, a definition of risk that reflects its two-sided nature — risk yields both positive (upside) and negative (downside) outcomes. Second, a judgment on whose perspective on risk matters when it comes to value. Third, a conversion of that risk definition into a risk measure that can then be used to adjust value. The history of risk measurement over the last few centuries strongly correlates with the development of finance as a discipline.
**Rules:** Three-step framework for risk adjustment: define risk (two-sided) → decide whose perspective matters → convert to a usable risk measure.

## p.7 — 4. The Time Value of Money

**Teaches:** The question: a dollar today is worth more than a dollar a year from now — the nostrum with which every finance class begins — but why? The intuition behind the time value of money is both simple and all-encompassing: it comes from (a) the preference for current consumption, (b) concerns about pricing power (inflation eroding purchasing power), and (c) the risk in the cash flows. The mechanics of computing present value are also simple but ubiquitous throughout finance.
**Rules:** Three drivers of the time value of money: preference for current consumption, expected inflation/pricing-power concerns, and risk in the cash flows.

## p.8 — 5. Valuation

**Teaches:** Valuation is simple: it is just an extension of the present value concept — the value of an asset or business is the present value of the expected cash flows on that asset or business. That said, the mechanics of valuation differ by the type of cash flow being valued.
**Rules:** Match valuation mechanics to cash flow type. (1) Contractually fixed cash flows: discount the promised cash flows at a discount rate adjusted for default risk. (2) Residual cash flows: discount expected cash flows at a discount rate that reflects the risk investors perceive in those cash flows. (3) Contingent cash flows: value the asset using an option pricing model.
**Compute:** MIXED — once cash flows and discount rates are given, discounting to present value is deterministic; choosing the cash flow type, estimating expected cash flows, and setting risk-adjusted discount rates is judgment.
**Tags:** valuation, present-value, discount-rate, default-risk, option-pricing

## p.9 — 6. Trading and Frictions

**Teaches:** Why markets matter: financial markets are the lubricants that allow businesses to raise capital and fund investments, and they are also the laboratories that generate the data used in measuring risk and value. Investors are in markets to make returns; unsurprisingly, they would like to invest little or nothing, take no risk, and make money — that is the essence of arbitrage. While many claim to find arbitrage opportunities in markets, two frictions get in the way: first, trading has costs; second, the government collects taxes.
**Rules:** Arbitrage definition: invest little or nothing, take no risk, and still make money. Two frictions that undermine claimed arbitrage: trading costs and taxes.
