## p.3 — Underlying theme: the real-options premium

**Teaches:** Traditional discounted cash flow (DCF) models underestimate investments that contain embedded options. Four such options exist: delay/defer the investment (delay); adjust production schedules as prices change (flexibility); expand into new markets or products after favorable early outcomes (expansion); and stop or abandon investments if early outcomes are unfavorable (abandonment). Real-option advocates therefore argue that you should pay a premium on top of DCF value estimates. The whole module asks whether and when that premium is justified.

**Tags:** real-options, dcf, option-premium, framing

## p.5 — The same investment becomes a good one (staged tree)

**Teaches:** The same type of investment becomes good when it is staged so that you can learn and respond: a first stage with probability 3/4 of an interim gain (+20) and 1/4 of a small loss (−20); conditional on the good interim outcome, a second stage with probability 2/3 of +80 and 1/3 of −100. Because the big downside is only taken after observing a favorable first stage (and the firm can stop after a bad first stage), the staged expected value turns positive. The point: optionality (the ability to abandon/continue based on information) converts a bad static investment into a good one.
**Example:** Stage 1: 3/4 → +20, 1/4 → −20 (stop). Stage 2 (after +20): 2/3 → +80, 1/3 → −100.

## p.6 — Three basic questions for real options

**Rules:** Only pay a real-option premium if all three questions can be answered affirmatively.

## p.7 — When is there an option embedded in an action?

**Teaches:** An option gives the holder the right to buy or sell a specified quantity of an underlying asset at a fixed price (the strike or exercise price) at or before the option's expiration date. For a real option to exist there must be: (a) a clearly defined underlying asset whose value changes over time in unpredictable ways, and (b) payoffs contingent on a specified event occurring within a finite period.
**Rules:** Option exists only if you can identify (1) an underlying asset with unpredictable value changes and (2) a contingent payoff within a finite time period.

**Tags:** real-options, option-definition, contingent-claims

## p.10 — When does the option have significant economic value?

**Teaches:** For an option to have significant economic value there must be a restriction on competition in the event of the contingency: in a perfectly competitive product market, no contingency — no matter how positive — generates positive net present value, because competitors compete excess returns away. Real options are most valuable at the limit of exclusivity (you and only you can exploit the contingency) and become progressively less valuable as barriers to competition weaken.
**Rules:** Exclusivity test: full exclusivity → full option value; no barriers to competition → zero option value; partial barriers → scaled-down option value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** real-options, exclusivity, competitive-advantage

## p.11 — Determinants of option value

**Teaches:** Six variables determine option value. Underlying-asset variables: (1) value of the underlying asset — as it rises, calls become more valuable and puts less valuable; (2) variance of that value — higher variance raises the value of BOTH calls and puts, because options have limited downside and gain from volatility upside; (3) expected dividends — they reduce the price-appreciation of the asset, lowering call value and raising put value. Option variables: (4) strike price — a lower strike makes calls more valuable and puts less valuable; (5) life of the option — both calls and puts gain from a longer life. Finally (6) the level of interest rates — as rates increase, the right to buy at a fixed future price becomes more valuable (calls up) and the right to sell becomes less valuable (puts down).
**Rules:** Call value: increases with S, variance, life, interest rates; decreases with K and dividends. Put value: increases with K, variance, life, dividends; decreases with S and interest rates.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (directional comparative statics; the actual pricing is deterministic given a model)
**Tags:** options, option-value-determinants, comparative-statics

## p.12 — When can option pricing models value real options?

**Teaches:** Option pricing models rest on the notion of a replicating portfolio, so they are best suited to real options where: the underlying asset is traded (giving observable prices and volatility, and making replication possible), an active marketplace exists for the option itself, and the cost of exercising is known with some certainty. When these conditions fail (as they typically do for real assets), value estimates become far more imprecise, and estimated value can deviate much more dramatically from market price because arbitrage is difficult.
**Rules:** Trust option-model output for real options roughly in proportion to: (a) underlying asset traded, (b) option itself tradable, (c) exercise cost known.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** real-options, option-pricing, replicating-portfolio, arbitrage

## p.13 — Creating a replicating portfolio

**Teaches:** The objective of a replicating portfolio is to combine riskfree borrowing/lending with a position in the underlying asset to produce exactly the same cash flows as the option. A call = Borrowing + Buying D (delta) units of the underlying stock; a put = Selling short D units of the underlying + Lending. The number of shares bought or sold is the option delta. By arbitrage, the value of the option must equal the value of its replicating portfolio.
**Formulas:** Call = Borrowing + D × (underlying stock); Put = Lending − D × (underlying stock), where D = option delta = number of shares held in the replicating portfolio.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given payoff states → delta and borrowing, hence option value)
**Tags:** options, replicating-portfolio, delta, arbitrage

## p.14 — The binomial option pricing model (worked tree)

**Teaches:** Two-period binomial pricing of a call by backward induction with replicating portfolios at each node. At each node solve two equations (one per successor state) of the form S_up·D − (1+r)·B = C_up and S_down·D − (1+r)·B = C_down for D (shares) and B (borrowing); node call value = D·S − B.
**Example:** Call with strike K = $40, t = 2 periods, r = 11% (per period discount factor 1.11). Stock starts at 50, moves to 70 or 35; from 70 to 100 or 50; from 35 to 50 or 25. Terminal call values: at 100 → 60; at 50 → 10; at 25 → 0. Upper node (S=70): solve 100D − 1.11B = 60 and 50D − 1.11B = 10 → D = 1, B = 36.04, Call = 1×70 − 36.04 = 33.96. Lower node (S=35): solve 50D − 1.11B = 10 and 25D − 1.11B = 0 → D = 0.4, B = 9.01, Call = 0.4×35 − 9.01 = 4.99. Root (S=50): solve 70D − 1.11B = 33.96 and 35D − 1.11B = 4.99 → D = 0.8278, B = 21.61, Call = 0.8278×50 − 21.61 = 19.42.
**Formulas:** At each node: S_u·D − (1+r)·B = C_u; S_d·D − (1+r)·B = C_d; Call = D·S − B. Symbols: S = current stock price, S_u/S_d = up/down prices, C_u/C_d = option values in up/down states, D = delta (shares bought), B = amount borrowed, r = riskless rate per period.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (tree prices, strike, r → call value via backward induction)
**Tags:** binomial-model, option-pricing, replicating-portfolio, worked-example

## p.15 — The limiting distributions

**Teaches:** As the binomial time interval shrinks (t → 0), the limiting price distribution takes one of two forms: if price changes become smaller as intervals shrink, the limit is the normal distribution and the price process is continuous; if price changes remain large, the limit is the Poisson distribution, which allows price jumps. Black-Scholes applies only in the continuous/normal limit — it explicitly assumes a continuous price process with no jumps.
**Rules:** Use Black-Scholes only when the price process is continuous (no jumps); jump processes require Poisson-based (jump) models.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** black-scholes, binomial-model, price-process, distributions

## p.17 — The Black-Scholes model (formula)

**Teaches:** The Black-Scholes call value formula, and the fact that a replicating portfolio is embedded in it: to replicate the call you buy N(d1) shares of stock (N(d1) is the option delta) and borrow K·e^(−rt)·N(d2).
**Formulas:** Value of call = S·N(d1) − K·e^(−rt)·N(d2), where d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r + sigma^2/2)·t] / (sigma·sqrt(t)) and d2 = d1 − sigma·sqrt(t). Symbols: S = current value of underlying asset, K = strike price, t = time to expiration, r = riskless rate, sigma = standard deviation of ln(value) of underlying, N(·) = cumulative standard normal distribution.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, r, sigma → call value)
**Tags:** black-scholes, call-option, formula, delta

## p.19 — Adjusting Black-Scholes for dividends

**Teaches:** If the dividend yield y (= dividends / current value of the asset) of the underlying is expected to stay constant over the option's life, Black-Scholes can be modified to account for dividends by discounting the asset value at the dividend yield; the same framework yields the put value.
**Formulas:** Dividend-adjusted call: C = S·e^(−yt)·N(d1) − K·e^(−rt)·N(d2), where d1 = [ln(S/K) + (r − y + sigma^2/2)·t] / (sigma·sqrt(t)) and d2 = d1 − sigma·sqrt(t). Put: P = K·e^(−rt)·(1 − N(d2)) − S·e^(−yt)·(1 − N(d1)). Symbols: S = current asset value, K = strike, t = time to expiration, r = riskless rate, y = dividend yield = dividends / current asset value, sigma = std dev of ln(asset value), N(·) = cumulative standard normal.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, r, y, sigma → call and put values)
**Tags:** black-scholes, dividend-adjustment, put-option, formula

## p.20 — Choice of option pricing models

**Teaches:** Most practitioners valuing real options prefer the binomial model over Black-Scholes because (a) early exercise is the rule rather than the exception with real options, and (b) underlying asset values are generally discontinuous (jumps). A binomial tree with outcomes at each node looks a great deal like a capital-budgeting decision tree, which raises the question of when and why the two approaches yield different value estimates.
**Rules:** Prefer binomial over Black-Scholes for real options when early exercise matters or the underlying value process has jumps.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** binomial-model, black-scholes, model-choice, real-options

## p.21 — The decision tree alternative

**Teaches:** Traditional decision tree analysis discounts every branch's cash flows at one cost of capital and uses probabilities to compute an expected value; those values generally differ from option pricing model values. But if you modify decision tree analysis — either using different discount rates at each node to reflect where you are in the tree (the Copeland solution), or discounting each branch at the riskfree rate, computing the probability-weighted expected value, and then adjusting that expected value for the market risk of the investment — decision trees can yield the same values as option pricing models.
**Rules:** A single-discount-rate decision tree ≠ option value; node-specific discount rates (Copeland) or riskfree discounting + risk adjustment can reconcile the two.
**Compute:** MIXED (tree arithmetic is deterministic; choosing node discount rates / risk adjustments is judgment)
**Tags:** decision-trees, option-pricing, discount-rates, copeland

## p.23 — Key tests for real options

**Teaches:** Three sequential tests before paying for a real option. (1) Option test: is there an option embedded — can you identify the underlying asset and specify the contingency under which you get a payoff? (2) Exclusivity test: if you have exclusivity there is option value; if not, there is none; if in between, scale the value down. (3) Pricing test: can an option pricing model be used — is the underlying asset traded, can the option itself be bought and sold, and is the cost of exercising known and clear?
**Rules:** Exclusivity: yes → full option value; no → zero; partial → scaled value. Use an option pricing model only if the underlying is traded, the option is tradable, and exercise cost is clear.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** real-options, checklist, exclusivity, pricing-test

## p.24 — I. Options in projects/investments/acquisitions

**Teaches:** Traditional investment analysis is static and fails to capture the options embedded in investments: (1) the option to delay taking an investment (when the firm has exclusive rights to it) until a later date; (2) the option to expand — taking one investment may allow the firm to take advantage of other future opportunities; (3) the option to abandon an investment if cash flows do not measure up. ## p.32 — Valuing a firm with patents

**Teaches:** A firm with many patents can be valued as the sum of three parts. The third term measures the firm's efficiency at converting R&D into commercial products; if the firm merely earns its cost of capital on research, that term is zero. Caution: do not double count — if patents are valued as options, do not also build a high growth rate into the DCF of existing products.
**Formulas:** Value of Firm = Value of commercial products (DCF value) + Value of existing patents (option pricing) + (Value of new patents to be obtained in the future − Cost of obtaining these patents).
**Rules:** If the firm earns exactly its cost of capital on research, the future-patents term = 0. Avoid double counting: option-valuing patents excludes assuming high growth from those patents in the DCF.
**Compute:** MIXED (summation is deterministic; each component requires estimation)
**Tags:** patents, firm-valuation, dcf, double-counting

## p.33 — Value of Biogen's existing products

**Teaches:** Biogen had two commercial products at the time of valuation (a Hepatitis B drug and Intron), licensed to other pharmaceutical firms. The license fees were contractually guaranteed, so they were discounted at the pre-tax cost of debt of the guarantors (7%) rather than a risky cost of capital.
**Formulas:** PV of an n-year annuity = CF × (1 − (1+r)^(−n)) / r, with CF = annual cash flow, r = discount rate, n = years.
**Example:** License fees of $50 million after-tax per year for 12 years, discounted at 7%: PV of License Fees = $50 million × (1 − (1.07)^(−12)) / 0.07 = $397.13 million.
**Rules:** Contractually guaranteed cash flows are discounted at the guarantor's pre-tax cost of debt, not the firm's cost of capital.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (CF, r, n → PV)
**Tags:** biogen, annuity, cost-of-debt, licensing

## p.34 — Value of Biogen's future R&D (assumptions)

## p.37 — The real options test applied to patents and technology

**Teaches:** Applying the three real-options tests to patents. Option test: underlying asset = the product the patent would generate; contingency: payoff = PV of CFs from development − cost of development if positive, else 0. Exclusivity test: patents restrict competitors from developing similar products, but do NOT restrict competitors from developing other products to treat the same disease — so exclusivity is partial. Pricing test: the underlying asset (the product) is not traded. You must therefore estimate present values and volatilities yourself, and you cannot construct replicating positions or arbitrage. The option (patent) is bought and sold, though less frequently than oil reserves or mines. The exercise cost (converting the patent to commercial production) can be estimated fairly precisely by experienced drug firms. Conclusion: you can estimate the real-option value of a patent, but the estimate is only as good as your capital budgeting. The approach works best for a publicly traded firm deriving most of its value from one or a few patents. In that case you can use the firm's market value and its variance in the option model.
**Rules:** Patent option valuation works best when: firm is publicly traded and most value comes from one or a few patents (use market value and its variance as option inputs).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** patents, real-options-test, exclusivity, pricing-test

## p.38 — Example 2: Valuing natural resource options

**Teaches:** In a natural resource investment the underlying asset is the resource itself; its value depends on two variables — the quantity of the resource available and the resource's price. Development carries a cost, and the profit to the owner is the difference between the value of the extracted asset and the development cost, giving the undeveloped reserve a call-option payoff.
**Formulas:** Payoff on natural resource investment = V − X if V > X; = 0 if V ≤ X. Symbols: V = estimated value of the resource; X = cost of development.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (V, X → payoff); estimating V and X is judgment
**Tags:** natural-resources, real-options, call-option, reserves
