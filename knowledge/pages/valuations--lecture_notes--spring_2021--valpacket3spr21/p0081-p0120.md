## p.81 — Eurotunnel: other inputs for equity-as-option valuation

**Teaches:** Continuation of the Eurotunnel example (valuing equity in a deeply troubled firm as a call option on the firm). To value equity as an option you need the variance of FIRM value, not just equity. When the firm has traded stock and traded bonds, firm-value variance can be estimated from four pieces: stock variance, bond variance, their correlation, and the debt/equity weights over the estimation period. Eurotunnel stock (London Exchange) had an annualized standard deviation of ln(prices) of 41%. Its traded bonds had an annualized std deviation of ln(price) of 17%. The correlation between stock and bond price changes was 0.5. The average debt proportion 1992–1996 was 85%. The riskless rate used is the 15-year bond rate of 6% (a bond of duration ~11 years chosen to match the option's life).
**Formulas:** Annualized variance in firm value = w_E^2 * sigma_E^2 + w_D^2 * sigma_D^2 + 2 * w_E * w_D * rho_ED * sigma_E * sigma_D. Here w_E = equity weight (0.15); w_D = debt weight (0.85); sigma_E = std dev of equity (0.41); sigma_D = std dev of debt (0.17); rho_ED = correlation between stock and bond price changes (0.5). Computation: (0.15)^2*(0.41)^2 + (0.85)^2*(0.17)^2 + 2*(0.15)*(0.85)*(0.5)*(0.41)*(0.17) = 0.0335.
**Rules:** Match the maturity of the riskless-rate instrument to the life of the option (here ~11-year duration bond for an ~11-year option).
**Example:** Eurotunnel: sigma_stock = 41%, sigma_bond = 17%, correlation = 0.5, debt weight = 85% → firm-value variance = 0.0335; riskless rate = 6%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (stock variance, bond variance, correlation, weights → firm value variance).

## p.82 — Valuing Eurotunnel equity and debt with Black-Scholes

**Teaches:** With the inputs assembled, equity in the deeply indebted Eurotunnel is valued as a call option on firm value using Black-Scholes. Even though firm value (£2,312m) is far below the face value of debt (£8,865m), equity has positive value (£122m) because of option time value — the chance firm value rises above the debt over the option's long life. The implied value of debt is firm value minus equity value (2312 − 122 = £2,190m), and the appropriate interest rate on the debt can be backed out from face value versus market value of debt over the debt's duration.
**Formulas:** Value of call (equity) = S * N(d1) − K * exp(−r*t) * N(d2). Inputs: S = value of the firm = £2,312 million; K = face value of outstanding debt = £8,865 million; t = weighted average duration of debt = 10.93 years; sigma^2 = variance in firm value = 0.0335; r = riskless rate = 6%. Computed: d1 = −0.8337, N(d1) = 0.2023; d2 = −1.4392, N(d2) = 0.0751. Value of call = 2312*(0.2023) − 8865*exp(−0.06*10.93)*(0.0751) = £122 million. Appropriate interest rate on debt = (Face value of debt / Market value of debt)^(1/t) − 1 = (8865/2190)^(1/10.93) − 1 = 13.65%.
**Example:** Eurotunnel: S = £2,312m, K = £8,865m, t = 10.93 yrs, sigma^2 = 0.0335, r = 6% → equity = £122m; debt market value = 2312 − 122 = £2,190m; implied debt interest rate = 13.65%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (S, K, t, sigma^2, r → equity value, debt value, implied debt yield via Black-Scholes).

## p.83 — Closing thoughts on real options

**Teaches:** Real options are everywhere, but most have no significant economic value because there is no exclusivity in exercising them. When options do have significant value, the binomial-model inputs can be used in traditional approaches (decision trees) to yield equivalent value. The real payoff from real-options thinking is (a) recognizing that building flexibility and escape hatches into large decisions has value, and (b) insight into how and why companies behave as they do in investment analysis and capital-structure choices.
**Rules:** A real option has significant economic value only if there is exclusivity in using it.

## p.86 — Long-term post-merger evidence is negative too

**Teaches:** Managers claim markets can't see long-term merger benefits, but evidence rejects this. McKinsey studied acquisition programs asking (1) did return on capital invested in acquisitions exceed the cost of capital, and (2) did acquisitions help the parent outperform competition — half of all programs failed one test and a quarter failed both. KPMG's study of global acquisitions concludes most mergers (>80%) fail, with merged companies doing worse than their peer group. Many acquisitions are reversed quickly: about 20% of acquisitions made 1982–1986 were divested by 1988, and over ten-plus-year horizons the divestiture rate rises to almost 50%.
**Data:** Key statistics — McKinsey: 50% of acquisition programs fail at least one value test, 25% fail both; KPMG: >80% of mergers underperform peer group; divestitures: ~20% of 1982–86 acquisitions divested by 1988, ~50% divested over 10+ years.

## p.93 — Lesson 1: Don't transfer your risk characteristics to the target

**Teaches:** The cost of equity used for an investment should reflect the risk of the INVESTMENT, not the risk characteristics of the investor who raised the funds. Risky businesses cannot become safe just because the buyer is in a safe business. So the target on p.91–92 is still worth 60, discounted at its own 20% cost of equity, regardless of the acquirer's 10% cost of equity.
**Rules:** Always discount target cash flows at a rate reflecting the target's risk, never the acquirer's.

## p.95 — Lesson 2: Render unto the target what is the target's — not a penny more

**Teaches:** An acquirer can often borrow much more than the target can on its own, and at a much lower rate. ## p.99 — The value of synergy: taxonomy

**Teaches:** Synergy is created when two firms are combined. It can be operating or financial. Operating synergy comes in two forms. Strategic advantages: higher returns on new investments (higher ROC → higher growth rate); more new investments (higher reinvestment → higher growth rate); more sustainable excess returns (longer growth period). Economies of scale: cost savings in current operations (higher margin, higher base-year EBIT). Financial synergy has three parts. Tax benefits: lower taxes on earnings due to higher depreciation or operating loss carryforwards. Added debt capacity: higher debt ratio and lower cost of capital. Diversification?: may reduce cost of equity, but only for a private or closely held firm. Each synergy type maps to a specific valuation input it changes.
**Rules:** Map each claimed synergy to the valuation input it affects. The inputs: higher ROC/growth rate; higher reinvestment; longer growth period; higher margin/base EBIT; lower taxes; higher debt ratio/lower cost of capital. Diversification lowers cost of equity only for private/closely held firms.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification framework).
**Tags:** synergy, operating-synergy, financial-synergy, taxonomy, valuation-inputs

## p.100 — Valuing synergy: three-step procedure

**Teaches:** A three-step process for valuing synergy. (1) Value the merging firms independently. Discount each firm's expected cash flows at its own weighted average cost of capital. (2) Add the two standalone values to get the no-synergy value of the combined firm. (3) Build the effects of synergy into expected growth rates and cash flows, then re-value the combined firm with synergy. The difference is the value of synergy.
**Formulas:** Value of Synergy = Value of the combined firm with synergy − Value of the combined firm without synergy, where the without-synergy value = sum of the standalone DCF values of the two firms (each at its own WACC).
**Compute:** MIXED — the subtraction and DCF mechanics are deterministic; specifying the synergy-adjusted growth rates and cash flows is judgment.
**Tags:** synergy, dcf, merger-valuation, three-step-process

## p.102 — Synergy Example 3: Tax benefits (Best Buy / Zenith)

**Teaches:** Poses a tax-synergy problem: Best Buy considers acquiring Zenith, which has net operating losses (NOLs) of $2 billion. With a 36% tax rate, the maximum tax benefit if losses can be used immediately is 36% x $2 billion = $720 million. If Best Buy had only $500 million of taxable income per year, the NOLs would be used over multiple years, so the benefit must be spread over time and discounted (present value is less than $720m). Third question: if Zenith's market value is $800 million, should you pay the tax benefit as a premium on market value? Only if the market price does not already reflect the tax benefit and no other bidder can also capture it — paying it out fully hands the synergy to the target's shareholders.
**Formulas:** Maximum tax benefit = Tax rate x Net operating losses = 0.36 x $2,000m = $720 million, if usable immediately. If taxable income is limited to $500m/year: yearly tax saving = $500m x 0.36 = $180m. The NOL lasts $2,000m / $500m = 4 years, so the benefit = present value of $180m/year for 4 years.
**Example:** Zenith NOL = $2 billion; Best Buy tax rate = 36% → up to $720m tax benefit. With $500m/yr taxable income, savings run $180m/yr for ~4 years, discounted to present value. Zenith's market value of $800m raises the question of whether to pay the benefit as a premium.
**Compute:** MIXED — tax savings per year and PV are deterministic given tax rate, NOL, income and discount rate; whether to pay it as a premium is judgment.

## p.105 — Biased samples = poor results

**Teaches:** Basing what you pay on what other acquirers have paid is a recipe for disaster: acquirers on average overpay, so matching their prices replicates their mistakes. Even sector pricing metrics may rest on firms that are not truly comparable. Exit multiples assume that what the market pays for comparable companies today is what it will continue to pay in the future — an unjustified assumption.
**Rules:** Do not benchmark acquisition prices on past acquisition prices (biased sample); treat exit multiples as embedding a stationarity assumption about future market pricing.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** acquisitions, biased-samples, transaction-multiples, exit-multiples

## p.106 — Lesson 5: Don't be a lemming

**Teaches:** Acquisitions are often justified by "everyone in the sector is doing it, we must too to survive" or by pricing off what others have paid, which may far exceed your own value estimate. With the right (cherry-picked) set of comparable firms, you can justify almost any price. EPS accretion is a meaningless measure: buying any company with a PE lower than yours mathematically produces EPS accretion, regardless of whether value is created.
**Rules:** EPS accretion test — acquiring a lower-PE firm always accretes EPS mechanically, so accretion is not evidence of value creation.

## p.108 — Lesson 6: Don't let egos or investment bankers beat common sense

**Teaches:** If your objective in a bidding war is to win at any cost, you will win — but beware the winner's curse. Premiums paid often have nothing to do with synergy, control, or strategy, though those are the stated reasons. They may just reflect CEO ego. Evidence shows "overconfident" CEOs are more likely to make acquisitions, and they leave a trail across the firms they run. Pre-emptive or defensive acquisitions are dangerous: you overpay because everyone else is, or from fear of being left behind. If the only way to stay competitive in a business is to make bad investments, it may be best to exit the business.
**Rules:** In a bidding war, winning at any cost triggers the winner's curse; if staying competitive requires bad investments, consider exiting the business.

## p.116 — Growth vs cost synergies: delivery rates (McKinsey 2002)

**Teaches:** McKinsey (2002) post-merger evidence: revenue (growth) synergies are far harder to deliver than cost synergies. 70% of mergers failed to achieve expected revenue synergies; typical revenue-estimation errors are ignoring/underestimating customer losses from integration (typically 2%–5%) and assuming growth/share targets out of line with market growth and competitive dynamics (no "outside view" calibration). Cost-synergy estimation is better: a large share of mergers achieve 90%+ of expected cost savings. Errors still arise from four sources. Firms underestimate one-time costs. They use benchmarks from noncomparable situations. They fail to sanity-check management estimates against precedent transactions. They fail to ground estimates in bottom-up analysis, e.g. a location-by-location review of overlaps.
**Data:** Revenue synergies (percent of expected achieved, N=77 mergers): <30%: 23; 30–50%: 5; 51–60%: 13; 61–70%: 6; 71–80%: 14; 81–90%: 8; 91–100%: 13; >100%: 17. Cost savings (percent of expected achieved, N=92): <30%: 3; 30–50%: 5; 51–60%: 1; 61–70%: 4; 71–80%: 12; 81–90%: 13; 91–100%: 25; >100%: 36. Source: McKinsey (2002) Postmerger Management Practice client survey and case studies.

## p.117 — Synergy: odds of success

**Teaches:** Findings from synergy studies. (1) Cost synergies are far more likely to be delivered than growth synergies. (2) Synergies that are concrete and planned for at the time of the merger beat fuzzy ones. (3) Synergy is much more likely to show up when someone is held responsible for delivering it. (4) You keep more of the synergy gains as a single bidder than as one of multiple bidders. Competition bids the synergy value into the price paid.
**Rules:** Synergy-realization heuristics: prefer cost over growth synergies; make plans concrete pre-merger; assign accountability; avoid multi-bidder situations.

## p.118 — Lesson 7: Stay disciplined for acquisitions to create value

**Teaches:** Five disciplines for value-creating acquisitions. (1) If you have a successful acquisition strategy, stay focused on it. Don't let size or hubris "expand" the strategy. (2) Put realistic plans for delivering synergy and control in place BEFORE the merger completes. Benefits must be reachable, not pipe dreams. Time frames must reflect that merging two organizations takes a while. (3) The best thing to do in a bidding war is to drop out. (4) Someone should be held to account for delivering the benefits — preferably the person pushing hardest for the merger. (5) Pay for investment bankers and other deal participants should be tied to how well the deal works, not to getting the deal done.
**Rules:** Five acquisition disciplines: stick to the proven strategy; pre-merger realistic synergy plans; drop out of bidding wars; assign accountability to the deal's champion; tie advisor compensation to deal outcomes.
