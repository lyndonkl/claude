## p.161 — Implications of the EVA/firm-value example: growth alone does not create value; MVA

**Teaches:** Growth by itself does not create value. Value comes only from growth with investment in excess-return projects — projects whose return on capital beats the cost of capital. In the preceding worked example, the 5% growth a year after year 5 creates no added value, because those investments earn exactly the cost of capital. "Market value added" (MVA) is the excess of market value over capital invested. MVA depends on the excess value created — the present value of EVA. In the example, the firm's market value of $170.85 million beats its book value of $100 million because the return on capital is 5% above the cost of capital.
**Formulas:** MVA = Market value of firm − Capital invested, where MVA = market value added, market value = DCF/EVA-based value of the firm, and capital invested = book capital invested in the firm. MVA equals the present value of expected EVA over time.
**Rules:** If new investments earn a return on capital equal to the cost of capital, growth adds no value; only excess-return growth (ROC > cost of capital) adds value.
**Example:** Continuation of prior example: market value $170.85 million vs. book value $100 million, so MVA = $70.85 million, arising because ROC exceeds the cost of capital by 5%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given market value and capital invested, MVA = market value − capital invested.

## p.162 — Year-by-year EVA changes: advantages and the hidden cost

**Teaches:** Firms are often evaluated on year-to-year changes in EVA rather than on the present value of EVA over time. This comparison has two advantages. First, it is simple and needs no forecasts of future earnings potential. Second, it can be broken down by any unit — person, division, etc. — as long as capital can be assigned and earnings allocated across those units. It is simpler than DCF valuation, but it has a cost: a firm that focuses on raising EVA year-to-year may end up less valuable.
**Rules:** Increasing current-year EVA is not equivalent to increasing firm value; value depends on the present value of all future EVA.

## p.163 — Gaming the system: three ways to deliver high current EVA while destroying value

**Teaches:** Managers judged on current EVA can boost it while destroying firm value in three ways. The Growth trade-off game: give up valuable future growth chances to deliver higher EVA this year. The Risk game: deliver higher dollar EVA from riskier businesses. The value of the business is the present value of EVA over time, so the risk effect may dominate the increased EVA. The Capital Invested game: make investments that do not show up as part of capital invested. Operating income then rises while measured capital invested falls, which inflates EVA.
**Rules:** When auditing an EVA increase, check (1) whether future growth was sacrificed, (2) whether risk (cost of capital) rose, and (3) whether capital invested was understated by keeping investments off the capital base.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT — qualitative diagnosis of managerial gaming behaviors.

## p.164 — Why high EVA may not raise the stock price: expectations matter

**Teaches:** The link between EVA and market value changes is more complex than the link between EVA and firm value. A firm's market value reflects the expected EVA of assets in place plus the expected EVA from future projects. Market prices already embed EVA expectations. So if actual economic value added comes in below the EVA the market expected, market value can fall even though EVA is higher than last year. Beating last year's EVA is not enough; the firm must beat expected EVA for the stock price to rise.
**Rules:** Stock price reaction depends on actual EVA versus expected EVA, not on actual EVA versus last year's EVA; higher EVA that falls short of expectations can lower market value.

## p.165 — Conditions under which year-to-year EVA focus has least side effects

**Teaches:** Focusing on year-to-year EVA changes causes the least damage when three conditions hold. (1) Most or all of the firm's assets are already in place, so little or none of firm value is expected to come from future growth. This minimizes the risk that increases in current EVA come at the expense of future EVA (the growth trade-off game). (2) Leverage is stable, and the firm's investment decisions cannot easily alter the cost of capital. This minimizes the risk that higher EVA arrives with a higher cost of capital (the risk game). (3) The firm is in a sector where investors anticipate little or no surplus returns; firms there are expected to just earn their cost of capital. This minimizes the risk that the EVA increase falls short of market expectations and triggers a price drop.
**Rules:** Year-to-year EVA is a safe performance metric only when three conditions hold. Assets are largely in place (low growth-value share). Leverage and the cost of capital are stable and hard to manipulate. The sector is expected to earn roughly its cost of capital (low expected surplus returns).
