## p.123 — The three (value) reasons for acquisitions
**Teaches:** There are only three value-based justifications for an acquisition. (1) Undervaluation: you believe the market is mispricing the target and you can buy it for less than its "fair" value. (2) Control: the target is badly managed and you intend to change how it is run; if you are right and can make the changes, firm value increases under your management. (3) Synergy: combined with a business/resource you already own, the target can do things neither could do separately. Synergy comes in three flavors: offensive synergy (higher growth and increased pricing power), defensive synergy (cost cutting, consolidation, preempting competitors), and tax synergy (directly from tax clauses or indirectly through debt).
**Rules:** Classify any acquisition rationale as undervaluation, control, or synergy; synergy subclassifies as offensive, defensive, or tax.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification framework).
**Tags:** acquisitions, synergy, control, undervaluation

## p.124 — Four numbers to watch and the acid test
**Teaches:** Evaluating any acquisition requires four numbers. (1) Acquisition Price: what you pay — negotiated (often off comparable deals) for a private business, a premium over market price for a public one. (2) Status Quo Value: value of the target run by existing management. (3) Restructured Value: value of the target with changed investing, financing, and dividend policies. (4) Synergy Value: value of the combined company with synergy benefits built in, minus (value of the acquirer stand-alone plus the restructured value of the target).
**Rules:** The Acid Test — the deal makes sense on each motive only if: Undervaluation: Price for target < Status Quo Value. Control: Price for target < Restructured Value. Synergy: Price for target < Restructured Value + Value of Synergy.
**Compute:** MIXED — the acid-test comparisons are deterministic once the four values exist; estimating the four values is judgment (full DCF work).
**Tags:** acquisitions, acid-test, synergy-value, control-value

## p.132 — Price enhancement versus value enhancement
**Teaches:** Distinguishes changing the market PRICE from changing intrinsic VALUE. Evidence: firms that added ".com" to their names during the dot-com bubble earned cumulative abnormal returns of roughly 100-200% around the announcement ("the market gives"); when the bubble burst, firms dropping ".com" from their names also saw price bounces ("and takes away"). Cosmetic changes can move price without any change in cash flows, growth, or risk — i.e., without creating value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual distinction supported by event-study charts).

## p.133 — The paths to value creation
**Teaches:** In the DCF framework there are only four ways to increase a firm's value. (1) Increase cash flows from existing assets: raise after-tax earnings from assets in place, or reduce reinvestment needs (net cap ex or working capital). (2) Increase the expected growth rate of those cash flows: raise the reinvestment rate, or improve the return on capital on those reinvestments. (3) Extend the length of the high growth period. (4) Reduce the cost of capital: lower the operating risk in investments/assets, change the financial mix, or change the financing composition. Any proposed "value enhancement" must work through at least one of these four levers.
**Rules:** An action creates value only if it raises existing-asset cash flows, raises expected growth, lengthens the growth period, or lowers the cost of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework).
**Tags:** value-creation, dcf, value-drivers, framework

## p.134 — Value creation 1: increase cash flows from assets in place
**Teaches:** Maps operational levers onto the FCFF calculation: Revenues x Operating Margin = EBIT; EBIT - Tax = EBIT(1-t); EBIT(1-t) + Depreciation - Capital Expenditures - Change in Working Capital = FCFF. Levers: more efficient operations and cost cutting raise margins; divest assets with negative EBIT; reduce the tax rate (moving income to lower-tax locales, transfer pricing, risk management); live off past over-investment (lower cap ex); better inventory management and tighter credit policies (lower working capital).
**Formulas:** FCFF = EBIT x (1 - t) + Depreciation - Capital Expenditures - Change in Working Capital, where EBIT = Revenues x Operating Margin and t = tax rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (FCFF from revenues, margin, tax rate, depreciation, cap ex, ΔWC); choosing the levers is judgment.
**Tags:** fcff, assets-in-place, cash-flow, value-creation

## p.135 — Value creation 2: increase value from expected growth
**Teaches:** Expected growth = Reinvestment Rate x Return on Capital. You can grow faster by reinvesting more (more projects, acquisitions) or by earning a higher return on capital (higher operating margins or higher capital turnover). Pricing strategy matters: Return on Capital = Operating Margin x Capital Turnover Ratio, so a price-leader strategy (high margin, low turnover) and a volume-leader strategy (low margin, high turnover) are alternative routes to the same ROC. Game theory caveat: consider how competitors will react to your moves and how you will react to theirs.
**Formulas:** Expected Growth Rate = Reinvestment Rate x Return on Capital. Return on Capital = Operating Margin x Capital Turnover Ratio.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (growth from reinvestment rate and ROC); strategy choice is judgment.
**Tags:** expected-growth, reinvestment-rate, return-on-capital, pricing-strategy

## p.136 — Value creating growth: evaluating the alternatives
**Teaches:** Not all growth creates equal value. McKinsey-style evidence for the consumer goods industry shows shareholder value created per incremental $1 million of growth varies widely by growth mode. New-product market development creates $1.75-2.00. Expanding an existing market creates $0.30-0.75. Maintaining or growing share in a growing market creates $0.10-0.50. Competing for share in a stable market destroys value (-$0.25 to -$0.40). Acquisitions (25th-75th percentile) also destroy value (-$0.5 to -$0.20). The revenue growth or acquisition size needed to double a typical company's share price rises correspondingly, from $5-6B for new products up to n/m-$50B for acquisitions.
**Data:**
| Category of growth | Value created per incremental $1M of growth | Revenue growth/acq. size to double share price ($B) |
|---|---|---|
| New-product market development | 1.75–2.00 | 5–6 |
| Expanding an existing market | 0.30–0.75 | 13–33 |
| Maintaining/growing share in a growing market | 0.10–0.50 | 20–100 |
| Competing for share in a stable market | -0.25–0.40 (negative) | n/m–25 |
| Acquisition (25th to 75th percentile) | -0.5–0.20 (negative) | n/m–50 |

**Rules:** Organic new-product growth creates the most value per dollar; acquisitions and share fights in stable markets typically destroy value.

## p.139 — Value creation 4: reduce cost of capital
**Teaches:** The fourth lever is lowering the cost of capital: Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity x (E/(D+E)) + Pre-tax Cost of Debt x (D/(D+E)) (after-tax on the debt side). Ways to reduce it: reduce operating leverage (outsourcing, flexible wage contracts and cost structure); make the product or service less discretionary to customers (changing product characteristics, more effective advertising) to lower business risk; change the financing mix toward the optimal debt ratio; and match debt to assets (using swaps, derivatives, hybrids) to reduce default risk.
**Formulas:** Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity x (E/(D+E)) + Pre-tax Cost of Debt x (D/(D+E)), where E = market value of equity, D = market value of debt.
**Compute:** MIXED — the WACC formula is deterministic; the risk-reduction actions are judgment.
**Tags:** cost-of-capital, wacc, operating-leverage, financing-mix

## p.145 — The expected value of control (framework)
**Teaches:** The expected value of control = (probability that you can change the management of the firm) x (change in firm value from changing management). The probability depends on takeover restrictions, voting rules and rights, access to funds (to mount a challenge), and the size of the company. The change in value = value of the firm run optimally minus value of the firm run status quo.
**Formulas:** Expected Value of Control = Probability of management change x (Optimal firm value - Status quo firm value).
**Compute:** MIXED — the multiplication is deterministic; both the probability and the optimal value are estimates.
**Tags:** value-of-control, expected-value, corporate-governance

## p.146 — Why the probability of management changing shifts over time
**Teaches:** The probability of management change is not constant. (1) Corporate governance rules change as new laws pass; changes that give stockholders more power raise the likelihood of management change. (2) Activist investing ebbs and flows with markets — activists are more visible in down markets and often emerge in response to scandals. (3) Events like hostile acquisitions make investors reassess the likelihood of change by reminding them of the power they possess.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** corporate-governance, activist-investing, management-change

## p.147 — Estimating the probability of change
**Teaches:** The probability of management change can be estimated from historical data on companies where change occurred, using statistical techniques such as probits or logits. Empirical determinants: (1) stock price and earnings performance — forced turnover is more likely in firms that underperformed peers and expectations; (2) board structure — forced CEO change is more likely when the board is small, composed of outsiders, and the CEO is not also chairman; (3) ownership structure — forced CEO changes are more common with high institutional and low insider holdings, and in firms more dependent on equity markets for new capital; (4) industry structure — CEOs are more likely to be replaced in competitive industries.
**Rules:** Higher probability of forced management change when: poor relative performance; small, outsider-dominated board with separate CEO/chairman; high institutional ownership and low insider ownership; equity-market dependence; competitive industry.
**Compute:** MIXED — a fitted probit/logit produces a probability deterministically from firm characteristics; specifying/fitting the model is statistical judgment.
**Tags:** management-change, probability-estimation, corporate-governance, probit-logit

## p.148 — Manifestations of the value of control
**Teaches:** The value of control shows up in four settings. (1) Hostile acquisitions: the control premium should reflect the value change from changing management. (2) Market prices of publicly traded firms: every market price should embed an expected value of control — Market value = Status quo value + (Optimal value - Status quo value) x Probability of management changing. (3) Voting vs non-voting shares: the voting-share premium should increase with the expected value of control. (4) Minority discounts in private companies: the discount for buying less than a controlling stake should increase with the expected value of control.
**Formulas:** Market value = Status quo value + (Optimal value - Status quo value) x Probability of management changing.
**Compute:** MIXED — the formula is deterministic given its three inputs; the inputs are estimates.
**Tags:** value-of-control, control-premium, voting-shares, minority-discount

## p.149 — 1. Hostile acquisition: example (Blockbuster)

## p.151 — Value of stock in a publicly traded firm (voting vs non-voting)
**Teaches:** For a badly managed firm the market prices in the chance of better management: value per share = [Status Quo Value + Probability of control change x (Optimal - Status Quo Value)] / Number of shares outstanding. With voting and non-voting share classes, a disproportionate share of the value of control goes to the voting shares. In the extreme case where non-voting shares are completely unprotected, non-voting shares get only status quo value and voting shares capture the entire expected value of control.
**Formulas:** Value per share = [Status Quo Value + Probability of control change x (Optimal Value - Status Quo Value)] / Number of shares outstanding. Value per non-voting share = Status Quo Value / (# Voting Shares + # Non-voting Shares). Value per voting share = Value of non-voting share + [Probability of control change x (Optimal Value - Status Quo Value)] / # Voting Shares.

## p.153 — 4. Minority discount: Kristin Kandy example
**Teaches:** In a private-company transaction, a controlling stake is worth its share of the OPTIMAL value (control lets you make changes), while a minority stake is worth its share of the STATUS QUO value. **Rules:** Controlling stake (>50%) is priced off optimal value; minority stake (<50%) is priced off status quo value; minority discount arises from this asymmetry.

## p.154 — Alternative approaches to value enhancement
**Teaches:** Instead of full DCF, firms sometimes maximize a variable correlated with firm value: an accounting variable (earnings, return on investment), a marketing variable (market share), a cash flow variable (cash flow return on investment, CFROI), or a risk-adjusted cash flow variable (Economic Value Added, EVA). Advantage: these are often simpler and easier to use than DCF value. Disadvantage: the simplicity comes at a cost — these variables are not perfectly correlated with DCF value, so maximizing them can diverge from maximizing value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (taxonomy and tradeoff discussion).
**Tags:** value-enhancement, eva, cfroi, proxy-metrics

## p.155 — Economic Value Added (EVA) and CFROI defined
**Teaches:** EVA measures the surplus value created on an investment: define return on capital (ROC) as the "true" cash flow return on capital earned, and the cost of capital as the weighted average of the costs of the financing instruments used; then EVA = (ROC - Cost of Capital) x Capital Invested. CFROI is a measure of the cash flow return made on capital, computed as an IRR based on the base value of capital invested and the cash flows on that capital.
**Formulas:** EVA = (Return on Capital - Cost of Capital) x (Capital Invested in Project). CFROI = IRR of the cash flows on the capital relative to the base capital invested.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, cost of capital, capital invested → EVA; cash flows and capital base → CFROI as IRR).
**Tags:** eva, cfroi, excess-returns, performance-metrics

## p.156 — The bottom line: consistent DCF approaches agree
**Teaches:** The value of a firm does not change just because you use a different metric. All discounted-cash-flow-based approaches (DCF, EVA, etc.) should yield the same value for a business if they make consistent assumptions. Any difference in value across approaches must be attributable to differences in assumptions — explicit or implicit — behind the valuations, not to the metric itself.
**Rules:** If EVA-based and FCFF-based values differ, hunt for the inconsistent assumption; the metric itself cannot create or destroy value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (equivalence principle).
**Tags:** eva, dcf, valuation-consistency
