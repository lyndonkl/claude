## p.162 — An investment opportunity? (IPO underpricing strategy question)

**Teaches:** Discussion question: if investment banks systematically underprice IPOs by roughly 10–15%, what strategy could an investor adopt to exploit this? The obvious play is to subscribe to IPOs at the offer price and sell on the offering day. The slide then asks why such a strategy might not work. The implicit answer is allocation bias (winner's curse): investors get full allotments in overpriced IPOs but are rationed in underpriced ones. Transaction costs and the episodic nature of IPO markets also weaken the strategy.

## p.163 — D. The offering quantity

**Teaches:** The fraction of the company offered in the IPO determines how much underpricing actually costs the owner. Suppose you (as owner of Twitter) offered 100% of the shares and investors are willing to pay $20 billion. A 15% underpricing means the offer raises only about $17 billion — you lose roughly $3 billion (15% of $20B). Now suppose you offer only 10% of the shares and plan to sell most of your remaining stake over the next two years. Underpricing then costs you only on the 10% sold now. The positive first-day pop can raise the price at which you sell the rest, so underpricing may even work in your favor.

**Example:** Twitter hypothetical: investors willing to pay $20 billion for the common stock; underpricing = 15%. If 100% offered, loss ≈ 0.15 × $20B = $3 billion. If only 10% offered, loss applies only to that 10% slice (≈ $0.3 billion), and remaining 90% may benefit from the post-IPO price.

**Compute:** MIXED — the loss-from-underpricing arithmetic (offered fraction × value × underpricing %) is DETERMINISTIC; whether underpricing helps or hurts the owner with a staged exit is JUDGMENT.

## p.164 — Private to VC to public offering: cost of equity at three stages

**Teaches:** For a private business headed toward a public offering, the cost of equity changes with who the marginal investor is at each stage. An undiversified investor perceives total risk rather than just market risk. The perceived (total) beta is the market beta divided by the correlation of the investor's portfolio with the market. Setup: the sector's publicly traded firms have average market beta = 1; average correlation of firms with the market = 0.25; riskfree rate = 4%; equity risk premium (ERP) = 5%.

**Formulas:**
- Perceived (total) beta = Market beta / Correlation with market, where "Correlation with market" is the correlation of the marginal investor's holdings with the market portfolio.
- Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Perceived beta × ERP.

**Example:**
- Stage 1 (nascent business, fully invested private owner; correlation = 0.25): Perceived beta = 1/0.25 = 4; Cost of equity = 4% + 4(5%) = 24%.
- Stage 2 (angel/VC financing from a specialized venture capitalist holding multiple tech investments; portfolio correlation with market = 0.5): Perceived beta = 1/0.5 = 2; Cost of equity = 4% + 2(5%) = 14%.
- Stage 3 (public offering, diversified retail and institutional investors): Perceived beta = 1; Cost of equity = 4% + 1(5%) = 9%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given market beta, correlation, riskfree rate, ERP → perceived beta and cost of equity per stage (choosing the correlation for each stage is JUDGMENT).

## p.165 — To value this company… (DCF with stage-varying cost of equity)

**Teaches:** Values the company from p.164 using discount rates that change as ownership transitions. The current (undiversified) owner holds it fully for years 1–2 (24% cost of equity). A venture capitalist funds it from the start of year 3 through year 5 (14%). It is public in the terminal phase (9%). Because the discount rate changes over time, cash flows must be discounted with the cumulated cost of equity (compounding each year's rate), not a single constant rate. Using one constant rate throughout gives the wrong answer: 24% forever undervalues the firm; 9% forever overvalues it.

**Formulas:**
- Terminal value at end of year 5 = Terminal-year cash flow / (stable cost of equity − stable growth rate) = 175 / (0.09 − 0.02) = $2,500. Growth rate = 2% forever after year 5.
- Cumulated cost of equity for year t = product over years 1..t of (1 + cost of equity in year s); PV of year-t cash flow = CF_t / Cumulated COE_t.

**Example:** Inputs and computation (cash flows in $):

| | Yr 1 | Yr 2 | Yr 3 | Yr 4 | Yr 5 | Terminal yr |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| E(Cash flow) | $100 | $125 | $150 | $165 | $170 | $175 |
| Market beta | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Correlation | 0.25 | 0.25 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 0.5 | 1 |
| Beta used | 4 | 4 | 2 | 2 | 2 | 1 |
| Cost of equity | 24.00% | 24.00% | 14.00% | 14.00% | 14.00% | 9.00% |
| Terminal value | | | | | $2,500 | |
| Cumulated COE | 1.2400 | 1.5376 | 1.7529 | 1.9983 | 2.2780 | 2.4830 |
| PV | $80.65 | $81.30 | $85.57 | $82.57 | $1,172.07 | |

(Year-5 PV discounts cash flow $170 plus terminal value $2,500 by cumulated COE 2.2780.)

Results:
- Value of firm = $1,502 — correct value, using changing costs of equity.
- Value of firm = $1,221 — using 24% as cost of equity forever (undervalues the firm).
- Value of firm = $2,165 — using 9% as cost of equity forever (overvalues the firm).

**Rules:** When the marginal investor changes over a company's life (owner → VC → public markets), use stage-specific costs of equity and discount with cumulated (compounded) discount factors; a single constant rate misvalues the firm.

## p.166 — Implications (of the private-to-public transition for value)

**Teaches:** Two propositions follow when costs of equity fall as a firm nears public markets. Proposition 1: a private firm that expects to become publicly traded is worth more than an identical private firm that does not expect that change. Corollaries: private firms in sectors that are "hot" for going public (e.g., social media in 2014) are worth more than firms in less sexy sectors. As IPOs boom (bust), private values rise (fall). Private firms in countries with easy access to public markets are worth more than firms in countries without that access. Proposition 2: a private firm that expects to go public sooner is worth more than a similar firm that will take longer. So private firms gain value when companies can go public earlier in their life cycle.

**Rules:** Expected transition to public ownership raises private-firm value; earlier expected transition raises it more; IPO market conditions and country access to public markets shift private valuations in the same direction.

## p.167 — Private company valuation: Closing thoughts

**Teaches:** The value of a private business depends on the potential buyer. A seller maximizes value by selling to a long-term investor who is well diversified (or whose own investors are). Ideally the buyer also does not think too highly of the seller personally, so no key-person discount applies. When valuing a private business for legal purposes (tax or divorce court), the assumptions used and the value reached depend on which side of the legal divide you are on. Final proposition: the owner of a private firm can always put wealth into publicly traded stocks instead. What those stocks can earn must anchor the return the owner demands on the firm.

**Rules:** Sell to long-term, diversified buyers who don't overvalue your personal involvement; expect legal-context valuations to be advocacy-driven; anchor the required return on a private business to returns available in public markets.
