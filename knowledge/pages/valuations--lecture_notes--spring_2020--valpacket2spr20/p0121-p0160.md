## p.122 — Process of valuing private companies
**Teaches:** The process of valuing private companies is the same in shape as valuing public companies: estimate cash flows, attach a discount rate based on the riskiness of the cash flows, and compute a present value. You can value either the entire business (discount cash flows to the firm at the cost of capital) or the equity (discount cash flows to equity at the cost of equity). Two standard problems arise with private firms: (1) there is no market value for either debt or equity, and (2) financial statements for private firms typically go back fewer years, have less detail, and have more holes in them.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framing of the process and its problems).
**Tags:** private-company-valuation, dcf, cost-of-capital, cash-flow-estimation

## p.123 — Problem 1: No market value
**Teaches:** Neither the debt nor the equity of a private business is traded. So any inputs that require market values cannot be estimated directly. Two examples: (1) debt ratios needed to go from unlevered to levered betas and to compute the cost of capital; (2) market prices needed to value options and warrants granted to employees. Also, when valuing public firms the market value serves as a measure of reasonableness (a sanity check); in private company valuation the value stands alone. Finally, market-price-based risk measures such as beta and bond ratings are not available for private businesses.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual problem statement).
**Tags:** private-company-valuation, market-value, beta, debt-ratio

## p.124 — Problem 2: Cash flow estimation issues
**Teaches:** Four cash-flow estimation issues specific to private firms: (1) Shorter history — private firms have usually been around for less time, so less historical information exists. (2) Different accounting standards — private-firm statements often follow looser standards than public firms, which face tighter constraints on what and when to report. (3) Intermingling of personal and business expenses — some personal expenses may be reported as business expenses. (4) Separating "salaries" from "dividends" — it is difficult to tell where salaries end and dividends begin, since both end up with the owner.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative diagnosis of accounting issues).

## p.125 — Motive matters in private company valuation
**Teaches:** Why you are valuing a private company matters. Motives fall into three groups:
- "Show" valuations: curiosity ("How much is my business really worth?") and legal purposes (estate tax, divorce court).
- Transaction valuations: sale to another individual or private entity, sale of one partner's interest to another, or sale to a publicly traded firm.
- A prelude to setting the offering price in an IPO. You can also value a division of a publicly traded firm. Reasons: as prelude to a spin-off; for sale to another entity; or for a sum-of-the-parts valuation. The last tests whether the firm is worth more broken up or is being efficiently run.

## p.127 — I. Private-to-private transaction: three key issues
**Teaches:** In a private-to-private transaction, one individual sells a private business to another. Three key issues arise. (1) Neither the buyer nor the seller is diversified. Risk-and-return models that focus only on non-diversifiable (market) risk will badly underestimate the discount rate. (2) The investment is illiquid, so the buyer must factor in an "illiquidity discount" when estimating value. (3) Key person value: revenues and operating profit may reflect the presence of the current owner, not just the business's own potential, so a significant personal component may be embedded in value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (issue identification).

## p.130 — Step 1: Estimating discount rates — why standard models fail
**Teaches:** Conventional risk-and-return models presume the marginal investors in a company are diversified and so care only about non-diversifiable risk, measured by a beta (or betas) usually estimated from past prices or returns. In this restaurant valuation both assumptions are violated. First, as a private business, the restaurant has no market prices or returns to use for estimation. Second, the buyer is not diversified — he will have his entire wealth tied up in the restaurant after the purchase.

## p.132 — Diagram: Private owner vs public company perceptions of risk
**Teaches:** Diagram contrasting risk perceptions. A private owner with 100% of wealth in the business is exposed to ALL the risk in the firm (illustrated as 80 units of firm-specific risk + 20 units of market risk) and demands a cost of equity reflecting total risk. A publicly traded company with diversified investors eliminates firm-specific risk in the portfolio and demands a cost of equity reflecting only market risk (the 20 units). Total Beta measures all risk; Market Beta measures just market risk.
**Formulas:** Total Beta = Market Beta / (Portion of the total risk that is market risk). Symbols: Market Beta = standard beta capturing only market risk; the denominator = fraction of total risk that is market risk (in practice the correlation of the firm with the market).

## p.133 — Estimating a total beta
**Teaches:** To go from market beta to total beta you need a measure of how much of the firm's risk is market risk versus firm-specific. The regressions of the publicly traded comparables that produced the bottom-up beta provide the answer: the average R-squared across the high-end retailer regressions is 25%. Since betas are based on standard deviations (not variances), use the correlation coefficient — the square root of R-squared — as the proportion of risk that is market risk.
**Formulas:** Total Unlevered Beta = Market Beta / Correlation with the market, where Correlation = sqrt(R-squared) of the comparable firms' market regressions. Here: Total Unlevered Beta = 1.18 / 0.5 = 2.36 (sqrt(0.25) = 0.5).
**Example:** High-end retailers: market unlevered beta 1.18, average R² = 25% → correlation = 0.50 → total unlevered beta = 1.18/0.5 = 2.36.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (market beta + comparable R² → total beta).
**Tags:** total-beta, r-squared, correlation, unlevered-beta

## p.134 — Levering the beta and cost of equity for the restaurant
**Teaches:** With public firms you re-lever beta using the firm's market D/E ratio; that is not feasible for private firms. Two alternatives exist. (1) Assume the firm's debt-to-equity ratio equals the average market D/E ratio of publicly traded firms in the sector. (2) Use your own estimates of debt and equity values as weights. The second creates a circular-reasoning problem: you need the cost of capital to get the values and the values to get the cost of capital. Here the restaurant is assumed to have the average publicly traded restaurant D/E of 14.33% (even though retailers were used for the unlevered beta).
**Formulas:** Levered beta = Unlevered total beta × (1 + (1 − tax rate) × (D/E)) = 2.36 × (1 + (1 − 0.4)(0.1433)) = 2.56. Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Levered beta × Equity risk premium = 4.25% + 2.56 × (4%) = 14.50%. (T-bond rate 4.25% at the time; 4% equity risk premium.)
**Rules:** For private firms, use the sector-average market D/E to lever the beta (or iterate your own estimated weights, accepting circularity).
**Example:** Restaurant: total unlevered beta 2.36, tax rate 40%, D/E 14.33% → levered beta 2.56 → cost of equity 14.50%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (unlevered beta, tax rate, D/E, riskfree rate, ERP → levered beta and cost of equity).

## p.135 — Estimating a cost of debt and cost of capital (synthetic rating)
**Teaches:** The private firm has no bond rating or recent bank loans, but it has operating income and lease expenses (treated as interest expense), so a synthetic rating can be built from the interest coverage ratio. The cost of capital uses the same industry-average debt ratio used to lever the beta; the D/E ratio of 14.33% converts to debt-to-capital weights of 14.33/114.33 for debt and 100/114.33 for equity.
**Formulas:** Coverage Ratio = Operating Income / Interest (Lease) Expense = 400,000 / 120,000 = 3.33. After-tax cost of debt = (Riskfree rate + Default spread) × (1 − tax rate) = (4.25% + 3.25%) × (1 − 0.40) = 4.50%. Cost of capital = Cost of equity × (E/(D+E)) + After-tax cost of debt × (D/(D+E)) = 14.50% × (100/114.33) + 4.50% × (14.33/114.33) = 13.25%.
**Rules:** Coverage ratio of 3.33 → synthetic rating BB+ → default spread 3.25% (from a coverage-to-rating lookup table not reproduced on this page).
**Example:** Restaurant: coverage 3.33 → BB+ → spread 3.25%; pre-tax cost of debt 7.50%; after-tax cost of debt 4.50%; cost of capital 13.25%.

## p.140 — Step 6: The illiquidity discount should vary
**Teaches:** Illiquidity is a constant theme in private company valuation, but practice collapses it into a rule of thumb: a fixed 20–30% discount for all private firms. Damodaran argues the discount should vary on three dimensions. (1) Companies: healthier, larger companies with more liquid assets deserve smaller discounts than money-losing smaller businesses with illiquid assets. (2) Time: liquidity is worth more when the economy is bad and credit is tight than when markets are booming. (3) Buyers: liquidity is worth more to buyers with short horizons and high cash needs than to long-term investors willing to hold.
**Rules:** Conventional rule of thumb: illiquidity discount = 20–30% of value, invariant across firms (criticized here); better practice: vary the discount by firm health/size, macro conditions, and buyer horizon.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (what drives the discount).
**Tags:** illiquidity-discount, private-company-valuation, liquidity

## p.141 — Standard approach: measuring illiquidity from restricted stock and pre-IPO deals
**Teaches:** The standard approach estimates the illiquidity discount from illiquid publicly traded assets. Restricted stock is stock issued by public companies bypassing SEC registration that cannot be traded for one year after issue. Pre-IPO transactions are trades before an IPO where equity investors in the private firm buy/sell each other's stakes. In both cases the discount is the difference between the market price of the liquid asset and the observed transaction price of the illiquid asset.
**Formulas:** Discount(restricted stock) = Stock price − Price on restricted stock offering. Discount(IPO) = IPO offering price − Price on pre-IPO transaction.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given the paired prices (liquid price, illiquid transaction price → discount).

## p.142 — The restricted stock discount and the Silber regression
**Teaches:** Three aggregate studies measured the restricted-stock discount. Maher (four mutual funds, 1969–73) found an average discount of 35.43% versus publicly traded stock of the same companies. Moroney reported a mean discount of 35% for 146 restricted stock issues bought by 10 investment companies (1970 data). Silber (1991), studying 1980s offerings, found a median discount of 33.75%. Silber also related the discount's size to offering characteristics via regression: bigger-revenue firms and profitable firms get smaller discounts; larger restricted blocks (relative to total stock) get bigger discounts.
**Formulas:** LN(RPRS) = 4.33 + 0.036 LN(REV) − 0.142 LN(RBRT) + 0.174 DERN + 0.332 DCUST.
Symbols:
- RPRS = relative price of restricted stock (to publicly traded stock)
- REV = revenues of the private firm in $ millions
- RBRT = restricted block relative to total common stock in %
- DERN = 1 if earnings are positive, 0 if negative
- DCUST = 1 if there is a customer relationship with the investor, 0 otherwise
**Data:**
| Study | Sample | Discount |
|---|---|---|
| Maher | 4 mutual funds' restricted stock purchases, 1969–73 | 35.43% average |
| Moroney | 146 restricted issues, 10 investment companies, 1970 data | 35% mean |
| Silber (1991) | 1980s restricted stock offerings | 33.75% median |

## p.145 — The sampling problem in discount studies
**Teaches:** Both restricted-stock and pre-IPO studies suffer significant sampling bias. Companies making restricted stock offerings tend to be small, troubled firms that have run out of normal financing options. IPOs where investors sell stakes at huge discounts in the five months before the IPO are likely those with significant pricing uncertainty. For restricted stock, the size of the bias was estimated by comparing the discount on ALL private placements to the discount on restricted stock offerings. One study concluded that illiquidity alone accounts for a discount of less than 10%. The remaining 20–25% is explained by sampling problems.
**Rules:** Do not take the ~35% restricted-stock / ~45% pre-IPO discounts at face value; the pure illiquidity component may be under 10%.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (bias assessment).
**Tags:** sampling-bias, illiquidity-discount, restricted-stock, empirical-studies

## p.148 — II. Private company sold to a publicly traded company
**Teaches:** In a private-to-public sale, the seller is undiversified but the buyer (or the buyer's investors) is diversified. As a result, the two parties see different amounts of risk in the same firm. The seller sees more risk (total beta) than the buyer (market beta). Cash flows can also differ because tax rates for publicly traded companies can diverge from those of private owners. Finally, there should be NO illiquidity discount for a public buyer, since the buyer's investors can sell their holdings in a market.
**Rules:** Public (diversified) buyer → use market beta, no illiquidity discount; private (undiversified) seller → total beta plus illiquidity discount. The gap creates a bargaining range.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework for whose perspective sets the discount rate).
**Tags:** private-to-public, total-beta, illiquidity-discount, acquisition

## p.154 — The twists in an initial public offering
**Teaches:** IPO-specific twists. Valuation issues: (1) Use of proceeds — offering proceeds can be held as cash by the firm for future investment, paid out to existing equity investors cashing out, or used to pay down debt; the treatment affects value. (2) Warrants/special deals with prior equity investors — if VCs and earlier investors hold rights to buy or sell equity at pre-specified prices, that affects value per share offered to the public. Pricing issues: (1) Institutional set-up — most IPOs carry investment-banking price guarantees, affecting how they are priced. (2) Follow-up offerings — the proportion of equity offered initially and plans for subsequent offerings affect pricing.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (issue checklist).

## p.155 — A. Use of the proceeds: decision rules
**Teaches:** IPO proceeds can be (a) taken out of the firm by existing owners, (b) used to pay down debt and other obligations, or (c) held as cash to cover future reinvestment needs. How you treat the issuance in valuation depends on the use.
**Rules:** If proceeds are taken out of the firm → ignore them in the valuation. If used to pay down debt → change the debt ratio, which may change the cost of capital and firm value. If held as cash for future reinvestment → add the cash proceeds to the DCF valuation of the company.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC once the use is known (use category → valuation treatment); determining the actual use is judgment/disclosure-reading.

## p.157 — B. Claims from prior equity investors
**Teaches:** When a private firm goes public there are already equity investors — founders, VCs, others — and some may hold warrants, options, or other special claims on the firm's equity. If such special claims exist, value per share must reflect them: value the options at the time of the offering and reduce the value of equity by the option value BEFORE dividing to get value per share.
**Rules:** Equity value per share = (Value of equity − value of options/warrants outstanding) / number of common shares.

## p.159 — C. The investment banking guarantee
**Teaches:** Almost all IPOs are managed by investment banks and backed by a pricing guarantee: the investment banker guarantees the offering price to the issuer. If the issuance price ends up lower than the guaranteed price, the banker must buy the shares at the guaranteed price and potentially bear the loss. This guarantee gives bankers an incentive to underprice IPOs.
