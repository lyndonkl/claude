## p.2 — The Essence of Relative Valuation (Pricing)

**Teaches:** In relative valuation (pricing), an asset's value is inferred from the market prices of similar or comparable assets rather than from its own cash flows. The process has three steps. (1) Identify comparable assets and obtain their market values. (2) Convert those market values into standardized values (price multiples), since raw absolute prices cannot be compared across firms of different sizes. (3) Compare the target's multiple to the comparables' multiples, controlling for any firm-specific differences that might affect the multiple, to judge whether the asset is under- or over-valued.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (identifying comparables and controlling for differences requires reasoning).
**Tags:** relative-valuation, multiples, pricing, comparables

## p.3 — Relative valuation is pervasive

**Teaches:** Most asset valuations in practice are relative, not intrinsic. Almost 85% of equity research reports are based on a multiple and comparables, and more than 50% of all acquisition valuations rest on multiples; rules of thumb built on multiples often drive final valuation judgments. Even many DCF valuations in consulting and corporate finance are relative valuations in disguise: analysts often back into a target obtained from a multiple, and the terminal value in a significant fraction of DCFs is itself estimated with a multiple.

## p.5 — The Market Imperative

**Teaches:** Relative valuation reflects market perceptions and moods far more than DCF does — an advantage when the price must reflect those perceptions, e.g. when selling a security today (an IPO) or when running momentum-based strategies. With relative valuation there will always be a significant proportion of securities classed as undervalued and overvalued (it grades on a curve). Because portfolio managers are judged relative to the market and to other money managers, relative valuation is more tailored to their needs. Relative valuation also generally requires less information than DCF, especially when multiples are used as screens.

## p.6 — Multiples are standardized estimates of price

**Teaches:** A multiple = Numerator (what you are paying for the asset) / Denominator (what you are getting in return). Numerator choices: Market value of equity; Firm value = market value of equity + market value of debt; Enterprise value (EV) = market value of equity + market value of debt − cash (market value of operating assets only). Denominator choices fall into four groups. Revenues: accounting revenues, or drivers such as number of customers/subscribers/units. Earnings: to equity, net income or EPS; to firm, operating income (EBIT). Cash flow: to equity, net income + depreciation or free cash flow to equity; to firm, EBIT + D&A (EBITDA) or free cash flow to firm. Book value: BV of equity; firm = BV of debt + BV of equity; invested capital = BV of equity + BV of debt − cash.
**Formulas:** Firm Value = MV of Equity + MV of Debt. Enterprise Value (EV) = MV of Equity + MV of Debt − Cash. Invested Capital = BV of Equity + BV of Debt − Cash. Multiple = price paid / measure of what you get (revenue, earnings, cash flow, or book value).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given equity value, debt, cash, and a denominator, the multiple follows arithmetically).
**Tags:** multiples, enterprise-value, firm-value, standardization

## p.7 — The Four Steps to Deconstructing Multiples

**Teaches:** Four-step framework for any multiple. (1) Define it — the same multiple is defined differently by different users, so understand how a borrowed multiple was estimated. (2) Describe it — know its cross-sectional distribution; without that you cannot judge whether a value is high or low. (3) Analyze it — understand the fundamentals that drive the multiple and the (usually nonlinear) relationship between the multiple and each driver. (4) Apply it — defining the comparable universe and controlling for differences is far harder in practice than in theory.
**Rules:** Always run all four steps (define, describe, analyze, apply) before using a multiple.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** multiples, framework, four-steps

## p.8 — Definitional Tests

**Teaches:** Two tests of a multiple's definition. Consistency — Proposition 1: numerator and denominator must belong to the same claimholders: equity value goes with equity earnings or equity book value; firm value goes with firm (operating) earnings or firm book value. Mixing them (e.g. price/EBITDA) is inconsistent. Uniformity — the variables must be estimated the same way across every firm in the comparable list; accounting rules for earnings (and book value) must be applied consistently across assets.
**Rules:** If numerator is an equity value, denominator must be an equity measure; if numerator is firm/enterprise value, denominator must be a firm-level measure. Reject or restate multiples estimated non-uniformly across comparables.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of consistency).
**Tags:** multiples, consistency, definitional-tests, claimholders

## p.11 — Example 3: EV/EBITDA multiple

## p.13 — Descriptive Tests

**Teaches:** Questions to ask about a multiple's distribution before using it: What are the average and standard deviation across the market universe? What is the median (often a more reliable comparison point than the mean)? How large are the outliers and how are they handled — throwing out outliers seems obvious, but since outliers of multiples lie mostly on one side (large positive numbers), trimming biases the estimate. Are there cases where the multiple cannot be estimated (e.g. negative earnings), and does ignoring them bias the sample? How has the multiple changed over time?
**Rules:** Prefer the median over the mean for skewed multiples; do not simply discard one-sided outliers; account for firms where the multiple is undefined to avoid selection bias.
**Compute:** MIXED (statistics are deterministic from data; bias handling is judgment).
**Tags:** multiples, distribution, median, outliers, bias

## p.20 — Analytical Tests

**Teaches:** Before using a multiple, ask what fundamentals determine it and how changes in those fundamentals change it. Proposition 2: embedded in every multiple are all the variables that drive a DCF valuation — growth, risk, and cash flow patterns. The relationship between a fundamental (like growth) and a multiple (like PE) is almost never linear. Proposition 3: it is impossible to properly compare firms on a multiple without knowing how the fundamentals and the multiple move together.
**Rules:** Do not compare firms on a multiple without identifying its fundamental drivers (growth, risk, cash flows) and the (nonlinear) relationship between driver and multiple.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** multiples, fundamentals, analytical-tests, growth, risk

## p.21 — A Simple Analytical Device: deriving intrinsic multiples

**Teaches:** A recipe for deriving the intrinsic version of any multiple. (1) Start with a basic intrinsic value model — a dividend/FCFE model for equity multiples, an operating asset value model for EV multiples. (2) Divide both sides of the equation by the denominator of the multiple you want to deconstruct. (3) The result is an intrinsic version of the multiple expressed as a function of fundamentals.
**Formulas:** Equity example: Price = EPS × Payout / (r − g); dividing by book value gives Price/Book = ROE × Payout / (r − g), so Price/Book = f(ROE, r, g, Payout). EV example: EV = EBIT(1 − t)(1 − RIR) / (WACC − g); dividing by sales gives EV/Sales = After-tax Operating Margin × (1 − RIR) / (WACC − g), so EV/Sales = f(after-tax operating margin, RIR, WACC, g). Symbols: EPS = earnings per share; Payout = dividend payout ratio; r = cost of equity; g = stable growth rate; ROE = return on equity; t = tax rate; RIR = reinvestment rate; WACC = cost of capital.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (fundamentals in → intrinsic multiple out).
**Tags:** intrinsic-multiples, price-to-book, ev-to-sales, drivers

## p.22 — I. PE Ratios from fundamentals (stable growth)

**Teaches:** To understand PE fundamentals, start from a stable-growth equity DCF model and divide by current EPS. PE is driven by the payout ratio (or FCFE/Earnings), expected stable growth, and the cost of equity.
**Formulas:** Dividend discount model: P0 = DPS1 / (r − gn), where P0 = price today, DPS1 = expected dividends per share next year, r = cost of equity, gn = stable growth rate. Dividing by current EPS0: P0/EPS0 = PE = Payout Ratio × (1 + gn) / (r − gn). FCFE version: P0 = FCFE1 / (r − gn) and P0/EPS0 = PE = (FCFE/Earnings) × (1 + gn) / (r − gn).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (payout, g, r → PE).
**Tags:** pe-ratio, dividend-discount-model, fcfe, stable-growth

## p.23 — Fundamental PE for a high-growth firm (two-stage model)

**Teaches:** PE for a high-growth firm follows from the two-stage dividend discount model: value equals the present value of dividends during n years of high growth plus the terminal value discounted back. Dividing both sides by EPS0 yields the two-stage intrinsic PE. For a firm that does not pay out what it can afford in dividends, substitute FCFE/Earnings for the payout ratio.
**Formulas:** P0 = [EPS0 × Payout Ratio × (1+g) × (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)] / (r − g) + [EPS0 × Payout Ratio_n × (1+g)^n × (1+g_n)] / [(r − g_n)(1+r)^n]. Dividing by EPS0: P0/EPS0 = [Payout Ratio × (1+g) × (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)] / (r − g) + [Payout Ratio_n × (1+g)^n × (1+g_n)] / [(r − g_n)(1+r)^n]. Symbols: EPS0 = current earnings per share; g = high-growth-phase growth rate; g_n = stable growth rate after year n; Payout Ratio = payout during high growth; Payout Ratio_n = stable-phase payout; r = cost of equity; n = length of high-growth period in years.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (g, g_n, payouts, r, n → PE).
**Tags:** pe-ratio, two-stage-model, high-growth, intrinsic-multiples

## p.24 — A Simple Example: intrinsic PE computation

**Teaches:** Worked example computing an intrinsic PE with the two-stage formula.
**Example:** Firm characteristics — high-growth phase: expected growth 25%, payout ratio 20%, beta 1.00, lasting 5 years; stable phase: growth 8%, payout 50%, beta 1.00, forever after year 5. Riskfree rate = T.Bond rate = 6%; required return r = 6% + 1 × (5.5%) = 11.5%. Computation: P0/EPS0 = [0.20 × 1.25 × (1 − 1.25^5/1.115^5)] / (0.115 − 0.25) + [0.50 × 1.25^5 × 1.08] / [(0.115 − 0.08) × 1.115^5] = 28.75. Intrinsic PE = 28.75.
**Formulas:** Same two-stage PE formula as p.23; required return via CAPM: r = riskfree rate + beta × equity risk premium (6% + 1 × 5.5% = 11.5%).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (growth rates, payouts, beta, riskfree, ERP, n → PE = 28.75).
**Tags:** pe-ratio, worked-example, two-stage-model, capm

## p.35 — II. PEG Ratio: definition and fundamentals

**Teaches:** PEG ratio = PE ratio / expected growth rate in EPS. For consistency the growth rate must match the EPS base used in the PE (current, trailing, or forward) and be over the same time period. The fundamental PEG is obtained by taking the two-stage DDM value, dividing by earnings (giving PE), then dividing again by expected growth g.
**Formulas:** PEG = PE / expected growth rate in EPS. Two-stage value: P0 = [EPS0 × Payout Ratio × (1+g) × (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)] / (r − g) + [EPS0 × Payout Ratio_n × (1+g)^n × (1+g_n)] / [(r − g_n)(1+r)^n]. Fundamental PEG: PEG = [Payout Ratio × (1+g) × (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+r)^n)] / [g(r − g)] + [Payout Ratio_n × (1+g)^n × (1+g_n)] / [g(r − g_n)(1+r)^n]. Symbols as on p.23; g in the denominator is the expected high-growth rate (as a decimal in the formula; conventionally PEG is quoted with g in percent).
**Rules:** Growth rate used in PEG must be consistent with the EPS definition in the PE and cover the same period.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (payouts, g, g_n, r, n → PEG).
**Tags:** peg-ratio, growth, intrinsic-multiples, consistency

## p.36 — PEG ratios and fundamentals

**Teaches:** Risk and payout, which drive PE, continue to drive PEG — so comparing PEG ratios across companies still embeds implicit assumptions about those variables. Moreover, dividing PE by expected growth does NOT neutralize growth's effect, because the relationship between growth and value is nonlinear and complex even in a 2-stage model. A low PEG is therefore not automatically cheap: low-growth or high-risk firms can have structurally different PEGs.
**Rules:** Do not treat PEG as growth-neutral; control for risk, payout, and the level of growth when comparing PEGs.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** peg-ratio, growth, risk, nonlinearity
