## p.41 — PEG Ratios and Fundamentals: Propositions

**Teaches:** Three propositions on why PEG ratios are not neutral to fundamentals. Proposition 1: high-risk companies trade at much lower PEG ratios than low-risk companies with the same expected growth rate; so the firm that looks most undervalued on PEG in a sector may simply be the riskiest firm. Proposition 2: companies that attain growth more efficiently (investing less in better-return projects) have higher PEG ratios than firms growing at the same rate less efficiently; firms that look cheap on PEG may just have high reinvestment rates and poor project returns. Proposition 3: companies with very low or very high growth rates tend to have higher PEG ratios than firms with average growth rates, with the bias worse for low-growth stocks — PEG ratios do not neutralize the growth effect.
**Rules:** A low PEG ratio alone does not indicate undervaluation — check whether it is driven by high risk, inefficient growth, or the non-linear growth bias before concluding a stock is cheap.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (interpreting why a PEG differs from peers requires assessing risk, reinvestment efficiency, and growth level).
**Tags:** peg-ratio, relative-valuation, equity-multiples, growth, risk

## p.42 — III. Price to Book Ratio: Derivation from DDM

**Teaches:** Derives the price-to-book (PBV) ratio from the stable-growth dividend discount model. Starting from P0 = DPS1/(r − gn) and defining ROE = EPS0 / Book Value of Equity, the equity value can be re-expressed in terms of book value, ROE, payout ratio, and growth, and dividing by book value gives the PBV multiple. If ROE is measured on next-period (expected) earnings, the (1+gn) term drops out.
**Formulas:**
- P0 = DPS1 / (r − gn), where P0 = price today, DPS1 = expected dividends per share next year, r = cost of equity, gn = stable growth rate.
- ROE = EPS0 / Book Value of Equity (EPS0 = current earnings per share).
- P0 = [BV0 × ROE × Payout Ratio × (1 + gn)] / (r − gn), where BV0 = current book value of equity.
- P0/BV0 = PBV = [ROE × Payout Ratio × (1 + gn)] / (r − gn) (ROE on current earnings).
- P0/BV0 = PBV = (ROE × Payout Ratio) / (r − gn) if ROE is based on expected next-period earnings.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs ROE, payout ratio, gn, r → PBV multiple).
**Tags:** price-to-book, pbv, dividend-discount-model, equity-multiples, roe

## p.43 — Price/Book Value for a Stable-Growth Firm: ROE vs Cost of Equity

**Teaches:** Simplifies the PBV formula further by substituting the sustainable growth relation g = (1 − Payout ratio) × ROE. The result shows that the price-book ratio of a stable firm is determined by the differential between its return on equity and its required rate of return. A firm expected to earn ROE above (below, equal to) its cost of equity should trade at a price-to-book ratio above (below, equal to) one.
**Formulas:**
- g = (1 − Payout ratio) × ROE (sustainable growth rate).
- P0/BV0 = PBV = (ROE − gn) / (r − gn), where ROE = return on equity, gn = stable growth rate, r = cost of equity.
**Rules:** If expected ROE > cost of equity, PBV should be > 1; if ROE < cost of equity, PBV should be < 1; if ROE = cost of equity, PBV should be = 1.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROE, gn, r → PBV).
**Tags:** price-to-book, excess-returns, cost-of-equity, stable-growth

## p.44 — EV/Book Capital: Enterprise-Value Analogue of PBV

**Teaches:** Translates the price-to-book logic to an enterprise-value multiple. Starting from the stable-growth FCFF model V0 = FCFF1/(WACC − g), dividing by book capital and substituting FCFF = EBIT(1−t) − (g/ROC)×EBIT(1−t) shows that the value-to-book-capital ratio is driven by the spread between return on capital and growth relative to the spread between WACC and growth.
**Formulas:**
- V0 = FCFF1 / (WACC − g), where V0 = value of operating assets, FCFF1 = expected free cash flow to the firm next year, WACC = weighted average cost of capital, g = stable growth rate.
- V0/BV = (FCFF1/BV) / (WACC − g), BV = book value of invested capital.
- FCFF = EBIT(1−t) − (g/ROC) × EBIT(1−t), where t = tax rate, ROC = return on capital (g/ROC = reinvestment rate).
- V0/BV = (ROC − g) / (WACC − g).
**Rules:** Analogous to PBV: EV/Book Capital exceeds 1 only when ROC > WACC.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, g, WACC → EV/book capital).
**Tags:** ev-to-book, enterprise-value-multiples, fcff, return-on-capital, wacc

## p.45 — IV. EV to EBITDA — Determinants

**Teaches:** Derives EV/EBITDA from the stable-growth FCFF model by expanding FCFF into its EBITDA components and dividing through by EBITDA. The determinants of EV/EBITDA are: the cost of capital, the expected growth rate, the tax rate, and the reinvestment rate (or return on capital).
**Formulas:**
- EV0 = FCFF1 / (WACC − g).
- EV = [EBITDA(1−t) + Depr(t) − CEx − Δ Working Capital] / (WACC − g), where Depr(t) = depreciation × tax rate (the depreciation tax shield), CEx = capital expenditures, Δ Working Capital = change in working capital, t = tax rate.
- EV/EBITDA = (1−t)/(WACC−g) + [Depr(t)/EBITDA]/(WACC−g) − [CEx/EBITDA]/(WACC−g) − [Δ Working Capital/EBITDA]/(WACC−g).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (t, WACC, g, Depr/EBITDA, CEx/EBITDA, ΔWC/EBITDA → EV/EBITDA).
**Tags:** ev-ebitda, enterprise-value-multiples, fcff, determinants

## p.46 — A Simple Example: Computing an Intrinsic EV/EBITDA

**Teaches:** Applies the EV/EBITDA determinant formula to a hypothetical stable-growth firm to compute its intrinsic Value/EBITDA multiple.
**Example:** Firm with Tax Rate = 36%; CapEx/EBITDA = 30%; Depreciation/EBITDA = 20%; Cost of Capital = 10%; no working capital requirements; stable growth of 5% forever. Value/EBITDA = (1 − 0.36)/(0.10 − 0.05) + (0.2 × 0.36)/(0.10 − 0.05) − 0.3/(0.10 − 0.05) − 0/(0.10 − 0.05) = 8.24.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (tax rate, CapEx/EBITDA, Depr/EBITDA, WACC, g, ΔWC/EBITDA → intrinsic EV/EBITDA = 8.24).
**Tags:** ev-ebitda, worked-example, intrinsic-multiple

## p.47 — The Determinants of EV/EBITDA (sensitivity charts)

**Teaches:** Three sensitivity charts showing how the intrinsic EV/EBITDA multiple varies with its drivers. (3) Excess returns: EV/EBITDA rises with return on capital, and at each ROC level the multiple is higher when WACC is lower (curves shown for WACC = 8%, 9%, 10%, with ROC on the x-axis from 6% to 15%).

## p.48 — V. EV/Sales Ratio

**Teaches:** When pre-tax operating margins are used, the appropriate multiple is enterprise value to sales. Substituting the expanded FCFF = EBIT(1 − tax rate)(1 − Reinvestment Rate) into a two-stage FCFF model gives Value/Sales as after-tax operating margin times a bracketed term reflecting growth, reinvestment, and discounting over both the high-growth and stable phases.
**Formulas:**
- Free Cash Flow to the Firm = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) × (1 − Reinvestment Rate).
- Value/Sales0 = After-tax Operating Margin × { [(1 − RIR_growth)(1+g) × (1 − (1+g)^n/(1+WACC)^n)] / (WACC − g) + [(1 − RIR_stable)(1+g)^n × (1+gn)] / [(WACC − gn)(1+WACC)^n] }.
- Symbols: g = growth rate in after-tax operating income for the first n years. gn = stable growth rate after year n, forever. RIR_growth and RIR_stable = reinvestment rates in the high-growth and stable periods. WACC = weighted average cost of capital. n = length of the high-growth period. After-tax Operating Margin = EBIT(1−t)/Sales.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (after-tax margin, g, gn, n, RIR_growth, RIR_stable, WACC → Value/Sales).
**Tags:** ev-sales, two-stage-model, operating-margin, enterprise-value-multiples

## p.49 — The Value of a Brand Name

**Teaches:** Traditional valuation is criticized for ignoring brand names and other intangibles, and ad-hoc brand valuation approaches can badly over- or understate their value. A well-known brand lets a firm charge higher prices for the same products, producing higher margins, higher price-to-sales ratios, and higher firm value — so brand value can be measured as the difference in value-to-sales ratios between the branded firm and a generic-product firm, applied to sales.
**Formulas:** Value of brand name = [(V/S)_b − (V/S)_g] × Sales, where (V/S)_b = value of firm/sales ratio with the benefit of the brand name and (V/S)_g = value of firm/sales ratio of a firm with the generic product.
**Compute:** MIXED (the arithmetic is deterministic given the two V/S ratios and sales; estimating the generic firm's margin/ratio is judgment).
**Tags:** brand-value, intangibles, ev-sales, price-to-sales

## p.51 — The Determinants of Multiples… (master summary map)

**Teaches:** A single summary diagram deriving every standard equity multiple from the DDM and every enterprise-value multiple from the FCFF model, with each multiple written as a function of its fundamental drivers. Equity multiples all start from P = Dividends1/(ke − g) = Net Income1(1 − Payout)/(ke − g); EV multiples all start from EV = FCFF1/(WACC − g) = EBIT1(1−t)(1 − RIR)/(WACC − g).
**Formulas:**
Equity side (ke = cost of equity, g = growth, Payout = dividends/net income, ROE = Net Income1/Book Equity0, Net Margin = Net Income/Sales):
- P/Div1 = 1/(ke − g); Div1/P (dividend yield) = f(ke, g).
- P/E1 = (1 − Payout)/(ke − g); PE = f(ke, g, payout).
- P/Book Equity = ROE(1 − Payout)/(ke − g); PBV = f(ROE, ke, g, payout).
- P/Sales1 = Net Margin(1 − Payout)/(ke − g); PS = f(ROE, ke, g, payout, net margin).
- Variants of Net Income (E): Net Income1 = Net Margin × Sales = ROE × Book Equity = Net Income0(1+g).
Enterprise-value side (WACC, g; RIR = reinvestment rate = (CapEx − DA + ChgWC)/EBIT(1−t); IC = invested capital = Book Equity + Debt − Cash; ATOM = after-tax operating margin = EBIT(1−t)/Sales; ROIC = EBIT1(1−t)/IC):
- EV/FCFF1 = 1/(WACC − g).
- EV/EBIT1(1−t) = (1 − RIR)/(WACC − g).
- EV/EBIT1 = (1−t)(1 − RIR)/(WACC − g).
- EV/Sales1 = ATOM(1 − RIR)/(WACC − g).
- EV/IC = ROIC(1 − RIR)/(WACC − g).
- Variants of EBIT(1−t): EBIT1(1−t) = ATOM × Sales = ROIC × IC = EBITDA1(1−t) + t×DA.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (each multiple computes from its listed fundamentals).
**Tags:** multiples-determinants, equity-multiples, enterprise-value-multiples, cheat-sheet

## p.52 — Application Tests

**Teaches:** Two tests for applying multiples. First, what is a "comparable" firm: traditional analysis assumes same-sector firms are comparable, but valuation theory says a comparable is any firm similar in fundamentals — risk, growth, and cash flow characteristics — even if in a very different business. Second, given comparables, how do you adjust for differences: since no exactly identical firm exists, you must control for differences across firms on the fundamentals.
**Rules:** Comparability is defined by risk, growth, and cash-flow similarity, not by sector membership; always control for fundamental differences before comparing multiples.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** comparable-firms, relative-valuation, application-tests

## p.53 — 1. The Sampling Choice

**Teaches:** Ideally you would compare your firm's pricing to many publicly traded firms that are just like it in fundamentals, needing no controls. In practice firms sharing the same risk, growth, and cash-flow characteristics are very rare, so the sampling trade-off is a spectrum: a small sample of firms that are "just like" your firm versus a large sample of firms similar on some dimensions but different on others.
**Rules:** Small tight sample = fewer control adjustments but noisy statistics; large loose sample = better statistics but requires controlling for differences.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** comparable-firms, sampling, relative-valuation

## p.54 — 2. The "Control for Differences" Choices

**Teaches:** Four ways to control for differences across comparable firms: (1) Direct comparisons — if comparables are "just like" your firm, compare multiples directly and call the firm expensive (cheap) if its multiple is higher (lower). (2) Story telling — if firms vary on one key dimension, tell a story about how value varies on that dimension (e.g., "this company trades at 12x earnings versus 10x for the sector, but is cheap because it has much higher growth"). (3) Modified multiple — modify the multiple to incorporate the differing dimension (e.g., PEG). (4) Statistical techniques — if firms vary on more than one dimension, use multiple regressions (or variants) to get a "controlled" estimate.
**Rules:** Choose the control technique by how many dimensions the comparables differ on: none → direct comparison; one → story telling or modified multiple; several → regression.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of technique); the regression option is MIXED once specified.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, controls, regression, modified-multiples

## p.61 — The Median Test

**Teaches:** A simplistic screening rule using sector medians as "par": we want stocks with low PBV, high ROE, and low risk, and the medians of each variable define what counts as low or high. Applied to the European bank sample: undervalued stocks trade at PBV below the sector median (2.07), generate ROE above the sector median (11.82%), and have standard deviation below the median (21.93%); overvalued stocks trade above the median PBV while generating below-median ROE.
**Rules:** Undervalued: PBV < median PBV (2.07) AND ROE > median ROE (11.82%) AND std dev < median std dev (21.93%). Overvalued: PBV > median PBV AND ROE < median ROE.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (each firm's PBV, ROE, std dev vs the three medians → under/overvalued classification).
**Tags:** median-test, screening, price-to-book, roe, risk

## p.77 — Solution 1: Use Proxies for Survival and Growth — Amazon in Early 2000

**Teaches:** When current fundamentals fail, use proxies for survival and future success: hypothesizing that firms with higher revenue growth and higher cash balances have a greater chance of surviving and becoming profitable, regress PS on ln(Revenues) (size control), revenue growth, and cash/revenues. Applied to Amazon: predicted PS 30.42 vs actual PS 25.63, so Amazon is undervalued relative to other internet stocks (which says nothing about absolute value).
**Formulas:** PS = 30.61 − 2.77 × ln(Rev) + 6.42 × (Rev Growth) + 5.11 × (Cash/Rev); t-statistics: ln(Rev) 0.66, Rev Growth 2.63, Cash/Rev 3.49; R squared = 31.8%. Symbols: ln(Rev) = natural log of revenues (size proxy), Rev Growth = revenue growth rate, Cash/Rev = cash balance divided by revenues.
**Example:** Amazon early 2000: Predicted PS = 30.61 − 2.77 × (7.1039) + 6.42 × (1.9946) + 5.11 × (0.3069) = 30.42. Actual PS = 25.63 → undervalued relative to other internet stocks.

## p.78 — Solution 2: Use Forward Multiples — Watch Out for Bumps in the Road (Tesla)

**Teaches:** Waterfall chart showing why forward (year-10) value cannot simply be discounted at the risk-free rate for a young company like Tesla — each real-world "bump" shrinks the value. Steps: Estimated value in year 10 = $68,271m (terminal value stack of $16,221 + $24,300 + $14,936 + smaller pieces + $3,645 + $8,152). Discounting at the 2.75% riskfree rate gives a value today of $52,050m. Using the risk-adjusted cost of capital of 10.03% instead gives $27,750m. Adjusting for dilution from new equity issues to finance growth gives $12,814m. Adjusting for a 10% failure risk gives $12,174m. Adjusting for debt and cash gives $11,797m. Adjusting for a 25.06m option overhang gives $8,152m of equity value.
**Rules:** When working back from forward multiples/values: discount at the risk-adjusted cost of capital (not risk-free), then adjust for expected dilution, failure risk, net debt, and option overhang.
**Example:** Tesla: $68,271m year-10 value → $52,050m (riskfree 2.75% discounting, wrong) → $27,750m (10.03% cost of capital) → $12,814m (dilution) → $12,174m (10% failure risk) → $11,797m (net debt & cash) → $8,152m (after 25.06m options).
**Compute:** MIXED (each discounting/adjustment step is deterministic given the inputs; estimating cost of capital, dilution, and failure probability is judgment).
**Tags:** forward-multiples, tesla, young-companies, failure-risk, dilution
