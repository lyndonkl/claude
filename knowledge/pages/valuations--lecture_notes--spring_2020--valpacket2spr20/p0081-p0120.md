## p.81 — Pricing across the entire market: why not?

**Teaches:** As an alternative to the 'comparable firm' approach (a small peer group), you can use the entire cross-section of firms in a market to predict PE ratios. The simplest way to summarize the market-wide information is a multiple regression with the PE ratio as the dependent variable and proxies for risk, growth, and payout as the independent variables. This lets every firm in the market inform the predicted multiple for any one firm.

## p.84 — Problems with the regression methodology

**Teaches:** Three weaknesses of market-wide PE regressions. First, the regression assumes a linear relationship between PE and the financial proxies; that may not be appropriate. Second, the relationship between PE and fundamentals may not be stable over time. If it shifts from year to year, predictions become unreliable. Third, the independent variables are correlated with each other (e.g., high-growth firms tend to be high-risk). This multicollinearity makes coefficients unreliable and may explain large period-to-period swings in coefficients.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** regression-problems, multicollinearity, pe-ratio, methodology

## p.92 — Statistically insignificant coefficients

**Teaches:** If a regression coefficient is statistically insignificant, it only adds noise to the prediction and should be dropped — even if fundamentals say the variable should matter, because in pricing it is the market that determines what matters. With small samples, don't overload the regression with independent variables.
**Rules:** t-statistic thresholds: t > 2 is very good; t between 1 and 2 is marginal; t < 1 is noise. Drop insignificant variables from pricing regressions.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (t-stat classification); the drop/keep decision is largely rule-based.
**Tags:** t-statistic, significance, regression-hygiene

## p.99 — Relative valuation: closing propositions

**Teaches:** Proposition 1: In a relative valuation, you only conclude that a stock is under- or over-valued RELATIVE to your comparable group — your relative judgment can be right while the stock is still hopelessly overvalued in absolute terms. Proposition 2: In asset valuation there are no truly similar assets; every asset is unique, so if you do not control for fundamental differences in risk, cash flows, and growth across firms, your conclusions reflect your flawed judgments rather than market misvaluations. Bottom line: relative valuation is pricing, not valuation.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, pricing-vs-value, propositions

## p.100 — Reviewing: the four steps to understanding multiples

**Teaches:** A four-step process for any multiple: (1) Define the multiple — check numerator/denominator consistency and that it is estimated uniformly across firms; (2) Describe the multiple — multiples have skewed distributions so averages are seldom good indicators of typical multiples, and check for bias when the multiple cannot be estimated (e.g., firms dropped for negative earnings); (3) Analyze the multiple — identify the companion variable that drives it and examine the nature of the relationship; (4) Apply the multiple.
**Rules:** Define consistently and uniformly; use medians not means (skewed distributions); identify the companion variable before applying.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** multiples-framework, four-steps, relative-valuation

## p.102 — What is asset based valuation?

**Teaches:** Three ways to value a business: intrinsic valuation values it from the cash flows it is expected to generate over time; relative valuation values it from how similar businesses are priced; asset-based valuation values the business by valuing its individual assets (tangible or intangible) one at a time.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, definitions, valuation-approaches

## p.103 — Why would you do asset based valuation?

**Teaches:** Three motives: (1) Liquidation — when selling assets piecemeal rather than as a going business, you want the value of each asset or asset class individually; (2) Accounting mission — US and international standards have moved to "fair value" accounting, requiring balance sheets restated at fair rather than book value; (3) Sum of the parts — value divisions individually either as a potential acquirer planning restructuring, or as an investor looking for firms trading below the sum of their parts ("cheap").
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, liquidation, fair-value, sum-of-the-parts

## p.104 — How do you do asset based valuation?

**Teaches:** Three ways to attach value to each asset: intrinsic value (estimate expected cash flows on each asset/asset class and discount at a risk-adjusted rate); relative value (look at recent sale prices of similar assets); accounting value (use book value as a proxy for asset value).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, methods, book-value

## p.105 — When is asset-based valuation easiest?

**Teaches:** Asset-based valuation is easiest when: (1) assets are separable — a collection of real estate holdings or independent businesses is easy; interrelated assets like brand name (which cuts across assets) are hard to value separately; (2) earnings/cash flows can be traced to individual assets — much harder when the business earns money but individual assets' roles cannot be isolated; (3) there is an active market for similar assets to draw transaction prices from (needed for relative valuation of the assets).
**Rules:** Prefer asset-based valuation when assets are separable, have stand-alone cash flows, and have an active market of comparable transactions.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, separability, applicability

## p.106 — I. Liquidation valuation

**Teaches:** Liquidation valuation assesses what you would get from selling the business's assets today rather than operating it as a going concern. It makes more sense to PRICE the assets (relative valuation) than to value them intrinsically: for separable, traded assets (e.g., real estate) pricing is easy; for assets that are not, book value is often used as a proxy for or basis of liquidation value. If the liquidation is urgent, attach an additional discount to the estimated value.
**Rules:** Urgent liquidation → apply a discount to estimated asset values; non-traded assets → book value as proxy.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** liquidation-valuation, fire-sale-discount, book-value

## p.107 — II. Accounting valuation: glimmers from FAS 157

**Teaches:** FAS 157 fair-value accounting asks accountants to attach the values that "market participants" would pay/receive. The definition tilts toward relative value: fair value is an EXIT price (price received to sell an asset or paid to transfer a liability), not an entry price, and the hierarchy puts market prices at the top, accepting intrinsic value only when market prices are unavailable. There is a split mission: accountants must also back relative valuations with intrinsic valuations, which often leads to reverse engineering — arriving at values first and building the valuations later.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** fas-157, fair-value-accounting, exit-price

## p.108 — III. Sum of the parts valuation

**Teaches:** You can value a company in pieces using either relative or intrinsic valuation; which you use depends on who you are and your motives. A long-term passive investor hunting for market mistakes that will correct over time should do an INTRINSIC valuation of the individual assets. An activist investor planning to acquire the company or push for a break-up should focus on RELATIVE valuation, since the goal is to split the company and capture the pricing gain.
**Rules:** Passive long-term investor → intrinsic sum-of-parts; activist/acquirer → relative (pricing) sum-of-parts.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** sum-of-the-parts, activist-investing, intrinsic-vs-relative
