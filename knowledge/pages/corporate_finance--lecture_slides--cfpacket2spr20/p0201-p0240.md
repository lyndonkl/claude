## p.201 — A Practical Framework for Analyzing Dividend Policy

**Teaches:** A decision tree for judging a firm's dividend policy. Step 1: compare what the firm actually paid out (Dividends + Equity Repurchases) with what it could have paid out (FCFE). Step 2: if the firm pays out too little (FCFE > Dividends), ask whether you trust managers with the cash. Judge trust from past project choice: compare ROE to Cost of Equity and ROC to WACC. A history of good projects earns managers the flexibility to keep cash and set dividends. A poor history means you force managers to justify holding cash, or return it to stockholders. If the firm pays out too much (FCFE < Dividends), look at its investment opportunities. With good projects it should cut dividends and reinvest more. With poor projects it should fix its investment problem first and then cut dividends.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) × (1 − DR) − Change in Working Capital × (1 − DR), where DR = debt ratio (fraction of reinvestment funded by debt). Cash actually returned = Dividends + Equity Repurchases.
**Rules:** If FCFE > Dividends: firm pays out too little → assess trust in management via past project quality (ROE vs Cost of Equity, ROC vs WACC); good history → flexibility, poor history → force justification or cash return. If FCFE < Dividends: firm pays out too much → good projects: cut dividends and reinvest; poor projects: fix investment problem first, then cut dividends.
**Compute:** MIXED — computing FCFE and comparing to cash returned is deterministic (inputs: net income, cap ex, depreciation, change in WC, debt ratio, dividends, buybacks → FCFE and surplus/deficit); the trust/project-quality judgment is JUDGMENT.

## p.202 — A Dividend Matrix

**Teaches:** A 2x2 matrix classifying firms by (a) cash returned relative to FCFE (cash surplus: cash return < FCFE; cash deficit: cash return > FCFE) and (b) quality of projects taken (excess returns: poor vs good). Cash Surplus + Poor Projects: heavy pressure to pay out more to stockholders as dividends or buybacks. Cash Surplus + Good Projects: maximum freedom in setting dividend policy. Cash Deficit + Poor Projects: cut or end cash return, but the real problem is investment policy. Cash Deficit + Good Projects: reduce cash payout, if any, to stockholders.
**Rules:** Cash return < FCFE and poor projects → pressure to pay out more; cash return < FCFE and good projects → maximum flexibility; cash return > FCFE and poor projects → cut cash return but fix investment policy; cash return > FCFE and good projects → reduce cash payout.
**Compute:** MIXED — classification into quadrant is deterministic given FCFE, cash returned, and excess returns; the advice part is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dividend-policy, dividend-matrix, fcfe, excess-returns

## p.225 — First Principles (of corporate finance)

**Teaches:** The recurring first-principles map: maximize the value of the business (firm), via three decisions. Investment decision: invest in assets earning a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the debt/equity mix used to fund it. Returns should reflect the magnitude and timing of cash flows and all side effects. Financing decision: find the right kind of debt and the right mix of debt and equity. The optimal mix maximizes firm value; the right kind of debt matches the tenor of your assets. Dividend decision: if you cannot find investments that beat your hurdle rate, return the cash to owners. How much depends on current and potential investment opportunities. The form (dividends vs buybacks) depends on stockholder preferences.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (a concept map)
**Tags:** first-principles, corporate-finance, investment-financing-dividend

## p.226 — Three approaches to valuation

**Teaches:** Intrinsic valuation: the value of an asset is a function of its fundamentals — cash flows, growth, and risk — generally estimated with discounted cash flow models. Relative valuation: the value is estimated from what investors are paying for similar assets, generally via value or price multiples across firms in the same business. Contingent claim valuation: when cash flows are contingent on an external event, value can be estimated using option pricing models.

## p.227 — Discounted Cash Flow Valuation (annotated formula)

**Teaches:** How a DCF is built: value equals discounted expected cash flows. Base earnings reflect the earnings power of existing assets net of taxes and sustaining reinvestment (cash flows from existing assets). The value of growth is the net of how quickly earnings grow (positive) against required reinvestment (negative): Expected Cash Flow in year t = Expected Earnings in year t − Reinvestment needed for growth. Risk is captured in the discount rate — beta in the cost of equity and the default spread in the cost of debt. Steady state: the value of growth comes from the capacity to generate excess returns, and the length of the growth period comes from the strength and sustainability of competitive advantages.
**Formulas:** Value of asset = E(CF1)/(1+r) + E(CF2)/(1+r)^2 + E(CF3)/(1+r)^3 + … + E(CFn)/(1+r)^n. Here E(CFt) = expected cash flow in period t. r = discount rate reflecting the risk of the cash flows. E(CF) in year t = Expected Earnings in year t − Reinvestment needed for growth.
**Compute:** MIXED — PV computation deterministic given cash flows and r; estimating cash flows, growth, and r is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, intrinsic-value, growth, discount-rate

## p.228 — Equity Valuation

**Teaches:** The value of equity is obtained by discounting expected cash flows to equity at the cost of equity. Cash flows to equity are the leftover cash flows after all expenses, tax obligations, and interest and principal payments. The cost of equity is the rate of return required by equity investors. The dividend discount model is a specialized case of equity valuation: the value of a stock is the present value of expected future dividends.
**Formulas:** Value of Equity = Sum over t = 1 to n of [CF to Equity_t / (1 + ke)^t], where CF to Equity_t = expected cash flow to equity in period t and ke = cost of equity.
**Compute:** MIXED — discounting deterministic given cash flows and ke; estimating them is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** equity-valuation, cost-of-equity, dividend-discount-model, dcf

## p.229 — Firm Valuation

**Teaches:** The value of the firm is obtained by discounting expected cash flows to the firm at the weighted average cost of capital (WACC). Cash flows to the firm are the leftover cash flows after operating expenses and taxes but prior to debt payments. WACC is the cost of the different financing components used by the firm, weighted by their shares of market value.
**Formulas:** Value of Firm = Sum over t = 1 to n of [CF to Firm_t / (1 + WACC)^t], where CF to Firm_t = expected cash flow to the firm in period t and WACC = weighted average cost of capital.
**Compute:** MIXED — discounting deterministic given cash flows and WACC; estimating them is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** firm-valuation, wacc, fcff, dcf

## p.230 — Choosing a Cash Flow to Discount

**Teaches:** Rules for picking the cash flow in a DCF. When you cannot estimate free cash flows to equity or the firm (e.g., financial service firms), the only discountable cash flow is dividends — for Deutsche Bank, dividends are discounted. If a firm's debt ratio is not expected to change, FCFE can be discounted to get equity value directly — used for Tata Motors. If the debt ratio might change over time, FCFE becomes hard to estimate, so discount FCFF instead — used for Vale and Disney.
**Rules:** Financial service firm / cannot estimate FCFE or FCFF → discount dividends. Stable debt ratio → discount FCFE at cost of equity. Changing debt ratio → discount FCFF at cost of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (model-selection rules)
**Tags:** dcf, model-choice, fcfe, fcff, dividend-discount-model

## p.231 — Stories + Numbers = Value

**Teaches:** A good valuation bridges the "numbers people" and the "narrative people." Numbers people favor accounting statements, Excel spreadsheets, statistical measures, and pricing data; their illusions/delusions are precision (data is precise), objectivity (data has no bias), and control (data can control reality). Narrative people favor anecdotes, experience (own or others'), and behavioral evidence; their illusions/delusions are that creativity cannot be quantified, that a good story guarantees a good investment, and that experience is the best teacher. A good valuation connects story to numbers.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** valuation-philosophy, narrative-and-numbers, behavioral

## p.232 — The Ingredients that determine value

**Teaches:** Timeline diagram of a DCF's inputs. Cash flows can be measured after debt payments to equity (dividends, FCFE) or before debt payments to the firm (FCFF). Growth rates can be in equity earnings (net income, EPS) or in operating earnings. Expected cash flows during an extraordinary growth phase are followed by a terminal value when the firm is in stable growth it can sustain forever. Discount rate: cost of equity if cash flows are equity cash flows, cost of capital if cash flows are to the firm. Present value: value of equity if equity cash flows are discounted at cost of equity; value of the operating assets of the firm if firm cash flows are discounted at cost of capital.
**Rules:** Match cash flow to discount rate: equity cash flows ↔ cost of equity → equity value; firm cash flows ↔ cost of capital → value of operating assets. Terminal value requires stable growth sustainable forever.
**Compute:** MIXED — discounting mechanics deterministic; estimating growth phase, terminal state, and rates is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, terminal-value, consistency-principle, valuation-inputs

## p.233 — I. Estimating Cash Flows

**Teaches:** The menu of cash flows used in valuation. Cash flow to equity comes in three versions: dividends; augmented dividends (dividends + stock buybacks); and free cash flow to equity (the potential dividend). Free cash flow to the firm is a pre-debt measure.
**Formulas:** FCFE (potential dividend) = Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in Working Capital + (Debt issued − Debt repaid). FCFF = EBIT × (1 − tax rate) − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in Working Capital. Augmented Dividends = Dividends + Stock Buybacks. All symbols: Cap Ex = capital expenditures; EBIT = earnings before interest and taxes.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given net income/EBIT, tax rate, cap ex, depreciation, change in WC, and net debt issued → FCFE and FCFF.
**Tags:** cash-flow-estimation, fcfe, fcff, augmented-dividends

## p.236 — Estimating FCFF: Disney

**Teaches:** Worked FCFF computation for Disney (fiscal year ended September 2013). After-tax operating income minus net capital expenditures minus change in working capital gives FCFF. Reinvestment is net cap ex plus change in working capital. The reinvestment rate is reinvestment over after-tax operating income.
**Formulas:** FCFF = Operating Income × (1 − tax rate) − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash Working Capital. Reinvestment = Net Cap Ex + Change in WC. Reinvestment Rate = Reinvestment / After-tax Operating Income.
**Example:** Disney FY2013 inputs: operating income (lease-adjusted) $10,032M; effective tax rate 31.02%; cap ex (incl. acquisitions) $5,239M; D&A $2,192M; change in non-cash WC $103M. Steps: after-tax operating income = 10,032 × (1 − 0.3102) = $6,920M; net cap ex = 5,239 − 2,192 = $3,629M; subtract change in WC $103M → FCFF = $3,188M. Reinvestment = 3,629 + 103 = $3,732M; Reinvestment Rate = 3,732 / 6,920 = 53.93%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — operating income, tax rate, cap ex, depreciation, change in WC → FCFF, reinvestment, reinvestment rate.

## p.237 — II. Discount Rates

**Teaches:** The discount rate is a critical ingredient in DCF valuation: errors in estimating it, or mismatching cash flows and discount rates, can cause serious valuation errors. The discount rate should be consistent with both the riskiness and the type of cash flow being discounted. The cost of equity is the rate for discounting cash flows to equity (dividends or FCFE); the cost of capital is the rate for discounting free cash flows to the firm.
**Rules:** Equity cash flows (dividends, FCFE) → discount at cost of equity. Firm cash flows (FCFF) → discount at cost of capital. Never mix.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (consistency principle)
**Tags:** discount-rates, cost-of-equity, cost-of-capital, consistency-principle

## p.240 — Current Cost of Capital: Disney

**Teaches:** Disney's cost of capital computed from cost of equity, after-tax cost of debt, and market-value weights. Beta in November 2013 was 1.0013, T-bond rate 2.75%, equity risk premium 5.76% → cost of equity 8.52%. Disney's bond rating (A, May 2009 rating cited) implies a pretax cost of debt of 3.75%; with a 36.1% marginal tax rate the after-tax cost of debt is 2.40%. Weighting by market values of equity (121,878) and debt (15,961) gives a cost of capital of 7.81%.
**Formulas:** Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate + Beta × ERP = 2.75% + 1.0013 × 5.76% = 8.52%. After-Tax Cost of Debt = Pretax Cost of Debt × (1 − marginal tax rate) = 3.75% × (1 − 0.361) = 2.40%. Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × [E/(D+E)] + After-tax Cost of Debt × [D/(D+E)]. E = market value of equity; D = market value of debt. Plugging in: 8.52% × (121,878/137,839) + 2.40% × (15,961/137,839) = 7.81%.
**Example:** Disney Nov 2013: beta 1.0013, riskfree 2.75%, ERP 5.76% → ke 8.52%; A rating → pretax kd 3.75%, tax 36.1% → after-tax kd 2.40%; E = $121,878M, D = $15,961M → WACC = 7.81%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — beta, riskfree rate, ERP, pretax cost of debt, tax rate, market values of debt and equity → cost of equity, after-tax cost of debt, cost of capital.
