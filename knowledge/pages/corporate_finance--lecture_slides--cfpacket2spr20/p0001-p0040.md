## p.3 — First principles of corporate finance
**Teaches:** The goal of corporate finance is to maximize the value of the business (firm). Three decisions serve that goal. (1) The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it. Returns should reflect the magnitude and timing of cash flows plus all side effects. (2) The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt and the right mix of debt and equity to fund operations. The optimal mix maximizes firm value; the right kind of debt matches the tenor of the firm's assets. (3) The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments that earn the minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to owners. How much to return depends on current and potential investment opportunities. The form (dividends vs. buybacks) depends on what owners prefer. This packet covers the financing decision (highlighted boxes: optimal mix and right kind of debt).

## p.4 — The choices in financing: debt vs. equity
**Teaches:** A business can raise money in only two ways. Debt is a promise to make fixed payments in the future (interest and principal); if you fail to pay, you lose control of the business. Equity is the residual: whatever cash flows are left after debt payments. Debt features a fixed claim, tax deductibility, high priority in financial trouble, fixed maturity, and no management control. Equity features a residual claim, no tax deductibility, lowest priority in financial trouble, infinite life, and management control. Actual securities lie on a continuum: pure debt (bank debt, commercial paper, corporate bonds), hybrids (convertible debt, preferred stock, option-linked bonds), and equity (owner's equity, venture capital, common stock, warrants).
**Rules:** A security is debt-like if its payments are contractually fixed, tax-deductible, high-priority, and finite-maturity, with no control rights. It is equity-like if it is a residual, non-deductible, lowest-priority, infinite-life claim with control rights. Hybrids share features of both.

## p.8 — Financing across the corporate life cycle

## p.10 — Measuring a firm's financing mix: debt to capital ratio
**Teaches:** The simplest measure of a firm's current debt/equity usage is the proportion of debt in total financing: the debt-to-capital ratio. Debt includes all interest-bearing liabilities, short-term as well as long-term. It also includes other commitments that meet the criteria for debt: contractually pre-set payments that must be made no matter the firm's financial standing (e.g., leases). Equity can be defined in accounting terms (book value of equity) or market value terms (current price). The resulting debt ratios can be very different.
**Formulas:** Debt to Capital Ratio = Debt / (Debt + Equity), where Debt = all interest-bearing liabilities (short- and long-term) plus debt-like contractual commitments, and Equity = book or market value of equity.
**Rules:** Count as debt any commitment with contractually pre-set payments that must be made regardless of the firm's financial standing.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: debt value, equity value → output: debt-to-capital ratio); classifying what counts as debt is JUDGMENT.

## p.12 — The illusory benefits of debt
**Teaches:** The apparent benefit of debt — that the cost of debt is lower than the cost of equity — is an illusion. Debt is cheaper for a simple reason. The lender gets both first claim on the cash flows and a contractually pre-set cash flow. The equity investor is last in line and must demand a higher rate of return. Borrowing at a lower rate does not make a business more valuable. It just moves the risk around (onto the equity holders).
**Rules:** Never justify debt purely on "debt is cheaper than equity." The cheaper rate reflects risk transfer, not value creation.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** cost-of-debt, cost-of-equity, risk-shifting, common-fallacies

## p.13 — Costs and benefits of debt (overview)
**Teaches:** The real trade-off of debt. Benefits: (1) Tax benefits — the tax code favors debt; interest is tax-deductible in most of the world while cash flows to equity are not. Costs: (1) Bankruptcy costs — borrowing raises the expected probability and cost of bankruptcy. (2) Agency costs — what's good for stockholders is not always good for lenders, and that creates friction and costs. (3) Loss of future flexibility — using up debt capacity today means you cannot draw on it in the future.

## p.14 — Tax benefits of debt
**Teaches:** Interest expenses are deducted from income to arrive at taxable income, which reduces taxes. Payments to equity (such as dividends) are not deductible. The dollar tax benefit in any year is a function of the tax rate and the interest payment. Caveat: the firm needs income to cover the interest payments to actually get the benefit. Proposition 1: Other things being equal, the higher the marginal tax rate of a business, the more debt it will have in its capital structure.
**Formulas:** Tax benefit each year = Tax Rate × Interest Payment, where Tax Rate = the firm's marginal tax rate and Interest Payment = dollar interest expense for the year.
**Rules:** Proposition 1: higher marginal tax rate → more debt in the capital structure, other things equal. The tax benefit accrues only if taxable income covers the interest payments.

## p.19 — Bankruptcy cost
**Teaches:** The expected bankruptcy cost is a function of two variables. First, the probability of bankruptcy, which depends on how uncertain future cash flows are. Second, the cost of going bankrupt. That cost includes direct costs (legal and other deadweight costs) and indirect costs (costs that arise because people perceive you to be in financial trouble). Proposition 2: Firms with more volatile earnings and cash flows will have higher probabilities of bankruptcy, at any given level of debt and for any given level of earnings. Proposition 3: Other things being equal, the greater the indirect bankruptcy cost, the less debt the firm can afford to use.
**Formulas:** Expected bankruptcy cost = Probability of bankruptcy × Cost of going bankrupt (direct + indirect costs). (Stated conceptually as "a function of" the two variables.)
**Rules:** Proposition 2: more volatile earnings/cash flows → higher bankruptcy probability at any debt level. Proposition 3: greater indirect bankruptcy costs → less debt the firm can afford.

## p.21 — Agency cost
**Teaches:** An agency cost arises whenever you hire someone to do something for you. Your interests (as principal) may deviate from those of the person you hired (the agent). When you lend to a business, the stockholders use your money, and their interests differ from yours. The lender wants the money back; stockholders want to maximize their wealth. The clash can lead stockholders to invest in riskier projects than the lender would want. It can also lead them to pay themselves large dividends when the lender would rather they keep the cash in the business. Proposition 4: Other things being equal, the greater the agency problems associated with lending to a firm, the less debt the firm can afford to use.
**Rules:** Proposition 4: greater lender-stockholder agency problems → less debt capacity.

## p.25 — Debt: summarizing the trade off
**Teaches:** Full summary table of the debt trade-off with implications. Advantages: (1) Tax benefit — interest is tax-deductible while cash flows to equity generally are not. Implication: the higher the marginal tax rate, the greater the benefits of debt. (2) Added discipline — borrowing may force managers to weigh investment decisions more carefully and reduce bad investments. Implication: as the separation between managers and stockholders increases, the benefits of using debt go up. Disadvantages: (1) Expected bankruptcy cost — the product of the probability of going bankrupt and the cost of going bankrupt (direct plus indirect). The probability is higher in businesses with more volatile earnings; the cost also varies across businesses. Implications: firms with more stable earnings should borrow more, for any given level of earnings; firms with lower bankruptcy costs should borrow more. (2) Agency costs — actions that benefit equity investors may hurt lenders. The greater the potential conflict, the greater the cost borne by the borrower, as higher interest rates or more covenants. Implication: firms where lenders can monitor or control how their money is used should be able to borrow more than firms where this is difficult. (3) Loss of flexibility — using up debt capacity today means you cannot draw on it in the future. This can be disastrous if funds are needed and access to capital is shut off. Implications: firms that can forecast future funding needs better should borrow more; firms with better access to capital markets should be more willing to borrow today.
**Rules:** Borrow more if: higher marginal tax rate; wider manager-stockholder separation; more stable earnings; lower bankruptcy costs; lenders can monitor easily; funding needs are forecastable; capital-market access is good. Borrow less in the opposite cases.

## p.29 — The Miller-Modigliani theorem
**Teaches:** In an environment with no taxes, no default risk, and no agency costs, capital structure is irrelevant. If the Miller-Modigliani theorem holds, a firm's value is determined by the quality of its investments, not by its financing mix. The firm's cost of capital does not change with leverage. As a firm increases leverage, the cost of equity rises just enough to offset any gains from the cheaper debt. Figure 7.9 plots financing costs against the debt ratio in the MM world: the cost of equity rises with leverage, the cost of debt rises as default risk increases, and the cost of capital stays flat.
**Rules:** Under MM assumptions (no taxes, no default risk, no agency costs): financing mix does not affect firm value or cost of capital.

## p.36 — Pathways to the optimal debt ratio
**Teaches:** Five approaches to finding the optimal financing mix. (1) The Cost of Capital Approach: the optimal debt ratio minimizes the firm's cost of capital. (2) The Enhanced Cost of Capital Approach: the optimal debt ratio generates the best combination of (low) cost of capital and (high) operating income. (3) The Adjusted Present Value (APV) Approach: the optimal debt ratio maximizes the overall value of the firm. (4) The Sector Approach: the optimal debt ratio brings the firm closest to its peer group's financing mix. (5) The Life Cycle Approach: the optimal debt ratio best suits where the firm is in its life cycle.
**Rules:** Choose the optimum by: minimizing cost of capital (approach 1); jointly optimizing cost of capital and operating income (2); maximizing firm value via APV (3); matching peers (4); or matching life-cycle stage (5).
**Compute:** MIXED (approaches 1-3 are computational once inputs are estimated; 4-5 are judgment-based benchmarking)

## p.37 — I. The cost of capital approach
**Teaches:** Value of a firm = present value of cash flows to the firm, discounted back at the cost of capital. If the cash flows to the firm are held constant and the cost of capital is minimized, the value of the firm is maximized. The question then becomes: as the debt ratio changes, how does the cost of capital change?
**Formulas:** Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × (E/(D+E)) + Pre-tax Cost of Debt × (1 − t) × (D/(D+E)), where E = market value of equity, D = market value of debt, t = marginal tax rate. Value of Firm = PV of expected cash flows to the firm, discounted at the cost of capital.
**Rules:** Holding firm cash flows constant, the debt ratio that minimizes the cost of capital maximizes firm value.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (cost of equity, pre-tax cost of debt, tax rate, D, E → cost of capital); estimating those inputs at each debt ratio is JUDGMENT.

## p.38 — The debt trade off on the cost of capital (mechanics)
**Teaches:** How each WACC component moves as the debt ratio changes. Cost of equity side: cost of equity = risk-free rate + levered beta × equity risk premium. As you borrow more, the D/E ratio rises; the levered beta rises with it, raising the cost of equity. Cost of debt side: as you borrow more, interest expense rises and the interest coverage ratio falls. As the coverage ratio falls, the synthetic rating drops and the default spread rises, raising the pre-tax cost of debt. Tax side: when interest expenses rise above operating income, the tax benefit dissipates — the tax rate applied decreases if interest expense > operating income. The net trade-off: more debt replaces expensive equity with cheaper debt but raises the costs of both equity and debt. The net effect determines whether the cost of capital rises, falls, or stays unchanged as the debt ratio changes.
**Formulas:** Cost of capital = Cost of Equity × (E/(D+E)) + (Risk-free Rate + Default Spread) × (1 − Tax Rate) × (D/(D+E)). Cost of equity = Riskfree Rate + Levered Beta × (Equity Risk Premium). Pre-tax cost of debt = Risk-free Rate + Default Spread, where the default spread comes from the synthetic rating implied by the interest coverage ratio.
**Rules:** As the debt ratio rises: levered beta rises → cost of equity rises; interest coverage falls → synthetic rating drops → default spread and cost of debt rise. If interest expense > operating income, the marginal tax benefit dissipates (the tax rate applied falls).
**Compute:** MIXED — the WACC arithmetic is DETERMINISTIC (betas, spreads, weights, tax rate → cost of capital); mapping coverage ratios to synthetic ratings and spreads uses lookup tables, and the rating logic embeds JUDGMENT.

## p.39 — Concept question: costs of debt & equity
**Teaches:** In-class question. An Asian business magazine argued that equity is cheaper than debt, because dividend yields are much lower than interest rates on debt. Agree? (Yes/No). And can equity ever be cheaper than debt? (Yes/No). The dividend yield is not the cost of equity. Equity holders expect dividends plus price appreciation, and they bear residual risk. So equity's true cost exceeds debt's for the same firm. Answers: No to the article's claim; and No, equity cannot be cheaper than debt for the same firm, since it is the riskier claim.

## p.40 — Applying the cost of capital approach: the textbook example
**Teaches:** A stylized worked example of the cost of capital approach. A firm has $200 million in cash flows, expected to grow 3% a year forever. At each debt ratio, the cost of equity and after-tax cost of debt are estimated and combined into a cost of capital. Firm value is computed as a growing perpetuity. The cost of capital is minimized at a 40% debt ratio (10.14%), which is also where firm value peaks ($2,885 million). This illustrates the rule: minimizing the cost of capital maximizes value when cash flows are unaffected by the debt ratio.
**Formulas:** Value = Expected Cash Flow to Firm next year / (Cost of Capital − g) = 200 × (1.03) / (Cost of Capital − 0.03), where g = 3% perpetual growth rate and 200 = current annual cash flow in $ millions. Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × (1 − D/(D+E)) + After-tax Cost of Debt × (D/(D+E)).
**Data:**

| 0% | 10.50% | 4.80% | 10.50% | $2,747 |
| 10% | 11.00% | 5.10% | 10.41% | $2,780 |

| 30% | 12.30% | 5.52% | 10.27% | $2,835 |
