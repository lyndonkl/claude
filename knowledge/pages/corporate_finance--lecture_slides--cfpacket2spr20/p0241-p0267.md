## p.242 — Expected growth: fundamental growth framework (section divider III)

**Teaches:** Section divider "III. Expected Growth" with the core framework diagram: fundamental (sustainable) growth is reinvestment times the return earned on that reinvestment, and the pairing differs by earnings measure. Growth in net income = retention ratio x return on equity. Growth in operating income = reinvestment rate x return on capital.
**Formulas:** Retention Ratio = 1 - Dividends / Net Income. Return on Equity (ROE) = Net Income / Book Value of Equity. Expected growth in Net Income = Retention Ratio x ROE. Reinvestment Rate = (Net Cap Ex + Change in Working Capital) / (EBIT * (1 - t)), where Net Cap Ex = capital expenditures minus depreciation and t = tax rate. Return on Capital (ROC) = EBIT * (1 - t) / Book Value of Capital. Expected growth in Operating Income = Reinvestment Rate x ROC.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given dividends, net income, book equity (for equity growth) or net cap ex, change in WC, EBIT, tax rate, book capital (for operating growth) → expected fundamental growth rate.
**Tags:** expected-growth, fundamental-growth, retention-ratio, reinvestment-rate, roe, roc

## p.243 — Estimating growth in EPS: Deutsche Bank (January 2008)

**Teaches:** Applies the equity fundamental-growth equation (g = retention ratio x ROE) to Deutsche Bank, and shows why you may need to normalize: using a single (possibly peak) year's net income can badly overstate sustainable growth, so replacing 2007 net income with the 2003–07 average income gives a much lower, more defensible growth rate.
**Formulas:** ROE = Net Income (year t) / Book Value of Equity (start of year t, i.e., end of year t-1). Retention Ratio = 1 - Dividends / Net Income. Expected Growth Rate = Retention Ratio x ROE.

Normalized version using average net income 2003–07 of 3,954 million: Normalized ROE = 3,954/33,475 = 11.81%; normalized retention ratio = 1 - 2,146/3,954 = 45.72%; expected growth (normalized fundamentals) = 0.4572 x 0.1181 = 5.40%.

## p.245 — ROE and leverage

**Teaches:** A higher ROE, holding everything else fixed, produces a higher expected growth rate in equity earnings. But ROE mixes two things: the quality of the firm's investments (ROC) and the amount of debt used to fund them. Leverage raises ROE whenever the return on capital exceeds the after-tax interest rate on debt, and lowers it otherwise.
**Formulas:** ROE = ROC + (D/E) * (ROC - i*(1-t)), where ROC = EBIT*(1 - tax rate) / Book Value of Capital; BV of Capital = BV of Debt + BV of Equity - Cash; D/E = book debt-to-equity ratio; i = interest rate on debt; t = tax rate on ordinary income.
**Rules:** Leverage increases ROE if and only if ROC > i*(1-t); the boost per unit of D/E is the spread (ROC - i*(1-t)).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given ROC, D/E, i, t → ROE.
**Tags:** roe, leverage, roc, dupont, growth

## p.246 — Decomposing ROE: a quiz

**Teaches:** In-class exercise applying the ROE-leverage decomposition. With ROC = 15%, after-tax cost of debt = 5%, and book D/E = 100%, the implied ROE = 15% + 1.0*(15% - 5%) = 25%. It then asks two follow-up questions about an unlevered firm in the same sector with the same ROE and payout ratio. Would it have the same EPS growth? Yes, mechanically, since g = retention x ROE. Would it have the same equity value? No — the levered firm's growth is partly manufactured by leverage, so it is riskier and gets discounted at a higher cost of equity.
**Example:** ROC 15%, after-tax cost of debt 5%, book D/E 100% → ROE = 0.15 + 1.0*(0.15 - 0.05) = 25%.
**Compute:** MIXED — the ROE computation is DETERMINISTIC; the equal-growth/equal-value discussion is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** roe, leverage, growth-quality, quiz

## p.248 — When everything is in flux: changing growth and margins

**Teaches:** The reinvestment-times-ROC link to operating-income growth breaks down for companies in transition whose margins are changing over time (e.g., young high-growth firms). For such firms, estimate cash flows in three steps instead. (1) Forecast revenue growth and future revenues from market potential and competition. (2) Forecast a "target" operating margin and a pathway from current margins to that target. (3) Estimate reinvestment from revenues using a sales-to-capital ratio (the dollars of revenue generated per dollar of investment): reinvestment = change in revenues / (sales/capital ratio).
**Rules:** Use the revenue/target-margin/sales-to-capital approach instead of reinvestment-rate x ROC whenever margins are not stable.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT — revenue growth path, target margin and its pathway, and the sales-to-capital ratio are all estimates; only the downstream arithmetic is mechanical.
**Tags:** growth-firms, revenue-forecasting, target-margin, sales-to-capital, transition-companies

## p.250 — Getting closure in valuation: terminal value (section divider IV)

**Teaches:** Section IV. You cannot forecast cash flows forever, so you forecast a finite "growth period" and cap it with a terminal value that captures all value beyond it. When cash flows grow at a constant rate forever, their value is a growing perpetuity. The constant growth rate used is called the stable growth rate and cannot exceed the growth rate of the economy the firm operates in (a hard cap).
**Formulas:** Value = Sum from t=1 to N of CF(t)/(1+r)^t + Terminal Value/(1+r)^N, where CF(t) = cash flow in year t, r = discount rate (cost of equity for equity cash flows, cost of capital for firm cash flows), N = length of the growth period. Terminal (perpetuity) value: Value = Expected Cash Flow Next Period / (r - g), where g = expected growth rate forever.
**Rules:** Stable growth rate g must be <= growth rate of the economy (in practice, capped at the risk-free rate).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given cash flows, r, g, N → value; choosing g and N is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** terminal-value, dcf, perpetuity-growth, stable-growth

## p.251 — Getting to stable growth: growth-period patterns

**Teaches:** Every DCF must specify the length and pattern of the high-growth period. Three canonical assumptions: (1) no high growth — the firm is already in stable growth (single-stage model); (2) high growth for a period, then an abrupt drop to stable growth (2-stage model); (3) high growth for a period, then a gradual decline to stable growth (3-stage model). How long high growth lasts depends on firm size, current growth rate, and barriers to entry / differential advantages.
**Rules:** Larger firm → shorter high-growth period. Higher current growth rate → longer high-growth period. Stronger barriers to entry / more sustainable competitive advantages → longer high-growth period.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT — choosing the growth pattern and period length is an estimation decision.
**Tags:** growth-period, stable-growth, dcf-stages, competitive-advantage

## p.254 — Estimating stable-period inputs: Disney

**Teaches:** Four disciplines when setting terminal-value inputs after a high-growth period, illustrated with Disney: (1) Respect the cap — the perpetual growth rate (2.5%) is set below the riskfree rate (2.75%). (2) Stable-period excess returns — ROC drops from 12.61% to 10%, still above the 7.29% cost of capital because Disney's competitive advantages won't fully dissipate by year 10. (3) Reinvest to grow — the stable reinvestment rate is forced consistent with growth: g/ROC = 2.5%/10% = 25%. (4) Adjust risk — beta goes to 1.00 (mature firm), the debt ratio rises to 20%, cost of debt stays 3.75%, giving a stable cost of capital of 7.29%.
**Formulas:** Stable Reinvestment Rate = g / ROC = 2.5% / 10% = 25%. Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate + Beta * Risk Premium = 2.75% + 1.00*5.76% = 8.51%. Cost of capital = 8.51%*(0.80) + 3.75%*(1-0.361)*(0.20) = 7.29%.
**Rules:** Cap stable growth below the riskfree rate. Stable-period ROC may exceed cost of capital only if competitive advantages plausibly persist; otherwise set ROC = cost of capital. Always tie stable reinvestment to g/ROC. Move beta toward 1 and debt ratio toward a mature-firm level in stable growth.
**Compute:** MIXED — the three formulas are DETERMINISTIC given inputs; choosing g = 2.5%, stable ROC = 10%, beta = 1, debt ratio = 20% is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** terminal-value, stable-growth, disney, reinvestment-rate, cost-of-capital

## p.255 — From firm value to equity value per share (section divider V)

**Teaches:** Section V. How you get to equity value per share depends on which cash flow you discounted. Dividends per share discounted at the cost of equity give equity value per share directly. Aggregate FCFE discounted at the cost of equity gives aggregate equity value, from which you subtract the value of management equity options and divide by shares. Aggregate FCFF discounted at the cost of capital gives the value of operating assets, which must be bridged to per-share equity value.
**Formulas:** FCFF bridge: PV of FCFF = Value of operating assets; + Cash & near-cash investments; + Value of minority cross holdings; - Debt outstanding; = Value of equity; - Value of equity options; = Value of equity in common stock; / Number of shares = value per share.
**Data:**

| Approach used | To get to equity value per share |
|---|---|
| Discount dividends per share at the cost of equity | Present value is value of equity per share |
| Discount aggregate FCFE at the cost of equity | Present value is value of aggregate equity; subtract the value of equity options given to managers and divide by number of shares |
| Discount aggregate FCFF at the cost of capital | PV = value of operating assets; + cash & near-cash investments; + value of minority cross holdings; - debt outstanding; = value of equity; - value of equity options; = value of equity in common stock; / number of shares |

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — given PV of cash flows, cash, cross holdings, debt, option value and share count → equity value per share.
**Tags:** equity-value, fcff, fcfe, dividend-discount, valuation-bridge

## p.265 — Ways of changing value

**Teaches:** Framework diagram: a firm's value can be changed through exactly four levers, each tied to a management question. (1) Cash flows from existing assets (cash flows before debt payments but after taxes and after reinvestment needed to maintain existing assets) — manage existing investments better. (2) Expected growth during the high-growth period, which comes from growth from new investments (a function of the amount and quality of new investment) and efficiency growth (using existing assets better) — invest optimally for future growth. (3) Length of the high-growth period — since value-creating growth requires excess returns, this depends on the magnitude and sustainability of competitive advantages; after it, the firm is a stable-growth firm with no or very limited excess returns. (4) Cost of capital applied to discount cash flows — determined by the operating risk of the company, its default risk, and its mix of debt and equity — use the right amount and kind of debt.
**Rules:** Value-creating growth requires excess returns; growth without excess returns does not add value, so extending the high-growth period only helps if competitive advantages sustain excess returns.

## p.267 — First Principles (closing framework slide)

**Teaches:** Closing summary diagram of the entire course: the objective is to maximize the value of the business (firm), served by three decisions. The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate — the hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it, and returns should be measured by the magnitude and timing of cash flows including all side effects. The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt and the right mix of debt and equity — the optimal mix maximizes firm value, and the right kind of debt matches the tenor of the assets. The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments that make the minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to owners — how much depends on current and potential investment opportunities, and the form (dividends vs buybacks) depends on what owners prefer.
