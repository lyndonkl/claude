## p.161 — Life Cycle Analysis of Dividend Policy (Figure 10.7)

**Teaches:** A firm's capacity to pay dividends follows its life cycle. As a firm moves from start-up through rapid expansion, high growth, mature growth, and decline, revenues and earnings first rise then flatten and fall; external funding needs shrink while internal financing grows, and dividend-paying capacity rises from none to high. Dividend policy should therefore change with the growth stage rather than stay fixed.

**Data:**
| Growth stage | External funding needs | Internal financing | Capacity to pay dividends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Start-up | High, but constrained by infrastructure | Negative or low | None |
| Stage 2: Rapid Expansion | High, relative to firm value | Negative or low | None |
| Stage 3: High Growth | Moderates, relative to firm value | Low, relative to funding needs | Very low |
| Stage 4: Mature Growth | Low, as projects dry up | High, relative to funding needs | Increasing |
| Stage 5: Decline | Low, as projects dry up | More than funding needs | High |

**Rules:** Pay little or nothing in early life-cycle stages; increase dividends only as the firm matures and internal financing exceeds funding needs.

## p.164 — Three Schools of Thought on Dividends

**Teaches:** Three positions on whether dividends affect firm value, each following from its assumptions. (1) If dividends carry no tax disadvantage and firms can issue stock at no cost whenever needed, dividends do not matter and dividend policy does not affect value. (2) If dividends create a tax disadvantage for investors relative to capital gains, dividends are bad and increasing them reduces value. (3) If dividends create a tax advantage and/or stockholders like dividends, dividends are good and increasing them increases value.

**Rules:** Which school applies depends on the relative taxation of dividends vs. capital gains and on issuance costs/investor preferences.

## p.165 — The Balanced Viewpoint

**Teaches:** The pragmatic middle position: if a company has excess cash and few good investment opportunities (NPV > 0 projects), returning money to stockholders via dividends or buybacks is good. If it lacks excess cash and/or has several good NPV > 0 opportunities, returning money to stockholders is bad.

**Rules:** Return cash if excess cash AND few NPV>0 projects; retain cash if no excess cash OR many NPV>0 projects.

## p.166 — The Miller-Modigliani Dividend Irrelevance Hypothesis

**Teaches:** The Miller-Modigliani Hypothesis: dividends do not affect value. Basis: if investment policy (and hence cash flows) does not change, firm value cannot change as dividends change. A firm that pays more dividends must issue new equity to fund the same projects. That lowers expected price appreciation, but a higher dividend yield exactly offsets it. Ignoring personal taxes, investors are indifferent between dividends and capital gains. Underlying assumptions:
- (a) No tax differences between dividends and capital gains.
- (b) Firms paying too much cash can issue new stock with no flotation costs or signaling consequences.
- (c) Firms paying too little do not waste the excess cash on bad projects or acquisitions.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual proposition).

**Tags:** miller-modigliani, dividend-irrelevance, dividend-policy, assumptions

## p.169 — Cashflows from Selling around Ex-Dividend Day

**Teaches:** Derives the link between the ex-dividend price drop and the dividend from investor indifference. The after-tax cash from selling before ex-dividend day must equal the after-tax cash from selling after (and collecting the dividend). The result: the ratio of price drop to dividend equals the ratio of after-tax keep rates on ordinary income vs. capital gains.

**Formulas:**
- Cash flow from selling before ex-dividend day: P_b - (P_b - P) * t_cg
- Cash flow from selling after ex-dividend day: P_a - (P_a - P) * t_cg + D * (1 - t_o)
- Indifference condition: P_b - (P_b - P) * t_cg = P_a - (P_a - P) * t_cg + D * (1 - t_o)
- Result: (P_b - P_a) / D = (1 - t_o) / (1 - t_cg)
where P = original purchase price, P_b = cum-dividend price, P_a = ex-dividend price, D = dividend per share, t_o = ordinary income tax rate, t_cg = capital gains tax rate.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given t_o and t_cg → expected price-drop/dividend ratio; or given observed ratio → implied tax differential).

**Tags:** ex-dividend-day, dividend-taxes, arbitrage, formula-derivation

## p.173 — Dividend Capture Strategy Example

**Teaches:** Worked example of a tax-exempt dividend-capture arbitrage exploiting a price drop smaller than the dividend.

**Example:** XYZ trades at $50 at close of May 3; goes ex-dividend May 4 with a $1 dividend; historical price drop is only 90% of the dividend. A tax-exempt US pension fund: (1) buys 1 million shares cum-dividend at $50/share; (2) sells ex-dividend at $49.10/share (50 - 1 x 0.90); (3) collects the $1/share dividend. Net profit = -$50 million + $49.10 million + $1 million = $0.10 million.

**Formulas:** Profit per share = D - drop = D - 0.90 * D = 0.10 * D, where D = dividend per share (for a tax-exempt investor, ignoring transaction costs and risk).

## p.189 — The Big Picture of Corporate Finance

**Teaches:** The framing map of corporate finance. The objective is to maximize the value of the business (firm). Three decisions serve it. The Investment Decision: invest in assets that earn more than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate reflects the riskiness of the investment and the debt/equity mix funding it; the return reflects the magnitude and timing of cash flows plus all side effects. The Financing Decision: find the right kind of debt and the right mix of debt and equity. The optimal mix maximizes firm value, and the right kind of debt matches the tenor of the assets. The Dividend Decision: if you cannot find investments that earn the minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to owners. How much you can return depends on current and potential investment opportunities. Whether you use dividends or buybacks depends on what owners prefer. This section highlights the dividend decision.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework).

**Tags:** big-picture, corporate-finance-framework, dividend-decision

## p.193 — FCFE: How Much a Company Could Have Paid Out

**Teaches:** Free Cashflow to Equity (FCFE) measures the cash left in the business after two claims are met. First, non-equity claimholders (debt and preferred stock) are paid. Second, any reinvestment needed to sustain assets and future growth is made. FCFE is the amount a firm could have afforded to pay out to stockholders. The page gives three equal forms: standard, modified (grouping reinvestment and net debt cash flow), and simplified for a constant debt ratio.

**Formulas:**
- Standard: FCFE = Net Income + Depreciation - Cap Ex - Change in Working Capital + New Debt Issued - Debt Repaid
- Modified: FCFE = Net Income - (Cap Ex - Depreciation + Change in Working Capital) + (New Debt Issued - Debt Repaid); the middle term is Reinvestment and the last is Net CF from Debt
- Simplified (if debt ratio is constant): FCFE = Net Income - (Cap Ex - Depreciation + Change in Working Capital) * (1 - Debt Ratio)
Symbols: Net Income = earnings to equity; Cap Ex = capital expenditures; Change in Working Capital = change in non-cash working capital; Debt Ratio = debt as a fraction of capital.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (financial statement inputs → FCFE).

**Tags:** fcfe, free-cash-flow, payout-capacity, formulas

## p.194 — Estimating FCFE when Leverage Is Stable

**Teaches:** Net cash flow from debt (issues minus repayments) is volatile and can distort FCFE in any one period. For a more balanced measure, estimate FCFE assuming a stable debt ratio funded reinvestment over the period.

**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income - (1 - Debt Ratio) * (Capital Expenditures - Depreciation) - (1 - Debt Ratio) * Working Capital Needs, where Debt Ratio = Debt/Capital ratio (either the actual or a target).

**Rules:** Use the stable-debt-ratio FCFE when actual debt cash flows are lumpy across the period examined.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, cap ex, depreciation, working capital change, debt ratio → FCFE).

**Tags:** fcfe, stable-leverage, debt-ratio, formulas

## p.196 — How Companies Get Big Cash Balances: Microsoft 1996

**Teaches:** When a firm generates positive FCFE and pays out nothing, the unreturned cash accumulates on the balance sheet as cash and marketable securities — this is how companies build big cash balances.

**Example:** Microsoft 1996 inputs: Net Income = $2,176M; Capital Expenditures = $494M; Depreciation = $480M; Change in Non-Cash Working Capital = $35M; Debt = None. FCFE = Net Income - (Cap Ex - Depr) - Change in non-cash WC - Debt CF = 2,176 - (494 - 480) - 35 - 0 = $2,127M. Microsoft could have paid $2,127M in dividends/buybacks in 1996 but paid nothing, so the $2,127M shows up as cash on its balance sheet.

**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income - (Cap Ex - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash Working Capital - Debt cash flow.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (the listed inputs → FCFE).

## p.197 — FCFE for a Bank

**Teaches:** For a bank, conventional cap ex and working capital are meaningless; reinvestment is redefined as investment in regulatory capital (book equity). A bank's FCFE is net income minus the increase in regulatory capital it must retain to support asset growth at its capital ratio. Deutsche Bank's projected FCFE is negative for the first three forecast years because regulatory capital needs exceed net income, turning positive only in years 4-5 as ROE recovers to 8%.

**Formulas:**
- FCFE_Bank = Net Income - Increase in Regulatory Capital (Book Equity)
- Illustration: bank with $10 billion loans, $750M book equity, 7.5% capital ratio, growing loans 10% (to $11 billion), expecting $150M net income: FCFE = 150 - (11,000 - 10,000) * 0.075 = $75 million.

## p.199 — Cash Buildup and Investor Blowback: Chrysler 1994

**Teaches:** Chrysler 1985-1994 illustrates the risk of cash buildup. In most years FCFE far exceeded the cash returned to stockholders. FCFE was around $1.4-1.6B in 1985-86 and about $2.6B in 1992, versus payouts of only a few hundred million. Cumulated cash therefore grew from roughly $1,000M to over $8,000M by 1994. A large cash pile with low payout invites investor blowback — in Chrysler's case, Kirk Kerkorian's pressure campaign.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC accounting (yearly FCFE minus cash returned → cumulated cash); the takeaway about investor pressure is JUDGMENT.

## p.200 — Application Test: Estimating Your Firm's FCFE

**Teaches:** Template for estimating a firm's FCFE from either general inputs or the cash flow statement, then comparing it with cash actually returned to stockholders. Note the sign conventions: from the cash flow statement, cap ex, working capital changes, preferred dividends, and borrowing changes are added as they appear (already signed) rather than subtracted.

**Formulas:**
- In general: FCFE = Net Income + Depreciation & Amortization - Capital Expenditures - Change in Non-Cash Working Capital - Preferred Dividend - Principal Repaid + New Debt Issued
- From the cash flow statement: FCFE = Net Income + Depreciation & Amortization + Capital Expenditures (as reported, negative) + Changes in Non-cash WC (as reported) + Preferred Dividend (as reported) + Increase in LT Borrowing + Decrease in LT Borrowing + Change in ST Borrowing
- Compare FCFE to: Dividends (Common) + Stock Buybacks.

**Rules:** If actual payout (common dividends + buybacks) is well below FCFE, the firm is accumulating cash; if above, it is overpaying.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cash flow statement line items → FCFE and payout comparison).

**Tags:** fcfe, application-test, cash-flow-statement, payout
