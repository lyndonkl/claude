## p.121 — Approaches for evaluating asset cash flows

**Teaches:** To design the right debt for a firm, first characterize the firm's asset cash flows. There are three approaches. (I) Intuitive approach: ask a set of questions. Are projects typically long term or short term? What is the cash flow pattern on projects? How much growth potential does the firm have relative to current projects? How cyclical are the cash flows, and what specific factors drive them? (II) Project cash flow approach: estimate expected cash flows on a typical project for the firm. Then run scenario analyses on those cash flows under different macroeconomic scenarios. (III) Historical data approach: use the firm's historical operating cash flows and firm value to infer cash-flow traits.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing and applying an approach requires reasoning about the firm's business).
**Tags:** debt-design, asset-cash-flows, financing-choices, corporate-finance

## p.124 — Project-specific financing

**Teaches:** With project-specific financing you match the financing choices to the individual project being funded, so the debt is truly customized to the project. This makes the most sense when a firm has a few large, independent projects to finance; it becomes impractical and costly when firms have portfolios of projects with interdependent cash flows.
**Rules:** Use project-specific financing when projects are few, large, and independent; avoid it for portfolios of interdependent projects.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

## p.125 — Duration of a Disney theme park project (worked example)

**Teaches:** Shows how to compute the duration of a project (a Brazilian Disney theme park) as the PV-weighted average time of its cash flows. Steps: discount each cash flow at the cost of capital; multiply each present value by its year t; sum the products; divide by the total present value.
**Formulas:** Duration of project = Sum over t of (t × PV(CF_t)) / Sum over t of PV(CF_t). Here PV(CF_t) = CF_t / (1+r)^t. Symbols: CF_t = cash flow in year t, including terminal value in the final year; r = discount rate (8.46% here).

## p.132 — Why the regression coefficient is duration

**Teaches:** The duration of a straight bond or loan can be written in terms of its coupons and face value. Duration measures how much the bond's price changes for a unit change in interest rates. The firm-value-vs-interest-rate regression slope measures exactly the same thing for the firm's assets. Holding other factors constant, bond duration increases with maturity and decreases with the coupon rate.
**Formulas:** Duration of Bond = (dP/P)/(dr/r) = Numerator / Denominator. Numerator = Sum from t=1 to N of (t × Coupon_t)/(1+r)^t + (N × Face Value)/(1+r)^N. Denominator = Sum from t=1 to N of Coupon_t/(1+r)^t + Face Value/(1+r)^N. Symbols: P = bond price; r = interest rate (yield); Coupon_t = coupon (interest payment) in period t; N = maturity in periods; Face Value = principal repaid at maturity; dP/P = proportional price change; dr/r = proportional rate change.
**Rules:** Duration rises with maturity; duration falls as the coupon rate rises.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: coupons, face value, maturity, yield → output: duration).
**Tags:** duration, bond-pricing, interest-rate-sensitivity, formula

## p.133 — Duration: comparing approaches

**Teaches:** Two routes lead to the same quantity, dP/dr — the percentage change in value for a percentage change in interest rates. Traditional duration measures use projected cash flows. They assume (1) cash flows are unaffected by changes in interest rates and (2) interest rate changes are small. The regression approach (dP = a + b·dr) uses historical data on changes in firm (market) value and interest rates. It assumes (1) past project cash flows resemble future project cash flows, (2) the link between cash flows and interest rates is stable, and (3) changes in market value reflect changes in the value of the firm.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing between approaches based on which assumptions hold).
**Tags:** duration, methodology-comparison, regression, assumptions

## p.149 — First principles of corporate finance (framework recap)

**Teaches:** The recurring framework: maximize the value of the business (firm) via three decisions.
- Investment decision: invest in assets that earn a return greater than the minimum acceptable hurdle rate. The hurdle rate should reflect the riskiness of the investment and the mix of debt and equity used to fund it. The return should reflect the magnitude and timing of the cash flows, plus all side effects.
- Financing decision: find the right kind of debt for your firm and the right mix of debt and equity to fund operations. The optimal mix maximizes firm value. The right kind of debt matches the tenor of your assets.
- Dividend decision: if you cannot find investments that make your minimum acceptable rate, return the cash to the owners. How much cash you can return depends on current and potential investment opportunities. How you return it (dividends vs. buybacks) depends on what stockholders prefer.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** first-principles, dividend-decision, corporate-finance-framework

## p.150 — Steps to the dividend decision (equity as a residual claim)

**Teaches:** A flow diagram of how cash should logically be returned when equity is treated as a residual claim. Start with cash flow from operations (= after-tax operating income + depreciation). First, pay the cash flows to debt: principal repaid and interest expenses ("how much did you borrow?"). The remainder is cash flow from operations to equity investors. From that, subtract reinvestment back into the business: long-term assets (cap ex) and short-term assets (working capital) ("how good are your investment choices?"). What remains is cash available for return to stockholders — Potential Dividends (FCFE). The firm then decides how much cash to hold back ("what is a reasonable cash balance?"). The rest is cash paid out, split between stock buybacks and dividends by stockholder preference ("what do your stockholders prefer?").
**Formulas:** Potential Dividends (FCFE) = Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash Working Capital − (Debt Repaid − Debt Raised). Cash flow from operations = After-tax Operating Income + Depreciation.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC for FCFE (inputs: net income, cap ex, depreciation, change in non-cash WC, debt repaid/raised → output: FCFE); JUDGMENT for cash-holdback and dividends-vs-buybacks choices.
**Tags:** fcfe, dividend-decision, residual-claim, potential-dividends

## p.151 — The roots of dividend dysfunction

**Teaches:** In practice, dividend policy is dysfunctional. It does not follow the logical process of starting with your investment options and working down to residual cash. Two dominant factors drive dividend policy around the world. (1) Inertia: companies hate to let go of their past dividend behavior. (2) Me-too-ism: companies want to behave like their peer group.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dividend-policy, behavioral-finance, inertia, peer-effects

## p.158 — Measures of dividend policy

**Teaches:** Two standard measures of dividend policy. Dividend payout measures the percentage of earnings the company pays in dividends; if net income is negative, the payout ratio cannot be computed. Dividend yield measures the return an investor can make from dividends alone and becomes part of the expected return on the investment.
**Formulas:** Dividend Payout = Dividends / Net Income. Dividend Yield = Dividends per share / Stock price.
**Rules:** If net income is negative, the payout ratio cannot be computed.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: dividends, net income, dividends per share, stock price → outputs: payout ratio, dividend yield).
