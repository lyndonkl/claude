## p.82 — Financing Strategies for a Financial Institution

**Teaches:** Three equity-capital strategies for financial service firms. (1) Regulatory minimum strategy: hold the bare minimum equity capital required by regulatory ratios; aggressive versions exploit regulatory loopholes to invest in businesses where required capital ratios are set too low relative to their true risk. (2) Self-regulatory strategy: raise equity not to meet regulatory ratios but to ensure that potential business losses can be covered by existing equity — the firm assesses needed equity from the riskiness of its businesses and loss potential. (3) Combination strategy: regulatory capital ratios operate as a floor for established businesses, with safety buffers added where needed.

**Rules:** Regulatory minimum = hold minimum required capital; Self-regulatory = size equity to cover potential losses from business risk; Combination = regulatory ratio as floor + buffers.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT — choosing a strategy and sizing buffers requires assessing business risk.

**Tags:** banks, capital-structure, regulatory-capital, financing-strategy

## p.85 — Determinants of Optimal Debt Ratio: 3. Operating Risk

**Teaches:** Firms with more operating risk/uncertainty (more variable operating income) will have lower optimal debt ratios than firms with predictable operations. Operating risk enters the cost of capital approach in two places. First, unlevered beta: riskier firms have higher unlevered betas, and borrowing magnifies that risk, so cost of equity climbs more steeply with leverage. Second, bond ratings: for any given level of operating income, riskier firms get lower ratings. Ratings are based on normalized income.

**Rules:** Higher operating risk → higher unlevered beta and lower rating at each debt level → lower optimal debt ratio.

## p.89 — III. The APV Approach to Optimal Capital Structure

**Teaches:** In the adjusted present value (APV) approach, firm value is written as the value of the unlevered firm plus the net effect of debt on value (tax benefits minus expected bankruptcy costs). The optimal dollar debt level is the one that maximizes total firm value under this decomposition.

**Formulas:** Firm Value = Unlevered Firm Value + (Tax Benefits of Debt - Expected Bankruptcy Cost from the Debt).

**Rules:** Optimal dollar debt = the debt level that maximizes Firm Value in the APV equation.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given the three components at each debt level; estimating the components is judgment.

**Tags:** apv, optimal-capital-structure, tax-benefits, bankruptcy-cost

## p.90 — Implementing the APV Approach

**Teaches:** Three-step APV implementation. Step 1: estimate unlevered firm value, in one of two ways. Either value the firm with the cost of equity based on the unlevered beta (which equals cost of capital for an unlevered firm). Or back it out: Unlevered Firm Value = Current Market Value of Firm - Tax Benefits of Debt (Current) + Expected Bankruptcy Cost from Debt. Step 2: estimate tax benefits at each debt level. With the simplest assumption that savings are perpetual, Tax benefits = Dollar Debt * Tax Rate. Step 3: estimate the probability of bankruptcy at each debt level. Multiply it by the cost of bankruptcy (direct plus indirect) to get expected bankruptcy cost.

**Formulas:** Unlevered Firm Value = Current Market Value of Firm - Current Tax Benefits of Debt + Expected Bankruptcy Cost from Debt; Tax Benefits (perpetual) = Dollar Debt * Tax Rate; Expected Bankruptcy Cost = Probability of Bankruptcy * Cost of Bankruptcy (direct + indirect).

**Compute:** MIXED — the arithmetic is deterministic given inputs; probability of bankruptcy and indirect bankruptcy cost require estimation.

**Tags:** apv, unlevered-firm-value, tax-benefits, bankruptcy-cost

## p.91 — Estimating Expected Bankruptcy Cost

**Teaches:** To estimate expected bankruptcy cost: for probability of bankruptcy, estimate the synthetic rating at each debt level, then use empirical studies (Altman, updated annually) to map that rating to the probability of default over time. For the cost of bankruptcy, direct costs are the easier component: generally 5-10% of firm value, based on empirical studies. Indirect costs are much harder to estimate. They should be higher in sectors where operating income is significantly hurt by default risk (e.g., airlines) and lower where it is not (e.g., groceries).

**Rules:** Direct bankruptcy cost ≈ 5-10% of firm value. Indirect bankruptcy cost: higher for sectors sensitive to default perception (airlines), lower for insensitive sectors (groceries). Probability of bankruptcy: synthetic rating → empirical default probability (Altman).

**Compute:** MIXED — rating-to-probability lookup is deterministic; indirect cost sizing is judgment.

**Tags:** bankruptcy-cost, default-probability, synthetic-rating, apv

## p.95 — IV. Relative Analysis

**Teaches:** The relative (comparables) approach to capital structure: the "safest" place for a firm to be is close to its industry-average debt ratio, with subjective adjustments to that average to get the right ratio for the specific firm.

**Rules:** Adjustments to the industry average. Higher tax rates → higher debt ratios (tax benefits). Lower insider ownership → higher debt ratios (greater discipline from debt). More stable income → higher debt ratios (lower bankruptcy costs). More intangible assets → lower debt ratios (more agency problems).

## p.97 — Getting Past Simple Averages (Regression Approach)

**Teaches:** Instead of comparing to a simple industry average, run a cross-sectional regression. Step 1: regress debt ratios on variables believed to determine debt ratios in the sector (e.g., tax rate, earnings variability, EBITDA/firm value); check statistical significance (t-statistics) and predictive ability (R-squared). Step 2: plug the firm's own values of those proxies into the regression to get a predicted debt ratio. Step 3: compare actual to predicted debt ratio to judge over/under-leverage.

**Formulas:** Debt Ratio = a + b*(Tax rate) + c*(Earnings Variability) + d*(EBITDA/Firm Value), where a,b,c,d are estimated regression coefficients.

**Rules:** Actual > predicted → over-levered relative to sector fundamentals; actual < predicted → under-levered.

**Compute:** MIXED — the regression fit and prediction are deterministic given data; variable selection and interpretation are judgment.

**Tags:** regression, predicted-debt-ratio, cross-sectional, capital-structure

## p.105 — A Framework for Getting to the Optimal (decision tree)

**Teaches:** A decision tree for moving actual leverage toward optimal. If Actual > Optimal (over-levered): ask whether the firm is under bankruptcy threat. If yes, reduce debt quickly via (1) equity-for-debt swaps, (2) selling assets and using cash to pay off debt, (3) renegotiating with lenders. If no, ask whether the firm has good projects (ROE > cost of equity, ROC > cost of capital). If it does, take the good projects with new equity or retained earnings. If it does not: (1) pay off debt with retained earnings, (2) reduce or eliminate dividends, (3) issue new equity and pay off debt. If Actual < Optimal (under-levered): ask whether the firm is a takeover target. If yes, increase leverage quickly via (1) debt/equity swaps, (2) borrowing money and buying back shares. If no, ask whether it has good projects: if yes, take good projects with debt; if no, ask whether stockholders like dividends — yes: pay dividends; no: buy back stock.

**Rules:** Over-levered + bankruptcy threat → fast debt reduction; over-levered, no threat + good projects → fund projects with equity/retained earnings; over-levered, no good projects → retain earnings to pay debt, cut dividends, issue equity to retire debt. Under-levered + takeover target → fast leverage increase (debt/equity swap, borrow and buy back); under-levered, no threat + good projects → fund projects with debt; no good projects → pay dividends if stockholders like them, else buy back stock. Good projects test: ROE > Cost of Equity and ROC > Cost of Capital.

## p.113 — Design the Perfect Financing Instrument

**Teaches:** The perfect financing instrument has all the tax advantages of debt while preserving the flexibility of equity. Design step 1: profile the cash flows on the assets or projects. The relevant traits are duration, currency, sensitivity to inflation and uncertainty about the future, growth patterns, and cyclicality. Each trait maps to a debt characteristic. Duration maps to debt maturity; currency of cash flows maps to currency mix. Use more floating rate when cash flows move with inflation or when uncertainty about the future is high. Use convertible rather than straight debt when cash flows are low now but expected growth is high. Add special features (options) to make debt cash flows match asset cash flows — examples: commodity bonds, catastrophe notes. Bottom line: design debt so its cash flows match the cash flows on the assets financed.

**Rules:** Duration of assets → maturity of debt; foreign-currency cash flows → currency mix of debt; CF moves with inflation or high uncertainty → floating rate; low current CF + high expected growth → convertible; commodity/catastrophe exposure → commodity bonds / catastrophe notes.

## p.118 — Soothe Bondholder Fears

**Teaches:** Design step 4 — factor in agency conflicts between stockholders and bondholders. Some firms face bondholder skepticism when raising debt. ## p.119 — Do Not Lock In Market Mistakes That Work Against You

**Teaches:** Ratings agencies can under-rate a firm and markets can under-price its stock or bonds; firms should not lock in these mistakes by issuing long-term securities. Issuing equity (or equity-based products including convertibles) when equity is under-priced transfers wealth from existing stockholders to new stockholders; issuing long-term debt when the firm is under-rated locks in rates far too high for its actual default risk. The posed solution: if you must use equity or debt while mispriced/under-rated, use short-term or delayed structures until the mistake corrects.

**Rules:** Equity under-priced → avoid issuing equity/convertibles long-term; firm under-rated → avoid locking in long-term debt at inflated rates.

## p.120 — Designing Debt: Bringing It All Together (full pipeline)

**Teaches:** The complete debt-design pipeline in one diagram, in sequence.
1. Start with the cash flows on assets/projects: duration, currency, effect of inflation and uncertainty about the future, growth patterns, cyclicality and other effects.
2. Define debt characteristics. Match duration/maturity and currency mix to the assets. Choose fixed vs. floating rate — more floating if cash flows move with inflation or uncertainty is high. Choose straight vs. convertible — convertible if cash flows are low now but expected growth is high. Add special features so debt cash flows match asset cash flows (commodity bonds, catastrophe notes). Design debt to have cash flows matching the assets financed.
3. Overlay tax preferences: deductibility of cash flows and differences in tax rates across locales. If tax advantages are large enough, they may override the previous step (zero coupons).
4. Consider ratings agency and analyst concerns. Analysts care about the EPS effect and value vs comparables. Ratings agencies care about ratio effects and ratios vs comparables. Regulators care about the measures they use. Ask whether securities can be designed to make all of them happy (operating leases, MIPs, surplus notes).
5. Factor in agency conflicts between stockholders and bondholders: cash-flow observability, asset type (tangible/liquid assets create fewer agency problems), and existing covenants. If agency problems are substantial, issue convertible bonds (convertibles, puttable bonds, rating-sensitive notes, LYONs).
6. Consider information asymmetries. With more uncertainty about future cash flows, short-term debt may be better. Firms with credibility problems will issue more short-term debt.

**Rules:** Six-stage sequence: asset cash flows → debt characteristics → tax overlay → ratings/analyst/regulatory concerns → stockholder-bondholder agency conflicts → information asymmetries (uncertainty or credibility problems → shorter-term debt).
