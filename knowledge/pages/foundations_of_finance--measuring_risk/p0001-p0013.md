## p.2 — We are risk averse... So what?
**Teaches:** If people were risk neutral, they would accept the risk-free rate as the expected return on every investment. They would treat expected cash flows as equal to guaranteed cash flows. People are risk averse, so they demand a risk premium for holding risky assets. Put differently, they pay less for an uncertain expected cash flow than for a guaranteed cash flow of the same amount. The essence of risk measurement is twofold: find a measure of risk that reflects what investors are averse to, then convert that measure into a risk premium.

## p.3 — The Mean-Variance Framework
**Teaches:** In the mean-variance framework, the variance of an investment measures the gap between actual and expected returns. A risk-free investment has actual returns that always equal the expected return, so its variance is zero. The greater the variance, the riskier the investment is viewed as being. The framework assumes investors pick investments on only two dimensions: expected return (the positive) and risk measured as variance (the negative). This is a strong assumption. It can hold only if returns are normally distributed, or if investors' utility functions (which determine how they view risk) make them care only about mean and variance.
**Rules:** Mean-variance investing is justified only if returns are normally distributed OR utility functions depend only on mean and variance.

## p.4 — A Breakdown of Risk: firm-specific to market (risk types)
**Teaches:** Risk lies on a scale from firm-specific (hits only one firm) to market-wide (hits all assets). Firm-specific risk: projects may do better or worse than expected. Hitting a few firms: rivals may be stronger or weaker than expected. Sector-level: an action may hit a whole sector. Hitting many firms: exchange rate and political risk. Market-wide: interest rates, inflation, and news about the economy. Firms can cut these risks, in order, by holding many projects, buying rivals, spreading across sectors, and spreading across countries. Firms cannot change market risk. Investors can offset firm-level through sector risk by holding many domestic stocks. They can offset country and political risk by going global, and market risk by holding many asset classes.
**Data:**
| Risk source | Scope | Firm can reduce by | Investors can mitigate by |
|---|---|---|---|
| Projects do better/worse than expected | Firm-specific (one firm) | Investing in lots of projects | Diversifying across domestic stocks |
| Competition stronger/weaker than anticipated | Affects few firms | Acquiring competitors | Diversifying across domestic stocks |
| Entire sector affected by action | Affects sector | Diversifying across sectors | Diversifying across domestic stocks |
| Exchange rate and political risk | Affects many firms | Diversifying across countries | Diversifying globally |
| Interest rate, inflation, news about economy | Market (all investments) | Cannot affect | Diversifying across asset classes |

## p.5 — The Effects of Diversification
**Teaches:** You can reduce, if not remove, firm-specific risk by holding more assets in a portfolio — by being diversified. Market-wide risk cannot be cut this way. The economic case has two parts. First, each holding becomes a much smaller share of the portfolio. That mutes the effect, good or bad, of any one holding on the whole. Second, firm-level events can be good or bad. In a large portfolio these effects average out to zero: for every firm where something bad happens, some other firm has something good happen.
**Rules:** Diversification reduces firm-specific risk only; it does not reduce market-wide risk.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (economic argument).
**Tags:** diversification, firm-specific-risk, portfolio-theory

## p.6 — Statistical proof that diversification works: two-stock example
**Teaches:** A two-stock example shows in numbers that diversifying lowers risk. With a 50/50 mix of two stocks that are not perfectly correlated, the portfolio standard deviation (9.81%) is lower than that of either stock alone (10% and 15%).
**Formulas:** Variance of a two-asset portfolio = w1^2 * sigma1^2 + w2^2 * sigma2^2 + 2 * w1 * w2 * rho12 * sigma1 * sigma2. Here w1 and w2 are the portfolio weights in stock 1 and stock 2. sigma1 and sigma2 are the standard deviations of the two stocks' returns. rho12 is the correlation between the two stocks' returns. Portfolio standard deviation = sqrt(portfolio variance).
**Example:** Stock 1: average monthly return 1.50%, standard deviation of monthly returns sigma1 = 10%. Stock 2: average monthly return 2.50%, sigma2 = 15%. Correlation rho12 = 0.20. With w1 = w2 = 0.5: Variance = (0.5)^2(0.10)^2 + (0.5)^2(0.15)^2 + 2(0.5)(0.5)(0.10)(0.15)(0.20) = 0.009625. Standard deviation = sqrt(0.009625) = 0.0981 = 9.81%, lower than either stock's standard deviation.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: weights, standard deviations, correlation → outputs: portfolio variance and standard deviation).

## p.7 — The Magic of Correlation
**Teaches:** The less correlated assets are with each other, the more you gain from diversifying. The mechanical challenge: as you go from two to three to four to n assets, the number of correlations to compute grows very fast. The marginal benefit: the gain from adding one more asset shrinks as the portfolio grows larger.
**Rules:** Lower correlation → greater diversification benefit; marginal diversification benefit declines with portfolio size.

## p.8 — The Role of the Marginal Investor
**Teaches:** The marginal investor in a firm is the investor most likely to buy or sell on the next trade and so to influence the stock price. Generally the marginal investor must own a lot of stock and also trade a lot. Because trading is required, the largest investor may not be the marginal investor. That is especially true if he or she is a founder or manager of the firm. All risk and return models in finance assume the marginal investor is well diversified.
**Rules:** To identify the marginal investor: must both hold a large stake and trade actively; a large but non-trading holder (e.g., founder/manager) is not the marginal investor.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (identifying the marginal investor requires reasoning about ownership and trading).
**Tags:** marginal-investor, diversification, risk-return-models

## p.9 — The Market Portfolio
**Teaches:** Assume diversification costs nothing (no transactions costs) and all assets can be traded. Then the limit of diversification is to hold every single asset in the economy, in proportion to market value. This portfolio is called the market portfolio. Individual investors do not adjust for risk by changing the portfolio's makeup. They adjust their allocation between this market portfolio and a riskless asset, such as a T-Bill. Every investor holds some combination of the risk-free asset and the market portfolio.
**Data:**
| Preferred risk level | Allocation decision |
|---|---|
| No risk | 100% in T-Bills |
| Some risk | 50% in T-Bills; 50% in Market Portfolio |
| Even more risk | 100% in Market Portfolio |
| A risk hog | Borrow money; invest in market portfolio |

## p.10 — The Risk of an Individual Asset (beta)
**Teaches:** The risk of any asset is the risk it adds to the market portfolio. Statistically, this is measured by how much the asset moves with the market — its covariance with the market. Beta is a standardized measure of this covariance. It is found by dividing the covariance of the asset with the market by the variance of the market. Beta measures the non-diversifiable risk of an asset. The required return on an investment is a linear function of its beta.
**Formulas:** Beta = Covariance(asset returns, market returns) / Variance(market returns). Expected Return = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Expected Return on the Market Portfolio - Riskfree Rate), where Beta is the asset's beta, the Riskfree Rate is the return on a riskless asset, and the term in parentheses is the market (equity) risk premium.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: riskfree rate, beta, expected market return → output: expected/required return; beta itself deterministic from covariance and market variance).
**Tags:** beta, capm, covariance, expected-return, non-diversifiable-risk

## p.11 — Alternatives to the CAPM
**Teaches:** There are three families of options beyond the CAPM. (1) Modified versions of the CAPM ease some assumptions, such as those about transactions costs, taxes, or return distributions. (2) Extended versions allow for more than one market risk factor. The arbitrage pricing model (APM) allows many market risk factors but leaves them unnamed (purely statistical). Multifactor models instead use macroeconomic variables as stand-ins for market risk factors. (3) Proxy models look for traits shared by stocks that earned higher returns in the past. Those traits are then used as proxies for risk.

## p.12 — Risk and Cost of Equity: the role of the marginal investor
**Teaches:** Three ideas link risk to the cost of equity. (1) Not all risk counts: it is intuitive that the cost of equity should be higher for riskier assets and lower for safer ones. The real question is which risk should be built into the cost of equity. (2) Risk through whose eyes: risk is usually defined as the variance of actual returns around an expected return. Risk and return models in finance instead say the risk that should be paid for, and thus built into the discount rate in valuation, is the risk seen by the marginal investor. (3) The diversification effect: most models also assume the marginal investor is well diversified. The only risk he or she sees is thus risk that cannot be diversified away — market or non-diversifiable risk. In effect, it is mostly economic, macro, ongoing risk that should go into the cost of equity.
**Rules:** Only non-diversifiable (market) risk, as seen by the well-diversified marginal investor, belongs in the cost of equity and discount rate.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual principles for choosing which risk to price).
**Tags:** cost-of-equity, marginal-investor, market-risk, discount-rate

## p.13 — Timeline: the history of risk measures
**Teaches:** A timeline pairs key events in the history of risk thinking with the risk measure in use at each stage. Risk measures moved from gut feeling, to computed probabilities, to sample-based probabilities, to expected loss, price variance, bond and stock ratings, variance added to a portfolio, market beta, factor betas, macro betas, and finally proxies.

|---|---|---|

| 1494 | Luca Pacioli posits his puzzle with two gamblers in a coin-tossing game | — |
| 1654 | Pascal and Fermat solve the Pacioli puzzle and lay foundations for probability estimation and theory | Computed probabilities |
| 1662 | Graunt generates life table using data on births and deaths in London | — |
| 1711 | Bernoulli states the "law of large numbers", providing the basis for sampling from large populations | Sample-based probabilities |
| 1738 | de Moivre derives the normal distribution as an approximation to the binomial; Gauss and Laplace refine it | — |
| 1763 | Bayes publishes his treatise on how to update prior beliefs as new information is acquired | — |
| 1800s | Insurance business develops, bringing actuarial measures of risk based on historical data | Expected loss |
| 1900 | Bachelier examines stock and option prices on Paris exchanges; defends thesis that prices follow a random walk | Price variance |

| 1992 | Fama and French conclude that market cap and book-to-price are better proxies for risk than beta or betas | Proxies |
