## p.2 — Accrual versus cash accounting
**Teaches:** Accrual accounting records transactions when they occur, not when cash moves. Revenues are booked when a product or service is sold, not when the customer pays. Expenses are matched to the period of the sale they support. This holds even if the cash was spent earlier or will be paid later. Cash accounting instead books revenues when payment arrives and expenses when paid. A firm that is not small or personal must follow accrual rules.
**Rules:** Accrual: book revenue at sale; match expenses to the same period as the related revenue. Cash: book on cash receipt or payment. Only small or personal businesses may use cash accounting.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must decide when a deal "occurs").

## p.3 — Classifying expenses: operating, financing, capital
**Teaches:** Every expense falls into one of three classes. Operating expenses come from running the business. They cover the direct costs of making the product or service sold, plus other costs of production such as S,G&A. Financing expenses come from using non-equity funding. Most often this is interest expense on debt. Capital expenses give benefits over many years. For a manufacturer they are plant and equipment. For firms that do not make goods, they take less solid forms. Accounting handles those poorly.
**Rules:** Classify by asking: tied to operations this period → operating; tied to non-equity funding → financing; gives benefits over many years → capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must sort each expense by hand).

## p.4 — Everything has a place: how expenses flow to statements
**Teaches:** A flow chart maps each expense class to a statement. Operating expenses are netted from revenues on this year's income statement. Financing expenses show up as financial expenses there too. The borrowings behind them sit on the balance sheet as debt. Capital expenses show up as assets on the balance sheet. They are then written off as depreciation over the asset's life. The slide's income statement table:

| Step | Item | Explanation |
|---|---|---|
| Start with | Revenues | The accountant's count of sales in the period |
| Net out | Operating Expenses | All expenses tied to operations this year, with no benefit spilling into future years |
| To get | Operating Profit | Profitability of business |
| Net out | Financial Expenses | Expenses tied to non-equity funding |
| To get | Taxable Income | Income to equity investors, prior to taxes |
| Net out | Taxes | Based on taxable income; may not equal cash taxes paid |
| To get | Net Income | Income to equity investors, after taxes |

The balance sheet's asset side: fixed assets (long-lived physical assets), current assets (short-lived assets), financial assets (stakes in securities and other firms), and intangible assets (non-physical assets). The other side: current liabilities (short-term obligations), debt (long-term debt), other long-term obligations, and shareholders' equity.
**Formulas:** Operating Profit = Revenues − Operating Expenses. Taxable Income = Operating Profit − Financial Expenses. Net Income = Taxable Income − Taxes.

## p.5 — The income statement structure, step by step
**Teaches:** The full income statement ladder, one line at a time. Start with revenues. Net out cost of goods sold to get gross profit. That shows profit from production alone. Net out other operating expenses to get operating profit. These are S,G&A and other costs of running the firm, not tied straight to making the goods. Net out financial expenses to get taxable income. That is income for equity investors before taxes. Net out taxes to get net income. That is income for equity investors after taxes.
**Formulas:** Gross Profit = Revenues − COGS. Operating Profit = Gross Profit − Other Operating Expenses. Taxable Income = Operating Profit − Financial Expenses. Net Income = Taxable Income − Taxes. (COGS = cost of goods sold.)
**Data:**

| To get | Taxable Income | Income for equity investors, prior to taxes |

## p.6 — Revenue recognition and ASC 606
**Teaches:** For most firms revenue recognition is simple: book revenue when the product or service is sold. It gets tricky for firms that sell over many years. How much revenue to book in the sale year, and how much later, is open to debate. ASC 606 states the core principle: "depict the transfer of promised goods or services to customers in an amount that reflects the consideration to which the entity expects to be entitled in exchange for those goods or services." So a real estate developer on a multi-year build books revenue as construction progresses. A software firm with a multi-year contract books revenue as performance obligations are met.
**Rules:** ASC 606: recognize revenue as goods or services transfer to the customer, at the expected consideration. For multi-year contracts, performance obligations set the timing.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must judge progress to spread revenue across years).
**Tags:** revenue-recognition, asc-606, accrual-accounting, multi-year-contracts

## p.8 — Operating expenses: COGS versus other operating expenses
**Teaches:** Operating expenses split into two groups. Cost of goods sold covers costs tied straight to making the goods or services sold. Other operating expenses relate to running the firm, but not straight to sales. COGS is netted from revenues to get gross profit. The rest is netted from gross profit to get operating income. In many firms the largest item outside COGS is S,G&A (selling, general and administrative). It is a vague catch-all that can hold almost anything.
**Formulas:** Gross Profit = Revenues − COGS. Operating Income = Gross Profit − Other Operating Expenses.
**Rules:** Directly tied to producing the revenue-generating goods → COGS. Operations-related but not directly tied to revenues → other operating expenses (e.g. S,G&A).
**Compute:** MIXED — the subtraction is DETERMINISTIC; the COGS versus other-operating split is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** operating-expenses, cogs, sga, gross-profit

## p.9 — Three forms of depreciation: economic, accounting, tax
**Teaches:** Depreciation comes in three forms. Economic depreciation is the real loss in value (earning power) as an asset ages. It needs nuance. It varies even within one asset type, based on how the asset is used. Accounting depreciation is more mechanical. It is driven mostly by the asset's age. The main choice: write it off evenly (straight-line) or faster up front (accelerated). Tax depreciation is what the tax office allows when computing taxable income.
**Rules:** Know which concept is in play: economic (true value loss), accounting (book reporting), tax (allowed for taxable income).
**Compute:** MIXED — accounting and tax depreciation are DETERMINISTIC given method, life, and cost; economic depreciation is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** depreciation, accounting-depreciation, tax-depreciation, economic-depreciation

## p.10 — Financial expenses: interest, implicit interest, netting
**Teaches:** The most common financial expense is interest on debt, from bank loans or corporate bonds. Accountants also treat other commitments, such as leases, as debt. Some interest expense is therefore implicit. They compute it from the debt value of the commitments and current interest rates. Some firms net interest expense against interest income earned on cash. They then report a net interest expense. If interest income exceeds interest expense, the line becomes net interest income.
**Formulas:** Net Interest Expense = Interest Expense − Interest Income (a negative value means net interest income). Implicit interest ≈ debt-equivalent value of commitment × current interest rate.
**Compute:** MIXED — the netting is DETERMINISTIC; estimating the debt-equivalent value of commitments like leases is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** interest-expense, financial-expenses, leases, net-interest

## p.11 — Income from non-operating investments
**Teaches:** Firms also earn income outside their core business. Most firms invest cash in marketable securities, like treasury bills and commercial paper. The income earned is reported on its own or netted against interest expense. For cross holdings in other firms, treatment depends on stake size. A small or minority holding's income usually appears as a line in the income statement. A majority stake usually forces consolidation. The parent then counts 100% of the subsidiary's revenues, operating expenses and operating income as its own.
**Rules:** Minority (small) holding → report the holding's income in the income statement. Majority stake → consolidate: count 100% of subsidiary revenues, operating expenses and operating income.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC in application (ownership percentage → reporting treatment).
**Tags:** non-operating-income, cross-holdings, consolidation, marketable-securities

## p.12 — Extraordinary income and expenses
**Teaches:** Extraordinary items are ones a firm does not face in the normal course of business. Examples: a one-time cost or gain from selling assets or divisions. Write-offs or charges from past projects, lawsuits or fines. Impairment of goodwill from past deals. A truly extraordinary item shows up rarely and its amount varies. If it appears every year, it is not extraordinary — even if it flips between profits and losses.
**Rules:** Extraordinary test: infrequent AND variable in amount. Shows up every year → not extraordinary, regardless of sign flips.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must weigh how often the item recurs and why).
**Tags:** extraordinary-items, goodwill-impairment, one-time-charges, earnings-quality

## p.13 — Pro-forma accounting: con game or legitimate restatement?
**Teaches:** Firms have grown creative with pro-forma financial statements. Some do it to fix what they see as flaws in the accounting rules. Others do it to make their profits look better. Never take pro-forma numbers at face value. Build your own smell test for what should be added back and what should not. Two items deserve the most attention. First, the shift of expenses from operating to capital — sometimes merited, sometimes not. Second, the removal of expenses as one-time or extraordinary.
**Rules:** Never accept pro-forma numbers at face value. Scrutinize (1) operating-to-capital expense shifts and (2) removal of "one-time or extraordinary" expenses.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must weigh whether each add-back is fair).
**Tags:** pro-forma, earnings-quality, adjustments, expense-classification
