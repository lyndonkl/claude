## p.2 — Reality check: Value ≠ Price
**Teaches:** Value and price are different quantities driven by different forces. Intrinsic value is driven by cash flows from existing assets, growth in those cash flows, and the quality of that growth, and is estimated from accounting and valuation estimates. Price is driven by market moods & momentum and by surface stories about fundamentals (demand and supply). Between them sits THE GAP, and the investor's three questions are: Is there a gap? If so, will it close? If it will close, what will cause it to close?
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** value-vs-price, intrinsic-value, market-pricing, behavioral-finance

## p.3 — The Drivers of Value (DCF structure diagram)
**Teaches:** A diagram of the generic DCF value drivers. Current cash flows are the cash flows from existing investments, net of any reinvestment needed to sustain future growth; they can be computed before debt cash flows (to the firm) or after debt cash flows (to equity investors). Expected growth during the high-growth period comes from two sources: growth from new investments (a function of the amount and quality of investments) and efficiency growth (using existing assets better). The length of the high-growth period depends on the magnitude and sustainability of competitive advantages, since value-creating growth requires excess returns. At the end sits the terminal value of the firm (equity): a stable-growth firm with no or very limited excess returns. Cash flows are discounted at a cost of financing (debt or capital) determined by the operating risk of the company, its default risk, and the mix of debt and equity used in financing.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** dcf, value-drivers, growth, terminal-value, cost-of-capital

## p.4 — The two faces of discounted cash flow valuation
**Teaches:** The value of a risky asset can be estimated two equivalent ways: (1) discount expected cash flows at a risk-adjusted discount rate, or (2) replace expected cash flows with their certainty equivalents (the guaranteed cash flows you would accept instead) and discount at the riskfree rate. Risk adjustment goes either in the discount rate or in the cash flows, never both.
**Formulas:**
- Value of asset = E(CF_1)/(1+r) + E(CF_2)/(1+r)^2 + E(CF_3)/(1+r)^3 + ... + E(CF_n)/(1+r)^n, where the asset has an n-year life, E(CF_t) is the expected cash flow in period t, and r is a discount rate that reflects the risk of the cash flows.
- Value of asset = CE(CF_1)/(1+r_f) + CE(CF_2)/(1+r_f)^2 + CE(CF_3)/(1+r_f)^3 + ... + CE(CF_n)/(1+r_f)^n, where CE(CF_t) is the certainty equivalent of E(CF_t) and r_f is the riskfree rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given cash flows and discount rate → present value); choosing the inputs is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, present-value, certainty-equivalent, risk-adjusted-discount-rate

## p.5 — DCF Choices: Equity Valuation versus Firm Valuation
**Teaches:** A financial balance-sheet view of the two DCF choices. Firm valuation values the entire business: assets in place (existing investments that generate cash flows today, including long-lived fixed assets and short-lived working capital) plus growth assets (expected value to be created by future investments). Equity valuation values only the equity claim. On the liability side, debt is a fixed claim on cash flows with little or no role in management, fixed maturity, and tax-deductible payments; equity is a residual claim on cash flows with a significant role in management and perpetual life.

**Tags:** firm-valuation, equity-valuation, financial-balance-sheet, debt-vs-equity

## p.8 — Value: The Drivers (FCFF driver map)
**Teaches:** The value drivers for a company map into a single free-cash-flow equation. Revenue growth is a function of the size of the total accessible market and market share; operating margins are determined by pricing power and cost efficiencies; growth/investment efficiency measures how much investment is needed to deliver growth. These feed expected FCFF, which is discounted at a risk-adjusted discount rate built from the cost of equity (the rate of return equity investors demand) and the cost of debt (the cost of borrowing money, net of tax advantages). The value of the business is also affected by failure risk: the chance of a grievous or catastrophic event putting the business model at risk.
**Formulas:** Expected FCFF = Revenues * Operating Margin - Taxes - Reinvestment, where FCFF is free cash flow to the firm, Revenues * Operating Margin is operating income (EBIT), Taxes are taxes on that operating income, and Reinvestment is net capital expenditure plus working-capital investment needed to deliver growth.
**Compute:** MIXED — the FCFF arithmetic is deterministic given revenues, margin, taxes, and reinvestment; estimating the drivers and failure risk is judgment.
**Tags:** fcff, value-drivers, revenue-growth, operating-margin, discount-rate, failure-risk

## p.9 — Healthy Valuation = Story + Numbers
**Teaches:** A good valuation bridges the numbers people and the narrative people. The numbers people favor accounting statements, Excel spreadsheets, statistical measures, and pricing data; their illusions/delusions are (1) precision — that data is precise, (2) objectivity — that data has no bias, and (3) control — that data can control reality. The narrative people favor anecdotes, experience (own or others'), and behavioral evidence; their illusions/delusions are (1) creativity cannot be quantified, (2) if the story is good, the investment will be, and (3) experience is the best teacher. A good valuation combines both.

**Tags:** narrative-and-numbers, valuation-philosophy, bias

## p.10 — The steps in valuation (5-step narrative-to-value process)
**Teaches:** Five steps to a valuation. Step 1: Develop a narrative for the business you are valuing — your story of how the business evolves over time. Step 2: Test the narrative to see if it is possible, plausible and probable — many narratives are possible, fewer plausible, only a few probable. Step 3: Convert the narrative into drivers of value — from potential market size down to cash flows and risk, so each part of the narrative has a place in your numbers and each number is backed by a portion of your story. Step 4: Connect the drivers of value to a valuation — an intrinsic valuation model linking the inputs to an end value. Step 5: Keep the feedback loop open — listen to people who know the business better than you do, fine-tune or alter your narrative, and work out the value effects of alternative narratives.
**Rules:** Narrative test hierarchy: possible → plausible → probable; only probable narratives belong in a base-case valuation. Every number must be backed by story; every story element must map to a number.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** valuation-process, narrative-and-numbers, story-to-numbers

## p.23 — Value and Price (Tesla value bridge)
**Teaches:** The bridge from operating-asset value to value per share, with three annotations. First, more than 80% of Tesla's value of equity comes from its terminal value — a feature of valuing a growth company, not a bug. Second, a DCF is a going-concern value; if there is a high risk the company will not make it, failure must be brought in explicitly (here probability of failure = 0%). Third, the value of options is subtracted from equity value — the option overhang effect. Result: estimated value per share is $571.29 versus a price of $1,200, so price is 210% of value. Tesla looks dramatically overpriced relative to this narrative.

Value per share = Value of equity in common stock / Number of shares. ## p.26 — Facing up to uncertainty with a simulation: Paytm
**Teaches:** Instead of point estimates, key inputs can be given probability distributions and the valuation run as a Monte Carlo simulation. The case is Paytm, valued ahead of its September 2021 IPO. Four inputs got distributions:
- GMV growth rate in years 1–5: lognormal distribution.
- Target take rate (revenue/GMV) in year 10: triangular distribution.
- Target operating margin in year 10: minimum-extreme distribution, with a 0.50 correlation between take rate and operating margin.
- Sales to invested capital in years 2–10: uniform distribution. Simulation findings from 100,000 trials: (1) the value of equity was negative about 3% of the time. (2) The median simulated value was ₹1,246,824 (millions), lower than the base-case DCF value of ₹1,456,708. (3) Extreme-value outliers explain the divergence; the maximum equity value approached ₹8,020,677. (4) A more reasonable range for extremes is the 10th percentile (₹627,263) to the 90th percentile (₹2,010,052).
