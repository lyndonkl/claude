## p.2 — Two ways to value equity
**Teaches:** Equity represents a residual cash flow rather than a promised cash flow (unlike debt, equity holders get whatever is left over, not a contractual payment). There are two ways to value equity. (1) Directly: discount cash flows to equity at the cost of equity, which yields the value of equity. (2) Indirectly: discount cash flows to the firm at the cost of capital to get the value of the whole business, then subtract the firm's outstanding debt. That difference should yield the value of equity.
**Rules:** Direct equity valuation: discount equity cash flows at cost of equity. Indirect: discount firm cash flows at cost of capital, then subtract outstanding debt to get equity value.

## p.4 — Equity cash flows: dividends and FCFE
**Teaches:** Two measures of cash flow to equity. (1) Dividends: strictly speaking, the only cash flow equity investors in a publicly traded firm actually receive from the firm is dividends; the earliest valuation models were built around dividends. (2) Free Cash Flow to Equity (FCFE): a measure of POTENTIAL dividends — a broader definition of cash flow, defined as the cash left over after operating expenses, interest expenses, net debt payments, and reinvestment needs. "Net debt payments" means the difference between new debt issued and repayments of old debt; if new debt issued exceeds debt repayments, FCFE will be higher.
**Formulas:** FCFE (conceptual) = Cash flows after operating expenses, interest expenses, net debt payments and reinvestment needs, where net debt payments = new debt issued − repayments of old debt.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given the inputs (operating cash flows, interest expense, new debt issued, debt repaid, reinvestment → FCFE); choosing/forecasting those inputs is judgment.
**Tags:** fcfe, dividends, equity-cash-flows, potential-dividends

## p.5 — The cost of equity
**Teaches:** The cost of equity is the rate of return that equity investors demand for being invested in the stock. It should reflect the risk they see in the stock — higher risk goes with a higher cost of equity. The cost of equity can differ across investors in the same business, but when valuing the business you should use the cost of equity of the MARGINAL investors in the stock.
**Rules:** Higher perceived risk → higher cost of equity. Use the marginal investor's cost of equity when valuing a business, not any individual investor's.

## p.6 — Worked example: Con Ed stable-growth dividend valuation
**Teaches:** Applies the stable-growth (Gordon growth) dividend discount model to a mature dividend-paying stock and compares the resulting intrinsic value to the market price to judge over/undervaluation.
**Formulas:** Value of equity per share = DPS0 × (1 + g) / (r − g), where DPS0 = current dividends per share, g = perpetual growth rate of dividends, r = cost of equity. (Gordon growth model; requires r > g and stable growth.)
**Example:** Consolidated Edison (NYC utility): paid dividends per share of $4.00 in 2016 (about 70% of that year's EPS); mature firm expected to grow 2%/year in perpetuity (dividends following suit); cost of equity 8%. Value of equity per share = $4.00 × (1.02) / (0.08 − 0.02) = $68.00. The stock was trading at $70/share at the time, so based on this valuation the stock is slightly overvalued.
**Rules:** If intrinsic value per share < market price, the stock is overvalued (and vice versa).
**Compute:** MIXED — the formula is DETERMINISTIC (DPS, g, r → value per share); choosing g and r is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dividend-discount-model, gordon-growth, con-ed, stable-growth, worked-example

## p.7 — FCFE aside: valuing Con Ed on FCFE instead of dividends
**Teaches:** Using dividends to value Con Ed implicitly assumes it is paying out what it can afford to pay. FCFE is the cash flow left over after reinvestment needs and debt payments. If FCFE differs from dividends, the valuation changes. Here FCFE is $4.25/share for the most recent 12 months versus dividends of $4.00, so the same stable-growth formula gives a higher value.
**Formulas:** Value per share = FCFE0 × (1 + g) / (r − g), same symbols as the dividend model with FCFE replacing dividends.
**Example:** Con Ed: FCFE = $4.25/share (trailing 12 months), g = 2%, r = 8%. Value of Con Ed = $4.25 × (1.02) / (0.08 − 0.02) = $72.25 per share. (Versus $68.00 with dividends; against a $70 market price, the FCFE-based value suggests the stock is slightly undervalued.)
**Compute:** MIXED — formula deterministic (FCFE, g, r → value); estimating FCFE, g, r is judgment.
**Tags:** fcfe, gordon-growth, con-ed, worked-example

## p.9 — Cash flow to the firm
**Teaches:** Defines the cash flow to the firm: a broader definition of cash flow that looks not just at equity investors but at the total cash flows generated by the asset for BOTH the equity investor and the lender. It is the cash flow BEFORE debt payments but AFTER operating expenses and taxes.
**Formulas:** Cash flow to the firm (conceptual) = cash flows after operating expenses and taxes but before any debt payments (available to both equity holders and lenders).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (operating cash flows, taxes → firm cash flow); forecasting is judgment.
**Tags:** fcff, firm-cash-flow, dcf

## p.10 — The cost of capital (WACC)
**Teaches:** The cost of capital is a composite cost of ALL the capital invested in an asset or business; it brings in the cost and weight of debt. The cost of debt is the rate at which the business can borrow money, long term, TODAY (a current market rate, not a historical one). The cost of capital is a weighted average of the cost of equity and the AFTER-TAX cost of borrowing.
**Formulas:** Cost of Capital = Cost of Equity × (Equity / (Debt + Equity)) + Cost of Debt × (1 − tax rate) × (Debt / (Debt + Equity)). Here Equity and Debt are the (market) values of equity and debt financing. Cost of Debt is the current long-term borrowing rate, and the tax rate captures the tax deductibility of interest.
**Rules:** Use today's long-term borrowing rate for cost of debt; use the after-tax cost of debt in the weighted average.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cost of equity, cost of debt, tax rate, debt and equity weights → cost of capital); estimating the component costs is judgment.
**Tags:** cost-of-capital, wacc, cost-of-debt, after-tax

## p.11 — The four determinants of DCF value
**Teaches:** A timeline diagram of discounted cash flow valuation showing the four determinants of the value of a business, each with a firm-vs-equity version: (1) Cash flows — Firm: pre-debt cash flow; Equity: after-debt cash flows. (2) Expected growth — Firm: growth in operating earnings; Equity: growth in net income/EPS. (3) Length of the period of high growth — cash flows CF1 through CFn are forecast during the high-growth period, after which the firm is in stable growth, growing at a constant rate forever, which is captured in a Terminal Value. (4) Discount rate — Firm: cost of capital; Equity: cost of equity. Value obtained is Value of Firm (firm approach) or Value of Equity (equity approach).
**Rules:** Match all four inputs to the same approach: firm valuation uses pre-debt cash flows, operating-earnings growth, and cost of capital; equity valuation uses after-debt cash flows, net income/EPS growth, and cost of equity. High-growth period ends in a terminal value based on constant stable growth forever.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework; the DCF mechanics it implies are deterministic once cash flows, growth, horizon, and discount rate are chosen).
**Tags:** dcf, terminal-value, expected-growth, discount-rate, valuation-framework

## p.12 — Valuation consistency
**Teaches:** Make consistent assumptions about cash flows, debt ratios, and risk, and you should get the SAME value for equity from both approaches. Direct means discounting cash flows to equity at the cost of equity. Indirect means discounting cash flows to the firm at the cost of capital and subtracting debt. In practice, it is really difficult to stay consistent as you move from one approach to the other.
**Rules:** Equity value (direct) = Equity value (indirect, firm value minus debt) if and only if assumptions about cash flows, debt ratios, and risk are consistent across the two approaches.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (consistency principle).
