## p.3 — Misconceptions about Valuation (three myths)
**Teaches:** Three common myths about valuation and their corrective "truths." Myth 1: valuation is an objective search for "true" value. Truth 1.1: all valuations are biased; the only questions are how much bias and in which direction. Truth 1.2: the direction and magnitude of the bias is directly proportional to who pays you and how much you are paid. Myth 2: a good valuation provides a precise estimate of value. Truth 2.1: there are no precise valuations. Truth 2.2: the payoff to valuation is greatest when valuation is least precise. Myth 3: the more quantitative a model, the better the valuation. Truth 3.1: your understanding of a valuation model is inversely proportional to the number of inputs it requires. Truth 3.2: simpler valuation models do much better than complex ones.
**Rules:** Prefer simpler valuation models over complex ones; expect and diagnose bias (follow who is paying the analyst); do not demand precision — the biggest payoff to valuation comes exactly where it is least precise (e.g., young/difficult-to-value firms).

## p.5 — Approaches to Valuation (the three approaches)
**Teaches:** There are three approaches to valuation. (1) Intrinsic valuation: relates the value of an asset to its capacity to generate cash flows and the risk in those cash flows. In its most common form, intrinsic value is computed with a discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation. There, value equals the present value of expected future cash flows on the asset. (2) Relative valuation or Pricing: estimates how much to pay for an asset by looking at what others pay for "comparable" assets. The prices are scaled to a common metric they all share, such as earnings, revenues, or subscribers. (3) Contingent claim (real option) valuation: adds value for assets whose cash flows depend on an event happening.

## p.6 — Basis for all valuation approaches
**Teaches:** Using valuation models in investment decisions rests on two beliefs. First, markets are inefficient and make mistakes in assessing value. Second, you hold some view of how and when those mistakes will be corrected. In an efficient market, the market price is the best estimate of value; any valuation model then merely justifies that price. Investment philosophies that claim to beat the market differ in what mistakes they see and how they expect those mistakes to be fixed.

## p.7 — Discounted Cashflow Valuation (DCF): definition and requirements
**Teaches:** What it is: in DCF valuation, the value of an asset is the present value of the expected cash flows on the asset. Philosophical basis: every asset has an intrinsic value that can be estimated based on its cash flows, growth, and risk. Information needed: (1) the life of the asset, (2) the cash flows during the life of the asset, and (3) the discount rate to apply to those cash flows to get present value. Market inefficiency assumption: markets make mistakes in pricing assets across time but correct themselves over time as new information comes out.
**Formulas:** Value of asset = Present value of expected future cash flows. In symbols: Value = sum over t of E(CF_t) / (1+r)^t. Here E(CF_t) = expected cash flow in period t; r = discount rate reflecting the riskiness of the cash flows; t = period.
**Compute:** MIXED — discounting is DETERMINISTIC given cash flows and discount rate (inputs: CF_t, r, asset life → output: present value); estimating cash flows, life, and discount rate is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, intrinsic-valuation, present-value, discount-rate

## p.8 — Advantages of DCF Valuation
**Teaches:** DCF, done right, is based on an asset's fundamentals. It is therefore less exposed to market moods and perceptions. If good investors buy businesses rather than stocks (the Warren Buffett adage), DCF is the right way to think about what you get when you buy an asset. DCF forces you to study the firm and understand its business. It brings you face to face with the assumptions built into the price you pay. If value comes from a company's cash flows, a long time horizon shields you from what the market thinks of your investment.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** dcf, advantages, fundamentals, long-horizon

## p.9 — Disadvantages of DCF valuation
**Teaches:** DCF requires far more explicit inputs and information than other approaches. Those inputs are noisy and hard to estimate. Worse, the analyst can bend them to reach a desired conclusion; the "quality" of such an analyst becomes how well the tampering is hidden. In an intrinsic valuation model there is no guarantee anything will emerge as under- or over-valued. It is possible to find every stock in a market overvalued. That is a problem for equity research analysts, who must recommend the most under- and over-valued stocks in their sector. It is also a problem for equity portfolio managers, who must stay fully or nearly fully invested in equities.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** dcf, disadvantages, input-noise, analyst-bias

## p.10 — When DCF Valuation works best
**Teaches:** DCF is built for assets (firms) whose value comes from their capacity to generate future cash flows. Investments that produce no cash flows — gold, currencies, collectibles — cannot be valued with it. It works best for three kinds of investors. First, those with a long time horizon, which allows the market time to correct its mistakes and lets price revert to "true" value. Second, those who can provide the catalyst that moves price to value — activist investors or potential acquirers of the whole firm. Third, those not easily swayed by peer pressure, meaning market movements contrary to their "value" views.
**Rules:** Use DCF only for cash-flow-generating assets; require either a long horizon or catalyst capability; do not apply DCF to gold, currencies, or collectibles.

## p.11 — Relative Valuation (Pricing): definition and requirements
**Teaches:** What it is: the "value" of any asset can be estimated from how the market prices "similar" or "comparable" assets. Philosophical basis: intrinsic value is impossible, or close to impossible, to estimate. The price of an asset is whatever the market will pay for it, given its traits. Information needed: (1) an identical asset or a group of comparable/similar assets. (2) A standardized measure of value — in equity, you divide price by a common variable such as earnings or book value, giving a multiple. (3) If assets are not perfectly comparable, variables to control for the differences. Market inefficiency assumption: pricing errors across similar assets are easier to spot, easier to exploit, and much more quickly fixed.
**Compute:** MIXED — computing a multiple (price / common variable) is DETERMINISTIC; choosing comparables and controlling for differences is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** relative-valuation, pricing, multiples, comparables

## p.12 — Advantages of Relative Valuation
**Teaches:** Relative valuation is much more likely to reflect market perceptions and moods than DCF. That is an advantage when price should reflect those perceptions. Examples: the objective is to sell the asset at that price today (IPO, M&A), or you are investing on momentum strategies. With relative valuation there will always be a significant proportion of securities that are under- and over-valued; since portfolio managers are judged on relative performance (versus the market and other money managers), relative valuation is tailored to their needs. It generally requires less explicit information than DCF. In relative valuation you are playing the "incremental" game — hoping to make money by getting the next increment (earnings report, news story, etc.) right.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** relative-valuation, advantages, momentum, portfolio-management

## p.13 — Disadvantages of Relative Valuation
**Teaches:** A portfolio of stocks undervalued on a relative basis may still be overvalued in absolute terms. It is just less overvalued than other securities. Relative valuation assumes markets are right in the aggregate but wrong on individual securities. To the degree whole markets can be over- or under-valued, relative valuation fails. It seems to need less information only because hidden assumptions are made about other variables that DCF would state explicitly. If those hidden assumptions are wrong, the relative valuation is also wrong.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** relative-valuation, disadvantages, aggregate-mispricing, implicit-assumptions

## p.14 — When relative valuation works best
**Teaches:** Relative valuation is easiest to use when: (1) there are a large number of assets comparable to the one being valued; (2) these assets are priced in a market; (3) there exists some common variable to standardize the price. It works best for investors with relatively short time horizons. It also suits investors judged against a relative benchmark, such as the market or other portfolio managers with the same style. Finally, the investor must be able to act on relative mispricing. A hedge fund can buy the undervalued assets and sell the overvalued ones; a long-only investor cannot.
**Rules:** Prefer relative valuation when many market-priced comparables and a standardizing variable exist; match the approach to a short horizon, relative benchmarking, and long-short capability.

**Tags:** relative-valuation, applicability, comparables, hedge-funds

## p.15 — Investments that can only be priced (value vs. price by asset type)
**Teaches:** Some investments can be both valued and priced; others can only be priced. Valuation requires cash flows; pricing only requires comparison.
**Data:**
| Investment type | To value | To price |
|---|---|---|
| Assets | Can be valued based upon expected cashflows, with higher cashflows & lower risk = higher value. | Can be priced against similar assets, after controlling for cash flows and risk. |
| Commodity | Can be valued based upon utilitarian demand and supply, but with long lags in both. | Can be priced against its own history (normalized price over time). |
| Currency | Cannot be valued. | Can be priced against other currencies, with greater acceptance & more stable purchasing power = higher price. |
| Collectible | Cannot be valued. | Can be priced based upon scarcity and desirability. |
**Rules:** Cash-flow assets: value or price. Commodities: value only via long-lag demand/supply, price against normalized own history. Currencies and collectibles: cannot be valued, only priced.

## p.16 — Asset Based Valuation: A Detour
**Teaches:** Instead of valuing a business as a going concern (cash flows) or by relative pricing, you can sometimes value it by valuing its assets one by one. Asset-based valuation is used in three contexts. (1) Liquidation valuation — valuing the assets for sale. (2) Accounting valuation — valuing individual assets for accounting reasons, such as fair value or goodwill estimation. (3) Sum-of-the-parts valuation — to see if a company is cheap as an investment or a good target for acquisition/restructuring. However, to value each asset you still must use either expected cash flows (intrinsic valuation) or the pricing of similar assets (relative valuation). So asset-based valuation is not a truly independent third approach. It is easiest when assets are separable and have stand-alone earnings or cash flows.
**Rules:** Use asset-based valuation for liquidation, accounting (fair value/goodwill), or sum-of-the-parts contexts; feasible mainly when assets are separable with stand-alone cash flows.

**Tags:** asset-based-valuation, liquidation, sum-of-the-parts, goodwill

## p.18 — Contingent Claim (Option) Valuation: features of options
**Teaches:** Options have three defining features. (1) They derive their value from an underlying asset, which has value. (2) The payoff on a call (put) option occurs only if the value of the underlying asset ends up greater (lesser) than an exercise price specified when the option is created. If this contingency does not occur, the option is worthless. (3) They have a fixed life. Any security that shares these features can be valued as an option.
**Rules:** If a security (a) derives value from an underlying asset, (b) pays off only on a contingency relative to an exercise price, and (c) has a fixed life, it can be valued as an option.

**Tags:** option-valuation, contingent-claims, real-options

## p.19 — Option Payoff Diagrams
**Teaches:** Payoff diagrams for call and put options, drawn against the value of the underlying asset at expiry. A call option pays nothing until the asset value reaches the strike price; the payoff line is flat there (at a loss equal to the premium, if the purchase cost is drawn in). Above the strike, its payoff rises one-for-one with asset value. A put option's payoff rises as asset value falls below the strike price and is flat (worthless) for asset values above the strike. The strike price is the kink point in both diagrams.
**Formulas:** Call payoff at expiration = max(S - K, 0); Put payoff at expiration = max(K - S, 0), where S = value of the underlying asset at expiration, K = strike (exercise) price.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC — inputs S and K → call and put payoffs via the max functions above.
**Tags:** options, payoff-diagrams, call-option, put-option

## p.21 — Indirect Examples of Options (real options)
**Teaches:** Assets that behave like options without being labeled as such. (1) Equity in a deeply troubled firm — one with negative earnings and high leverage — can be viewed as an option to liquidate held by the stockholders. Viewed as such, it is a call option on the assets of the firm. (2) Reserves owned by natural resource firms are call options on the underlying resource, since the firm can decide whether and how much of the resource to extract. (3) A patent owned by a firm, or an exclusive license issued to it, is an option on the underlying product (project); the firm owns this option for the duration of the patent. (4) The rights a firm possesses to expand an existing investment into new markets or new products.

## p.22 — Advantages of Using Option Pricing Models
**Teaches:** Two advantages. (1) Option pricing models let us value assets we otherwise could not value. Equity in deeply troubled firms and stock of a small biotechnology firm with no revenues and no profits are hard to value with DCF or multiples. They can be valued with option pricing. (2) Option pricing models provide fresh insights into the drivers of value. When an asset derives value from its option characteristics, more risk or variability can increase value rather than decrease it.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT
**Tags:** option-pricing, advantages, distressed-firms, volatility-value

## p.23 — Disadvantages of Option Pricing Models
**Teaches:** Three disadvantages. (1) When real options (natural resource options, product patents) are valued, many inputs for the option pricing model are hard to obtain. Projects do not trade, so getting a current value or a variance for a project can be a daunting task. (2) Option pricing models derive value from an underlying asset, so you must first value the assets — option pricing is an addendum to another valuation approach, not a standalone one. (3) There is a danger of double counting assets. Suppose an analyst uses a higher growth rate in a DCF for a pharmaceutical firm because it has valuable patents. If the analyst then also values the patents as options and adds them to the DCF value, the patents are counted twice.
**Rules:** Never both embed an option's payoff in DCF growth assumptions and add the option's separately estimated value — pick one treatment to avoid double counting.

**Tags:** option-pricing, disadvantages, double-counting, real-options

## p.24 — In summary: three approaches, know all three
**Teaches:** There are hundreds of valuation models and metrics, but only three approaches. (1) Intrinsic valuation — usually, but not always, a DCF valuation. (2) Relative valuation or pricing. (3) Contingent claim valuation. The three can yield different values for the same asset at the same point in time. To truly grasp valuation you must know and use all three. There is a time and a place for each, and knowing when to use each is key to mastering valuation.
