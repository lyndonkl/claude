## p.2 — The Financial Balance Sheet
**Teaches:** A financial (not accounting) balance sheet describes any business in four boxes. On the asset side, Assets in Place are the value of investments the firm has already made over its history. That value is updated to reflect their current cash flow potential. Growth Assets are the value of investments you expect the firm to make in the future. This value rests on the chances you see for the firm. On the other side, Debt holders (lenders, both short and long term) get first claim on any cash flow the firm makes. Equity investors get what is left over after the debt is paid. This framing values assets by what they will earn, not by past cost, and it ranks claims by their place in line.

## p.3 — Assets in Place
**Teaches:** Assets in place reflect investments a business has already made. The investments were made in the past, but the value attached to them is your estimate of what they will generate for you in the future. So the value of assets in place can differ from cost. It can be far lower than what you invested, if earnings power has deteriorated. It can be far higher, if earnings power has improved. It can be about what you invested, if earnings power has stagnated. The key point: value is forward-looking cash flow potential, not historical cost.
**Rules:** Value of assets in place vs. original investment: earnings power deteriorated → value < invested amount; earnings power improved → value > invested amount; earnings power stagnated → value ≈ invested amount.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (requires estimating future earnings power of existing investments).
**Tags:** assets-in-place, valuation, earnings-power, forward-looking-value

## p.4 — Growth Assets
**Teaches:** Growth assets reflect the value of investments you expect a business to make in the future. That value is measured by the "excess returns" you expect on those future investments — returns above the cost of funding them — not by the raw size of future investment. The value of growth assets is higher if the business can access lots of investments that generate excess returns. It is lower if the business has very few investments, or low or zero excess returns. It is negative if the business faces bad (value-destroying) investments and makes them anyway. Growth alone adds no value without excess returns, and growth can destroy value.
**Rules:** Growth asset value: many excess-return investments → high; few investments or zero/low excess returns → low (≈0); bad investments made anyway → negative.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (requires forecasting future investment opportunities and their excess returns).
**Tags:** growth-assets, excess-returns, value-creation, valuation

## p.5 — Debt
**Teaches:** A business can choose to borrow money to fund some or a big part of its value. Three characteristics set debt apart from equity. First, debt carries contractual commitments to make payments to lenders (interest and principal). Second, if the firm fails to meet these commitments, it can go out of business, or the owners can lose their equity stake. Third, in much of the world, tax law is tilted to give a benefit when you borrow; interest is typically tax-deductible. So debt combines a fixed contractual claim, default risk for the borrower, and a tax advantage.
**Rules:** Classify a financing instrument as debt if it has: contractual interest/principal payments; default consequences (loss of business/equity stake) for non-payment; and tax-favored treatment of its payments.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (definitional/classification content).
**Tags:** debt, capital-structure, tax-benefit, default-risk

## p.6 — Equity
**Teaches:** Equity is a residual claim. If you are the sole owner of a business, you can claim whatever cash flows are left over after everyone else has been paid their contractual dues. The Dividend Claim is different: if you are a stockholder in a public company, managers decide how much of those residual cash flows is paid out to you as dividends. So public-company equity holders receive the residual only at managers' discretion. That is a key distinction between private ownership and public stockholding.

**Tags:** equity, residual-claim, dividends, corporate-governance

## p.7 — The Corporate Life Cycle
**Teaches:** Firms move through a six-stage life cycle, drawn as revenues and earnings curves over time. Revenues start near zero, rise steeply through growth, plateau in maturity, and decline at the end. Earnings lag revenues: they are negative early, turn positive later, and also eventually decline. The transitions between stages have names: The Lightbulb (Idea) Moment, From idea to business, The Bar Mitzvah, The Scaling-up Test, The Midlife Crisis, and The End Game.
**Data:**
| Growth stage | Description |
|---|---|
| Stage 1: Start-up | Have an idea for a business that meets an unmet need in the market. |
| Stage 2: Young Growth | Create a business model that converts ideas into potential revenues & earnings. |
| Stage 3: High Growth | Build the business, converting potential into revenues. |
| Stage 4: Mature Growth | Grow your business, shifting from losses to profits. |
| Stage 5: Mature Stable | Defend your business from new competitors & find new markets. |
| Stage 6: Decline | Scale down your business as market shrinks. |

## p.8 — The Asset Side across the Life Cycle
**Teaches:** The mix between assets in place and growth assets shifts predictably across the six life-cycle stages (same revenues/earnings life-cycle diagram as p.7). Early in the life cycle, nearly all value sits in growth assets, and those are unproven. As the firm matures, value migrates into assets in place. In decline, assets in place are all of the value, and they are themselves declining; it is doubtful any growth assets exist.
**Data:**
| Growth stage | Assets in Place | Growth Assets |
|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Start-up | None | All & unproven |
| Stage 2: Young Growth | Very low | Most, and first proof |
| Stage 3: High Growth | Building substance | Most of the value |
| Stage 4: Mature Growth | Becoming larger | Scaling down |
| Stage 5: Mature Stable | Primary component | Tough to find |
| Stage 6: Decline | All & declining | What growth assets? |

## p.9 — The Financing Side across the Life Cycle
**Teaches:** Financing choices track the life cycle ("Financing Choices across the life cycle" diagram). External funding needs are high early, though constrained by lack of infrastructure, and they fall as the firm matures. Internal financing (cash generated by operations) starts negative and eventually exceeds funding needs. The fitting external financing evolves in step: owner's equity and bank debt first, then venture capital and common stock, then debt, and finally retiring debt and buying back stock. Key financing transitions along the way: accessing private equity, the initial public offering, seasoned equity issues, and bond issues.
**Data:**
| Growth stage | External funding needs | Internal financing | External financing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Start-up | High, but constrained by infrastructure | Negative or low | Owner's equity, bank debt |
| Stage 2: Rapid Expansion | High, relative to firm value | Negative or low | Venture capital, common stock |
| Stage 3: High Growth | Moderate, relative to firm value | Low, relative to funding needs | Common stock, warrants, convertibles |
| Stage 4: Mature Growth | Declining, as a percent of firm value | High, relative to funding needs | Debt |
| Stage 5: Decline | Low, as projects dry up | More than funding needs | Retire debt, repurchase stock |

Financing transitions between stages: Accessing private equity → Initial Public Offering → Seasoned equity issue → Bond issues.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing financing requires assessing firm stage, funding needs, and internal cash generation).

## p.10 — The Harvesting Side: Dividend Policy across the Life Cycle
**Teaches:** Dividend policy also follows the life cycle (Figure 10.7: Life Cycle Analysis of Dividend Policy). The gap between internal financing and funding needs sets the capacity to pay dividends. Young firms have negative or low internal cash flows and high external funding needs, so they have no capacity to pay dividends. Capacity first appears in high growth, and even then it is very low. It rises through mature growth. It becomes high in decline, when cash generated exceeds funding needs — the harvesting phase, when cash goes back to owners.
**Data:**
| Growth stage | External funding needs | Internal financing | Capacity to pay dividends |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stage 1: Start-up | High, but constrained by infrastructure | Negative or low | None |
| Stage 2: Rapid Expansion | High, relative to firm value | Negative or low | None |
| Stage 3: High Growth | Moderate, relative to firm value | Low, relative to funding needs | Very low |
| Stage 4: Mature Growth | Low, as projects dry up | High, relative to funding needs | Increasing |
| Stage 5: Decline | Low, as projects dry up | More than funding needs | High |
**Rules:** Capacity to pay dividends = internal financing relative to funding needs: internal cash < funding needs → little or no dividend capacity; internal cash > funding needs → high dividend capacity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (assessing dividend capacity requires estimating internal cash flows vs. funding needs).
**Tags:** dividend-policy, corporate-life-cycle, payout, harvesting
