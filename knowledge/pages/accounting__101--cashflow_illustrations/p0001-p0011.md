## p.2 — Cash Flows: A Life Cycle Perspective

**Teaches:** Operating, investing, and financing cash flows follow a set pattern as a firm ages. The stages are: start-up ("The Lightbulb (Idea) Moment"), young growth ("The Product Test"), high growth ("The Bar Mitzvah"), mature growth ("The Scaling up Test"), mature stable ("The Midlife Crisis"), and decline ("The End Game"). Revenues rise, plateau, then decline over the cycle. Earnings lag revenues: they start negative and turn positive later. Reading a firm's cash flow statement against its life-cycle stage tells you whether its cash pattern is normal for its age. The idea is simple: young firms burn cash and raise it from others, while old firms make cash and give it back.

**Data:**

| Lifecycle stage | Start-up | Young Growth | High Growth | Mature Growth | Mature Stable | Decline |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Operating Cash Flows | Negative, as it starts with losses | Continues to be negative, with working capital adding to losses | Starts to turn positive, as company reports profits | Increasingly positive, driven by net income growth | Positive and stable | Declines with profits dropping |
| Investing Cash Flows | Large investing needs | Continued investing activity | Investing growth starts to slow | Investing stabilizes | Maintenance investing | Asset sales & divestitures |
| Financing Cash Flows | Cash inflows from new equity issuances | Continued cash inflows from new equity issuances | Not much activity | Start of inflows from debt financing & first outflows to equity | Regular debt issuances/payments and significant cash return to equity | Debt repayments & equity cash return |

**Rules:** Classify life-cycle stage by the sign and trend of the three cash flow sections. Negative operating cash + heavy investing + equity inflows = start-up or young growth. Positive, growing operating cash + slowing investing = high or mature growth. Stable operating cash + maintenance investing + cash returned to equity + routine debt rollover = mature stable. Falling operating cash + divestitures + debt repayment + equity cash return = decline.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must judge which stage best fits the firm's cash flow pattern).

## p.3 — A Young Company: Peloton's Cash Flow Statement (2019)

**Teaches:** Peloton's prospectus statement (fiscal years ended June 30, 2017–2019, $ millions) shows the young-growth profile. Three features are flagged. First, operating cash flow is negative or barely positive: (18.6) in 2017, 49.7 in 2018, (108.6) in 2019. The driver is net losses of (71.1), (47.9), and (195.6). Second, financing comes from new equity. Proceeds from redeemable convertible preferred stock (net of issuance costs) were 315.6 in 2017 and 539.1 in 2019. Net financing cash was 143.6, 3.1, and 417.2. Third, stock-based compensation is a large and growing add-back: 10.3, 8.5, then 89.5. It is a non-cash expense, so it widens the gap between net loss and operating cash. Working capital also drags: inventories used (111.3) in 2019, partly offset by accounts payable of 117.3. Investing cash used was (10.2), (56.7), and (297.5); the 2019 figure includes (249.8) of marketable-securities purchases and (83.0) of PP&E. This matches the life-cycle story for a young firm: it loses cash from its business, spends more each year, and pays for all of it with new shares.

**Example:** Peloton FY2019: net loss (195.6) + D&A 21.7 + stock-based comp 89.5 + other adjustments and working-capital changes → operating cash (108.6). Investing: (297.5). Financing: +417.2 (preferred stock issuance 539.1, less repurchases of (130.3)). Net change in cash: +11.3. Ending cash, equivalents and restricted cash: 163.0.

## p.4 — A Growth Company: Netflix's Cash Flow Statement (2020)

**Teaches:** Netflix (years ended Dec 31, 2017–2019, $ thousands) is profitable on the income statement but cash-negative on operations. Net income was 1,866,916 in 2019. Yet operating activities used (2,887,322). The cause: additions to streaming content assets of (13,916,683) in 2019, (13,043,437) in 2018, and (9,805,763) in 2017. Netflix's real capital spending is content, and it runs through the operating section, not investing. Amortization of streaming content assets (9,216,247 in 2019) is added back. The slide also highlights the standard working-capital adjustments: other current assets, accounts payable, accrued expenses and other liabilities, deferred revenue, and other non-current assets and liabilities. Debt fills the funding gap. Proceeds from debt issuance were 4,469,306 (2019), 3,961,852 (2018), and 3,020,510 (2017), plus common-stock issuance of 72,490 in 2019. Net financing cash in 2019: 4,505,662. Lesson: where a firm books its reinvestment matters. A growth firm can show a profit on paper while its business eats cash, with the hole filled by new debt each year.

**Example:** Netflix 2019.
- Net income: 1,866,916. Content additions: (13,916,683). Content amortization add-back: 9,216,247.
- Operating cash: (2,887,322). Investing: (387,064). Financing: +4,505,662.
- Net cash increase: 1,231,745. Ending cash, equivalents and restricted cash: 5,043,786.

## p.7 — Sector and Industry Differences

**Teaches:** Three points about the statement of cash flows. First, it is the most direct financial statement: it is built from actual cash in and cash out, so accounting game playing affects it the least. Second, its stated goal is to explain why the cash balance changed. But it also holds all the items needed to estimate cash flows for analysis and valuation. Third, income statements and cash flow statements can fairly send different messages about a firm's health. Yet steady, lasting gaps between the two can point to accounting problems.

**Rules:** If earnings and operating cash flow keep moving apart (e.g., steady profits but weak operating cash year after year), treat it as a red flag for accounting problems.

**Compute:** JUDGMENT (you must decide whether a gap between earnings and cash flows is a problem).

## p.11 — Bottom Line

**Teaches:** The statement of cash flows views cash flows through the eyes of the equity investors in a business. So if you want cash flows to equity investors, all the needed information should be on the statement. You can also read off what equity investors actually received as cash (dividends and buybacks). If instead you want cash flows prior to debt payments (cash flow to the firm), you can still start from the statement. But interest expenses were netted out on the way to net income, so you must trace them and add them back.

**Rules:** For cash flow to equity: use the statement of cash flows directly. For cash flow to the firm (pre-debt): start from the statement, find interest expense, and add it back.
