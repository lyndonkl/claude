## p.2 — The essence of intrinsic value
**Teaches:** Intrinsic valuation values an asset based on its fundamentals (intrinsic characteristics). For cash-flow-generating assets, intrinsic value is a function of (a) the magnitude of expected cash flows over the asset's lifetime and (b) the uncertainty about receiving those cash flows. Discounted cash flow (DCF) valuation is the tool for estimating intrinsic value. The value of an asset is written as the present value of its expected cash flows. Either the cash flows or the discount rate are adjusted to reflect risk.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual definition).
**Tags:** intrinsic-value, dcf, valuation-fundamentals

## p.3 — The two faces of discounted cash flow valuation
**Teaches:** There are two equivalent ways to risk-adjust in DCF. Way 1: discount expected cash flows at a risk-adjusted discount rate. Way 2: replace expected cash flows with their certainty equivalents — the guaranteed cash flows you would accept instead — and discount those at the riskfree rate.
**Formulas:** Value of asset = E(CF_1)/(1+r) + E(CF_2)/(1+r)^2 + E(CF_3)/(1+r)^3 + ... + E(CF_n)/(1+r)^n. Here the asset has an n-year life. E(CF_t) = expected cash flow in period t. r = discount rate reflecting the risk of the cash flows. Alternative: Value of asset = CE(CF_1)/(1+r_f) + CE(CF_2)/(1+r_f)^2 + CE(CF_3)/(1+r_f)^3 + ... + CE(CF_n)/(1+r_f)^n. CE(CF_t) = certainty equivalent of E(CF_t). r_f = riskfree rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (cash flows, discount rate, n → present value); estimating the inputs is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dcf, risk-adjusted-discount-rate, certainty-equivalent, present-value

## p.4 — Risk Adjusted Value: Two Basic Propositions
**Teaches:** Restates both DCF formulas (risk-adjusted rate on expected cash flows; riskfree rate on certainty equivalents) and gives three propositions. (1) The "IT" proposition: if something (IT) does not affect the expected cash flows or the riskiness of the cash flows, IT cannot affect value. (2) The "DUH" proposition: for an asset to have value, expected cash flows must be positive at some time over its life. (3) The "DON'T FREAK OUT" proposition: assets that generate cash flows early in their life are worth more than assets that generate cash flows later, though the latter may have greater growth and higher later cash flows to compensate.
**Formulas:** Same two formulas as p.3: Value = sum over t of E(CF_t)/(1+r)^t and Value = sum over t of CE(CF_t)/(1+r_f)^t.
**Rules:** If a factor changes neither expected cash flows nor their riskiness, it cannot change value; assets need positive expected cash flows at some point to have value; earlier cash flows are worth more than equal later cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (principles), with the PV formulas DETERMINISTIC given inputs.
**Tags:** dcf, valuation-principles, time-value-of-money

## p.5 — DCF Choices: Equity Valuation versus Firm Valuation
**Teaches:** A financial balance sheet frames two DCF choices. Assets side: Assets in Place (existing investments generating cash flows today, both long-lived fixed and short-lived working-capital assets) and Growth Assets (expected value to be created by future investments). Liabilities side: Debt (fixed claim on cash flows, little/no role in management, fixed maturity, tax deductible) and Equity (residual claim on cash flows, significant role in management, perpetual life). Firm valuation values the entire business (all assets); equity valuation values only the equity claim.

## p.6 — Equity Valuation (Figure 5.5)
**Teaches:** In equity valuation, the cash flows considered are cash flows from assets AFTER debt payments and after reinvestment needed for future growth. The discount rate reflects only the cost of raising equity financing (cost of equity). The resulting present value is the value of just the equity claims on the firm.
**Rules:** Equity cash flows (post-debt) must be discounted at the cost of equity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework).
**Tags:** equity-valuation, cost-of-equity, fcfe

## p.7 — Firm Valuation (Figure 5.6)
**Teaches:** In firm valuation, the cash flows considered are cash flows from assets PRIOR to any debt payments but after the firm has reinvested to create growth assets. The discount rate reflects the cost of raising both debt and equity financing, in proportion to their use (cost of capital / WACC). The present value is the value of the entire firm, reflecting all claims on the firm.
**Rules:** Pre-debt (firm) cash flows must be discounted at the cost of capital.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (framework).
**Tags:** firm-valuation, cost-of-capital, wacc, fcff

## p.10 — Equity versus Firm Valuation (worked example)
**Teaches:** Demonstrates that discounting equity cash flows at the cost of equity and discounting firm cash flows at the cost of capital give consistent answers.

## p.11 — First Principle of Valuation: Discounting Consistency
**Teaches:** The Discounting Consistency Principle: never mix and match cash flows and discount rates; mismatching is "deadly." Discounting equity (post-debt) cash flows at the weighted average cost of capital gives an upwardly biased estimate of equity value. Discounting pre-debt (firm) cash flows at the cost of equity gives a downwardly biased estimate of firm value.
**Rules:** Equity cash flows → cost of equity; firm cash flows → cost of capital. Equity CF at WACC → equity overvalued; firm CF at cost of equity → firm undervalued.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (principle).
**Tags:** discounting-consistency, mismatching, valuation-principles

## p.12 — The Effects of Mismatching Cash Flows and Discount Rates
**Teaches:** Quantifies the three mismatch errors using the p.9–10 example (true equity value $1,073). Error 1: discounting CF to equity at cost of capital overstates equity. Error 2: discounting CF to firm at cost of equity understates equity. Error 3: discounting CF to firm at cost of equity AND forgetting to subtract debt overstates equity even more.
**Example:** Error 1: PV of Equity = 50/1.0994 + 60/1.0994^2 + 68/1.0994^3 + 76.2/1.0994^4 + (83.49+1603)/1.0994^5 = $1,248 → equity overstated by $175. Error 2: PV of Firm = 90/1.13625 + 100/1.13625^2 + 108/1.13625^3 + 116.2/1.13625^4 + (123.49+2363)/1.13625^5 = $1,613. PV of Equity = $1,612.86 − $800 = $813 → equity understated by $260. Error 3: Value of Equity = $1,613 → overstated by $540.

## p.14 — Discounted Cash Flow Valuation: The Steps
**Teaches:** Five steps of DCF valuation. (1) Estimate the discount rate(s): a cost of equity for equity valuation, or a cost of capital for firm valuation. The rate is nominal or real depending on whether cash flows are nominal or real, and it can vary across time. (2) Estimate current earnings and cash flows, to either equity investors (CF to Equity) or all claimholders (CF to Firm). (3) Estimate future earnings and cash flows, generally via an expected growth rate in earnings. (4) Estimate when the firm will reach "stable growth" and what risk and cash-flow characteristics it will have then. (5) Choose the right DCF model for the asset and value it.
**Rules:** Match discount rate type to cash-flow type (equity vs firm; nominal vs real).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (process framework).
**Tags:** dcf-steps, valuation-process, stable-growth

## p.15 — Generic DCF Valuation Model
**Teaches:** Diagram of the generic DCF model: cash flows (firm: pre-debt cash flow; equity: after-debt cash flows) grow during a high-growth period (expected growth: firm = growth in operating earnings; equity = growth in net income/EPS), CF_1 ... CF_n, then the firm reaches stable growth — growing at a constant rate forever — captured in a terminal value. Discounting at the matching rate (firm: cost of capital; equity: cost of equity) gives value of firm or value of equity respectively.

## p.17 — Start easy: The Dividend Discount Model
**Teaches:** DDM structure diagram: Dividends = Net Income × Payout Ratio; Expected dividends = Expected net income × (1 − Retention ratio), where the retention ratio is what is needed to sustain the expected growth in net income. Value of equity = PV of dividends during the high-growth period plus PV of the stable-growth terminal value, discounted at the cost of equity (the rate of return demanded by equity investors). Stable growth is when net income and dividends grow at a constant rate forever.
**Formulas:** Dividends = Net Income × Payout Ratio; Expected dividends = Expected Net Income × (1 − Retention Ratio).

## p.18 — The "potential dividends" or FCFE model
**Teaches:** FCFE (free cash flow to equity) replaces dividends with potential dividends. Value of equity in non-cash assets = PV of FCFE during high growth plus stable-growth terminal value, discounted at the cost of equity; add cash to get total value of equity. Expected FCFE = Expected net income × (1 − Equity Reinvestment rate), where equity reinvestment is what is needed to sustain growth. Stable growth is when net income and FCFE grow at a constant rate forever.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Non-cash Net Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash Working Capital − (Debt repaid − Debt issued). Expected FCFE = Expected Net Income × (1 − Equity Reinvestment Rate). Value of equity = Value of equity in non-cash assets + Cash.
**Compute:** MIXED — FCFE from accounting inputs and PV are DETERMINISTIC; growth and reinvestment estimation is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** fcfe, potential-dividends, equity-reinvestment, cost-of-equity

## p.19 — Valuing the entire business: The FCFF model
**Teaches:** FCFF (free cash flow to firm) model: value of operating assets = PV of FCFF during high growth plus stable-growth terminal value, discounted at the cost of capital (weighted average of costs of equity and debt). Then: Value of operating assets + Cash & non-operating assets − Debt = Value of equity. Expected FCFF = Expected operating income × (1 − Reinvestment rate). Stable growth is when operating income and FCFF grow at a constant rate forever.
**Formulas:** FCFF = After-tax Operating Income − (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − Change in non-cash Working Capital. Expected FCFF = Expected Operating Income × (1 − Reinvestment Rate). Value of equity = Value of operating assets + Cash & non-operating assets − Debt.
**Compute:** MIXED — FCFF computation and PV DETERMINISTIC given inputs; growth/reinvestment estimation JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** fcff, firm-valuation, reinvestment-rate, cost-of-capital

## p.21 — Estimating Inputs: Discount Rates
**Teaches:** Discount rates matter in DCF but not as much as most analysts think. The discount rate must be consistent with both the riskiness and the TYPE of cash flow being discounted, along three dimensions. (1) Equity vs Firm: cash flows to equity are discounted at the cost of equity. Cash flows to the firm are discounted at the cost of capital. (2) Currency: the currency of the cash flows must match the currency of the discount rate. (3) Nominal vs Real: nominal cash flows (reflecting expected inflation) require a nominal discount rate.
**Rules:** Match discount rate to cash flow on claim type (equity/firm), currency, and nominal/real basis.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** discount-rates, consistency, currency, nominal-vs-real

## p.22 — Risk in the DCF Model
**Teaches:** In DCF, value = Expected Cash Flows / Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate. The numerator is the expectation of cash flows across all scenarios, good and bad, incorporating all risks affecting the asset/business. The discount rate should reflect the risk perceived by the MARGINAL investor in the company.
**Formulas:** Risk-Adjusted Cost of Equity = Riskfree Rate in the currency of analysis + Relative risk of the company/equity in question × Equity Risk Premium required for average-risk equity.
**Compute:** MIXED — the formula is DETERMINISTIC given riskfree rate, relative risk (beta) and ERP; the inputs require JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** cost-of-equity, marginal-investor, equity-risk-premium, risk

## p.23 — Not all risk is created equal
**Teaches:** Three ways to classify uncertainty. (1) Estimation vs Economic: estimation uncertainty is having the wrong model or wrong inputs; economic uncertainty is that markets/economies change over time and even the best models fail to capture unexpected changes. (2) Micro vs Macro: micro uncertainty concerns a firm's product market, competition, and management quality; macro uncertainty is exposure to the macroeconomic environment. (3) Discrete vs Continuous: discrete risks lie dormant and show up at points in time — e.g., a drug failing an FDA approval stage, or nationalization of a company in Venezuela. Continuous risks (interest rates, economic growth) change continuously and affect value as they happen.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (taxonomy).
**Tags:** risk-taxonomy, estimation-risk, macro-risk, discrete-risk

## p.24 — Risk and Cost of Equity: The role of the marginal investor
**Teaches:** Not all risk counts in the cost of equity. Risk is usually defined as variance of actual returns around expected returns, but risk-and-return models in finance assume the risk that should be rewarded (built into the discount rate) is the risk perceived by the marginal investor. Since most models assume the marginal investor is well diversified, the only risk priced is risk that cannot be diversified away (market/non-diversifiable risk). In effect, primarily economic, macro, continuous risk should be incorporated into the cost of equity; the other categories (estimation, micro, discrete) belong in expected cash flows or are diversified away.
**Rules:** Only non-diversifiable (market) risk — economic, macro, continuous — goes into the cost of equity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** marginal-investor, diversification, market-risk, cost-of-equity

## p.25 — The Cost of Equity: Competing "Market Risk" Models
**Teaches:** Four competing models for expected return / cost of equity and the inputs each requires.
**Formulas:** CAPM: E(R) = R_f + beta × (R_m − R_f), where R_f = riskfree rate, beta = beta relative to market portfolio, (R_m − R_f) = market risk premium. APM: E(R) = R_f + sum over j of beta_j × (R_j − R_f) — betas relative to each (statistical) factor and factor risk premiums. Multi-factor: E(R) = R_f + sum over j of beta_j × (R_j − R_f) with specified macroeconomic factors, betas relative to macro factors, and macro risk premiums. Proxy models: E(R) = a + sum of b_j × Y_j, where Y_j are proxy variables (e.g., size, book-to-market) and b_j are regression coefficients.
**Data:**
| Model | Expected Return | Inputs Needed |
|---|---|---|
| CAPM | E(R) = Rf + beta (Rm − Rf) | Riskfree rate; beta relative to market portfolio; market risk premium |
| APM | E(R) = Rf + sum beta_j (Rj − Rf) | Riskfree rate; number of factors; betas relative to each factor; factor risk premiums |
| Multi-factor | E(R) = Rf + sum beta_j (Rj − Rf) | Riskfree rate; macro factors; betas relative to macro factors; macroeconomic risk premiums |
| Proxy | E(R) = a + sum b_j Y_j | Proxies; regression coefficients |
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given estimated inputs (rates, betas, premiums → expected return); input estimation is JUDGMENT.

## p.28 — The Risk Free Rate: Laying the Foundations
**Teaches:** On a riskfree investment the actual return equals the expected return — there is no variance around the expected return. For an investment to be riskfree it must have (a) no default risk and (b) no reinvestment risk. Consequences: (1) Time horizon matters — the riskfree rate depends on when the cash flow is expected to occur and varies across time. (2) Currencies matter — a riskfree rate is currency-specific and can differ greatly across currencies. (3) Not all government securities are riskfree — some governments face default risk and their bond rates are not riskfree.
**Rules:** Riskfree = no default risk AND no reinvestment risk; match the riskfree rate's horizon and currency to the cash flows; do not automatically treat government bond yields as riskfree.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, default-risk, reinvestment-risk, currency

## p.32 — Sovereign Default Spread: Three paths to the same destination
**Teaches:** Three ways to estimate a sovereign default spread. (1) Sovereign dollar/euro-denominated bonds: find US-dollar-denominated bonds issued by the emerging sovereign; Default spread = Emerging government bond rate (in US$) − US Treasury bond rate of the same maturity. (2) CDS spreads: use the traded sovereign Credit Default Swap spread (perhaps adjusted for CDS market frictions, e.g. netting out the US CDS spread). (3) Sovereign-rating-based spread: for countries without dollar bonds or CDS, use the average spread of other countries with the same sovereign rating.
**Formulas:** Default spread = Emerging Govt Bond Rate in US$ − US Treasury Bond rate (same maturity); Default spread = Sovereign CDS spread (with adjustment for market frictions).
**Rules:** Prefer $-bond spread or CDS spread when available; fall back to rating-based average spread otherwise.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given market quotes/rating tables; choosing among the three is JUDGMENT.

## p.37 — Getting to a risk free rate in a currency: Brazil example
**Teaches:** Worked example applying all three default-spread approaches to derive a nominal Brazilian Real riskfree rate from the 6.77% government bond rate (January 1, 2020). Each approach gives a somewhat different answer.
**Example:** Brazil, Jan 1 2020, R$ government bond rate = 6.77%. Approach 1 (Govt $ bond spread): 2028 Brazil US$-denominated bond spread over US Treasury = 1.71% → Riskfree rate in R$ = 6.77% − 1.71% = 5.06%. Approach 2 (CDS spread): Brazil CDS net of US CDS = 1.56% → Riskfree rate = 6.77% − 1.56% = 5.21%. Approach 3 (rating-based): Brazil Moody's local currency rating Ba2 → default spread 2.51% → Riskfree rate = 6.77% − 2.51% = 4.26%.
**Formulas:** Riskfree rate in local currency = Local-currency government bond rate − sovereign default spread (from $-bond, CDS, or rating).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (bond rate, chosen spread → riskfree rate); choice of approach is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, brazil, sovereign-default-spread, worked-example

## p.39 — No default free entity: Choices with riskfree rates
**Teaches:** When no default-free entity exists in a currency, you have three options. Option 1: estimate the local riskfree rate. Do this either by subtracting the government's local-currency default spread from the local government bond rate, or by using forward exchange rates plus the riskless rate in an index currency (Euros or dollars) to back out the local riskless rate. Option 2: do the analysis in real terms using a real riskfree rate. Get that rate from an inflation-indexed government bond if one exists, or set it approximately equal to the long-term real growth rate of the economy where the valuation is done. Option 3: do the analysis in a currency where a riskfree rate is available, e.g. US dollars or Euros.
**Formulas:** Local riskfree rate = Government bond rate in local currency − Default spread for the government in local currency. Real riskfree rate ≈ long-term real growth rate of the economy (approximation when no indexed bond exists).
**Rules:** If no default-free rate exists: subtract default spread, use forward rates, switch to real terms, or switch valuation currency.
**Compute:** MIXED — subtraction/forward-rate arithmetic DETERMINISTIC; choice of route and growth-rate approximation JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, emerging-markets, real-terms, currency-choice

## p.40 — Why risk free rates vary across currencies (January 2020 chart)
**Teaches:** Bar chart of January 2020 riskfree rates by currency (government-bond-based estimate), decomposing each local-currency government bond rate into a rating-based default spread and the resulting riskfree rate. Riskfree rates vary enormously across currencies, primarily because of differences in expected inflation. After spread removal, rates are negative at the low end (Croatian Kuna, Bulgarian Lev, Swiss Franc, Japanese Yen, Euro, Danish Krone). They are very high at the top end (Turkish Lira, Kenyan Shilling, Zambian Kwacha, above 10–25%). Higher-rate currencies also carry larger default spreads. Currencies are ordered from lowest to highest total rate; e.g., US$ riskfree ≈ 1.92% (no spread), Zambian Kwacha 32.5% total with ~7.5% spread.
**Rules:** Differences in riskfree rates across currencies are driven mainly by expected inflation differences; strip the default spread from the government bond rate to get the riskfree component.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC per currency (bond rate − rating-based spread → riskfree rate); interpretation JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** riskfree-rate, currencies, inflation, default-spread
