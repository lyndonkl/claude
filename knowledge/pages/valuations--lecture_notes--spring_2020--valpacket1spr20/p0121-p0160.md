## p.122 — Dealing with Operating Lease Expenses

**Teaches:** Accounting treats operating lease expenses as operating expenses. For valuation, they should be treated as financing expenses instead. The fix has three parts. Turn the future lease payments into a debt value. Create a matching leased asset of exactly the same value. Then adjust operating earnings: add back the lease expense and subtract depreciation on the leased asset. A quicker shortcut adds back only the implied interest (pre-tax cost of debt times the PV of leases).
**Formulas:**
- Debt Value of Operating Leases = Present value of future operating lease payments, discounted at the pre-tax cost of debt.
- Adjusted Operating Earnings = Operating Earnings + Operating Lease Expenses − Depreciation on Leased Asset.
- Approximation: Adjusted Operating Earnings = Operating Earnings + (Pre-tax cost of Debt × PV of Operating Leases).
**Rules:** When converting operating leases to debt, always create a counter-asset of exactly the same value as the lease debt.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (lease payments + pre-tax cost of debt → lease debt value and adjusted operating earnings).
**Tags:** operating-leases, lease-capitalization, adjusted-earnings, debt

## p.124 — The Collateral Effects of Treating Operating Leases as Debt

**Teaches:** A two-column view of The Gap under standard accounting and with operating leases treated as debt. - Standard books: Return on capital = 1012 × (1 − 0.35) / (3130 + 1970) = 12.90% (book equity 3130, tax rate 35%).
- With leases: Return on capital = 1362 × (1 − 0.35) / (3130 + 6367) = 9.30%.

## p.127 — R&D Expenses: Operating or Capital Expenses

**Teaches:** Accounting standards force firms to expense R&D as an operating cost. Yet R&D exists to create future growth, so it is more logical to treat it as a capital expenditure. The process has three steps. First, pick an amortizable life for R&D (2–10 years, based on how long research takes to pay off in the industry). Second, collect past R&D expenses going back as many years as that life. Third, build the research asset as the sum of unamortized portions. With a 5-year life and straight-line amortization: research asset = (1/5) of R&D from 5 years ago + (2/5) of R&D from 4 years ago + … + all of the current year's R&D.
**Rules:** Amortizable life for R&D should be chosen between 2 and 10 years; collect past R&D for exactly that many years.
**Formulas:** Research asset = sum over past years of R&D_t × (unamortized fraction), where with straight-line amortization over life L the R&D from k years ago carries fraction (L − k)/L unamortized.
**Compute:** MIXED — choosing the amortizable life is JUDGMENT; given the life and the R&D history, building the research asset is DETERMINISTIC.
**Tags:** r-and-d, capitalization, research-asset, amortization

## p.130 — III. One-Time and Non-recurring Charges (concept question)

**Teaches:** What earnings should you use when a firm reports a $500m loss caused by a $1 billion one-time charge? The loss of $500m, or the profit of $500m (earnings with the charge removed)? Implied answer: strip truly one-time charges and use the $500m profit. But if such "one-time" losses come back (e.g., once every five years), they are not really non-recurring. In that case an annualized portion of the charge should stay in earnings.
**Rules:** Remove genuinely one-time, non-recurring charges from earnings before valuing; if the charges keep coming back, treat them as recurring (spread them over the years instead of removing them).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (deciding whether charges are recurring or non-recurring).

## p.131 — IV. Accounting Malfeasance

**Teaches:** Even under the same accounting standards, firms differ in how they apply them. Aggressive firms show higher earnings than careful ones. You cannot catch outright fraud. But you should look for warning signals in financial statements and correct for them.
**Rules:** Six warning signals to check for:
1. Income from unnamed sources — hidden holdings in other businesses or special purpose entities.
2. Income from asset sales or financial transactions at a non-financial firm.
3. Sudden changes in standard expense items — e.g., a big drop in SG&A or R&D as a percent of revenues.
4. Frequent accounting restatements.
5. Accrual earnings that consistently run ahead of cash earnings.
6. Big differences between tax income and reported income.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative red-flag screening).
**Tags:** accounting-quality, earnings-management, red-flags

## p.132 — V. Framework for Negative or Abnormally Low Earnings

**Teaches:** Decision-tree framework for valuing companies with negative or abnormally low earnings. The path depends on WHY earnings are depressed. There are five causes: temporary problems; cyclicality (e.g., auto firm in a recession); life-cycle reasons (young firms, infrastructure firms); leverage problems (otherwise healthy firm with too much debt); long-term operating problems (structural production or cost problems).
**Rules:**
- If the cause is temporary or cyclical → normalize earnings. If the firm's size has NOT changed significantly over time: use average dollar earnings over time. That means average net income if valuing equity, average EBIT if valuing the firm. If the firm's size HAS changed: apply the firm's average ROE to current book value of equity (when valuing equity), or average ROC to current book value of capital (when valuing the firm).
- If the cause is life-cycle, leverage, or long-term operating problems → value the firm with detailed cash flow forecasts. Start from revenues and reduce or eliminate the problem over time.
  - Structural problem → target the operating margins of stable firms in the sector.
  - Leverage problem → target a debt ratio the firm will be comfortable with by end of period; that could be its own optimal or the industry average.
  - Operating problem → target an industry-average operating margin.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (finding the cause and choosing how to normalize); the arithmetic itself is deterministic once chosen.
**Tags:** negative-earnings, normalization, framework, distressed-firms

## p.135 — The Right Tax Rate to Use

**Teaches:** The real choice is between the effective and the marginal tax rate. For projections it is far safer to use the marginal tax rate. The effective rate mostly reflects gaps between accounting books and tax books, and those gaps are unreliable to project. The marginal rate tends to understate after-tax operating income in early years, but it is more accurate in later years. If you start with the effective tax rate, adjust it toward the marginal rate over time. A weighted average marginal rate across countries can be argued for. Still, it is safest to use the marginal tax rate of the home country.
**Rules:** Use the marginal tax rate for projections; if starting from the effective rate, converge it to the marginal rate over the forecast period; prefer the country's marginal rate over a multi-country weighted average.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (rate choice), DETERMINISTIC once the rate path is set.

## p.137 — Net Capital Expenditures

**Teaches:** Net capital expenditures = capital expenditures minus depreciation; depreciation is a cash inflow that funds some, most, or all of cap ex. Net cap ex is a function of how fast the firm is growing or expects to grow — high-growth firms have much higher net cap ex than low-growth firms. Therefore assumptions about net cap ex can never be made independently of assumptions about future growth.
**Formulas:** Net Capital Expenditures = Capital Expenditures − Depreciation.
**Rules:** Link net cap ex assumptions to growth assumptions; never set them independently.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cap ex, depreciation → net cap ex); linking to growth is JUDGMENT.

## p.141 — Working Capital Investments

**Teaches:** Accounting working capital = current assets minus current liabilities. Current assets are inventory, cash, and accounts receivable. Current liabilities are accounts payable, short-term debt, and debt due within the year. For cash flow and valuation work, a cleaner measure is non-cash working capital. Take non-cash current assets (inventory and accounts receivable) minus non-debt current liabilities (accounts payable). In other words, leave cash out of assets and debt out of liabilities. For firms in some sectors, working capital investment is the bigger part of reinvestment.
**Formulas:**
- Accounting working capital = Current assets − Current liabilities.
- Non-cash working capital (valuation definition) = (Inventory + Accounts receivable) − Accounts payable, i.e., non-cash current assets − non-debt current liabilities.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (balance sheet items → non-cash working capital).
**Tags:** working-capital, reinvestment, non-cash-wc

## p.142 — Working Capital: General Propositions

**Teaches:** Three propositions for forecasting working capital. (1) Detail: breaking working capital into pieces (inventory, deferred taxes, payables) is pointless unless you have specific information that lets you forecast the details. (2) Volatility: year-to-year changes in non-cash working capital jump around, so building off the most recent year's change is dangerous — better to estimate the change from working capital as a percent of sales while checking against industry averages. (3) Negative working capital: some firms have negative non-cash working capital; assuming it continues means reinvestment becomes a source of positive cash flow that grows with growth — a strong assumption to be wary of.
**Rules:** Forecast working capital as a percent of sales rather than carrying forward the latest year's change; check against industry averages; be careful about assuming negative working capital lasts forever.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of WC ratio), DETERMINISTIC once the ratio is set (WC% × revenue change → WC investment).
**Tags:** working-capital, forecasting, negative-working-capital

## p.145 — Dividends and Cash Flows to Equity

**Teaches:** Strictly, the only cash flow an equity investor in a public firm receives is the dividend. But actual dividends are set by managers. They may be much lower than potential dividends (what could have been paid out). Managers are careful and smooth dividends, and they hold cash for surprises and investment chances. When actual dividends are less than potential dividends, a dividend-only model will understate the true value of equity.
**Rules:** If actual dividends < potential dividends, a dividend discount model understates equity value — use potential dividends (FCFE) instead.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** dividends, fcfe, equity-valuation

## p.146 — Measuring Potential Dividends

**Teaches:** Earnings do not equal potential dividends, for three reasons.
- Earnings are not cash flows; they include non-cash revenues and expenses.
- Even if they were cash flows, a firm that paid out all earnings would invest nothing and could not grow.
- Discounting earnings as if they were dividends overestimates equity value.
Potential dividends are the cash flows left over after two subtractions: the investments the firm needs to create future growth, and net debt repayments (debt repayments minus new debt issues). The common split of cap ex into discretionary and non-discretionary loses its basis once future growth is built into the valuation. Growth-creating cap ex is required to earn that growth.
**Formulas:** Net debt repayments = Debt repayments − New debt issues.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (conceptual).
**Tags:** potential-dividends, fcfe, earnings-vs-cash-flows

## p.147 — Estimating Cash Flows: FCFE

**Teaches:** The definition of free cash flow to equity for a levered firm. Start from net income. Subtract net capital expenditures and the change in non-cash working capital. Then subtract net principal repayments. Preferred dividends, if any, must also be netted out.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income − (Capital Expenditures − Depreciation) − Changes in non-cash Working Capital − (Principal Repayments − New Debt Issues). If preferred stock exists: subtract preferred dividends as well.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, cap ex, depreciation, ΔWC, debt flows → FCFE).
**Tags:** fcfe, cash-flow-estimation, equity-valuation

## p.148 — Estimating FCFE when Leverage is Stable

**Teaches:** When the debt ratio is stable, debt flows can be modeled as financing a constant fraction DR of reinvestment: FCFE = Net Income − (1 − DR) × (Cap Ex − Depreciation) − (1 − DR) × Working Capital Needs, where DR = debt-to-capital ratio. This is equivalent to assuming proceeds from new debt issues = principal repayments + DR × (net cap ex + working capital needs). In computing FCFE this way, use the book value debt-to-capital ratio when looking back in time, but the market value debt-to-capital ratio when looking forward.
**Formulas:** FCFE = Net Income − (1 − DR)(Capital Expenditures − Depreciation) − (1 − DR)(Working Capital Needs), where DR = Debt/Capital ratio. Implied: New debt issues = Principal Repayments + DR × (Cap Ex − Depreciation + Working Capital Needs).
**Rules:** Backward-looking FCFE: use book-value debt-to-capital; forward-looking FCFE: use market-value debt-to-capital.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, net cap ex, ΔWC, DR → FCFE).
**Tags:** fcfe, stable-leverage, debt-ratio

## p.149 — FCFE from the Statement of Cash Flows

**Teaches:** FCFE can be backed out of the statement of cash flows if you work through it with care rather than trusting it fully. Primary route: FCFE = Cash flow from operations − Capital expenditures (from investing section) − Cash acquisitions (from investing section) − (Debt repaid − Debt issued) (from financing section). Cross-check route: FCFE should also equal Dividends + Stock buybacks − Stock issuances + Change in cash balance (i.e., what was actually returned to equity holders plus cash accumulated).
**Formulas:**
- FCFE = Cash flow from Operations − Capital Expenditures − Cash Acquisitions − (Debt Repaid − Debt Issued).
- Alternatively: FCFE = Dividends + Stock Buybacks − Stock Issuances + Change in Cash Balance.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (statement-of-cash-flows line items → FCFE).
**Tags:** fcfe, statement-of-cash-flows, cross-check

## p.151 — Estimating FCFE: Disney (worked example)

**Teaches:** A worked example of the stable-leverage FCFE formula for Disney (1997). FCFE of $704 million was about double the $345 million actually paid in dividends. Disney could have paid out far more than it did.
**Example:** Disney 1997: Net income = $1,533m; capital spending = $1,746m; depreciation = $1,134m; increase in non-cash working capital = $477m; debt-to-capital ratio DR = 23.83%. Dividends actually paid = $345 million.

## p.156 — The Value of Growth

**Teaches:** It is easy to treat growth as a pure good, where higher growth always means higher value. But growth cuts both ways. The net effect on value is whether the good outweighs the bad. Growth can be good, bad, or value-neutral.

## p.159 — Historical Growth

**Teaches:** Historical growth rates can be measured in different ways: arithmetic vs geometric averages, and simple vs regression models. The results are sensitive to the time period used (starting and ending points). They are also sensitive to the metric growth is measured in (revenues vs EBITDA vs EBIT vs EPS). Using historical growth also forces you to wrestle with two problems. One is negative earnings: growth rates are meaningless off a negative base. The other is scaling up: large firms cannot sustain the percentage growth they achieved when small.
**Rules:** Be aware that historical growth depends on: averaging method (arithmetic vs geometric), model (simple vs regression), period endpoints, and metric chosen; handle negative earnings and scaling effects head-on.
**Compute:** MIXED — computing averages is DETERMINISTIC; choosing the period and metric, and handling negatives, is JUDGMENT.

## p.160 — Motorola: Arithmetic vs Geometric Growth Rates

**Teaches:** A data table of Motorola's 1994–1999 revenues, EBITDA, and EBIT with yearly growth. Arithmetic averages come out above geometric averages.
