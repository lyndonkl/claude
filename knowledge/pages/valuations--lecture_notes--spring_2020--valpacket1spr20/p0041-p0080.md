## p.41 — Risk free rate when you don't have/trust the government bond rate

**Teaches:** When a currency has no reliable (default-free) government bond, you can still estimate a risk free rate in that currency two ways. (1) Build-up approach: the risk free rate in any currency is the sum of expected inflation in that currency plus the expected real interest rate (proxied e.g. by the US TIPS rate). (2) Differential-inflation approach: scale up the US dollar risk free rate by the ratio of inflation in the foreign currency to inflation in US dollars.

**Formulas:**
- Risk free rate = Expected inflation in the currency + Expected real interest rate.
- Risk free rate_Currency = (1 + Riskfree rate_US$) * (1 + Expected Inflation_ForeignCurrency) / (1 + Expected Inflation_US$) − 1, where Riskfree rate_US$ is the US dollar risk free rate and the inflation terms are expected inflation rates in the foreign currency and in US dollars.

**Example:** Build-up: expected inflation 15%, TIPS rate 1% → risk free rate = 16%. Differential inflation: US$ risk free rate 2.00%, foreign inflation 15%, US inflation 1.5% → (1.02)*(1.15)/(1.015) − 1 = 15.57%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: US$ risk free rate, expected inflation rates or real rate → output: local-currency risk free rate); choosing the inflation estimates is JUDGMENT.

## p.47 — Perils of trusting the past: noise and survivorship bias

**Teaches:** Two problems with historical risk premiums. (1) Noisy estimates: even ~90 years of data gives a large standard error; with a 20% annual standard deviation of stock returns and 90 years, the standard error of the premium exceeds 2%. (2) Survivorship bias: the US was among the most successful equity markets of the 20th century, so US historical data overstates the premium an investor could have expected ex ante.

**Formulas:** Standard error in premium = Annualized standard deviation of stock returns / sqrt(Number of years of data). Example: 20% / sqrt(90) = 2.1%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (std dev, years → standard error); the bias argument is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** equity-risk-premium, standard-error, survivorship-bias, estimation-risk

## p.49 — Country risk premium via country default spread

**Teaches:** The simplest way to add a country risk premium (CRP) is to set it equal to the country's default spread. That spread can be estimated three ways. (1) The spread on a US-dollar-denominated sovereign bond of the country (Brazil $ bond, Jan 2020: 1.71%). (2) The sovereign CDS spread adjusted for the US CDS (Brazil 10-yr, Jan 2020: 1.56%). (3) A default spread looked up from the country's local-currency sovereign rating (Brazil Ba2 → about 2.51% in Jan 2020). The CRP is then added to a mature-market premium to get the country's total ERP.

**Formulas:** Total ERP_country = Mature market premium + Country default spread (CRP).

**Rules:** Three acceptable proxies for the country default spread: US$ sovereign bond spread, sovereign CDS spread (net of US CDS), or rating-based default spread.

**Example:** Brazil, January 2020: mature market premium 5.20%, rating-based CRP 2.51% → Total ERP for Brazil = 5.20% + 2.51% = 7.71%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (mature premium + default spread → total ERP); choice among the three spread measures is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, default-spread, sovereign-rating, cds, brazil

## p.50 — Equity-volatility approach to country total ERP

**Teaches:** An alternative to the default-spread approach: scale the US risk premium by the relative volatility of the emerging market's equity index to the US equity index. The country's total ERP rises with how much more volatile its equity market is than the US market; the CRP is the excess over the US premium.

**Formulas:** Total equity risk premium = Risk Premium_US * (sigma_CountryEquity / sigma_USEquity), where sigma_CountryEquity is the standard deviation of the emerging market equity index and sigma_USEquity is the standard deviation of the US equity index (S&P 500). Country ERP = Total ERP − US ERP.

**Example:** US ERP 5.20%, Bovespa (Brazil) std dev 30%, S&P 500 std dev 18% → Total ERP Brazil = 5.20% * (30%/18%) = 8.67%; Country ERP Brazil = 8.67% − 5.20% = 3.47%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (US ERP, two equity std devs → total ERP and CRP).

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, relative-volatility, equity-risk-premium, brazil

## p.51 — Melded approach: default spread scaled by equity/bond volatility

**Teaches:** Country ratings measure default risk, but equity is riskier than debt, so equity spreads should exceed debt spreads. The melded approach multiplies the country bond default spread by the relative volatility of the country's equities versus its government bonds, producing a CRP larger than the raw default spread.

**Formulas:** Country equity risk premium = Default spread on country bond * (sigma_CountryEquity / sigma_CountryBond), where sigma_CountryEquity is the std dev of the country's equity market and sigma_CountryBond is the std dev of the country's government bond. Total ERP = Mature market premium + CRP.

**Example:** Brazil, January 2020: default spread 2.51%, Bovespa std dev 30%, Brazil government bond std dev 20% → CRP = 2.51% * (30%/20%) = 3.77%; Total ERP = 5.20% + 3.77% = 8.97%.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (default spread, equity std dev, bond std dev, mature premium → CRP and total ERP).

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, melded-approach, default-spread, relative-volatility, brazil

## p.52 — Template: ERP estimation procedure (January 1, 2020)

**Teaches:** Damodaran's four-step template for estimating any country's ERP. Step 1: estimate the mature market premium as the implied ERP for the S&P 500 (5.20% on Jan 1, 2020; updated monthly). Step 2: get the country's Moody's sovereign local-currency rating (or convert S&P's rating to Moody's equivalent). Step 3: convert the country risk measure into a default spread using (a) US$ sovereign bond spread, (b) CDS spread, or (c) a ratings table; if there is no sovereign rating, use a PRS (Political Risk Services) composite country risk score. Step 4: ERP for country = US ERP + Default spread * Relative Equity Market Volatility, where relative volatility = std dev of emerging-market equity index / std dev of emerging-market bond index (1.18 in January 2020). Country steps updated every six months (January and July).

**Rules:**
- If sovereign rating is AAA → ERP for country = US ERP (CRP = 0).
- If rating below AAA → ERP = US ERP + Default spread * Relative Equity Market Volatility (1.18 in Jan 2020).
- If no sovereign rating → estimate ERP from PRS country risk score.
- Mature market (implied S&P 500) premium updated monthly; country spreads every six months.

**Compute:** MIXED — spread-to-ERP conversion is DETERMINISTIC (rating/spread, relative volatility multiplier → ERP); rating conversion and PRS-based estimates involve JUDGMENT.

## p.54 — From country ERP to corporate ERP: three approaches

**Teaches:** Three ways to build a company's cost of equity from a country risk premium. Approach 1 assumes every company in the country is equally exposed to country risk (CRP added as a constant). Approach 2 assumes exposure to country risk scales with beta, like other market risk. Approach 3 treats country risk as a separate risk factor with its own exposure measure lambda (e.g. based on the share of non-domestic revenues), allowing firms to differ in country-risk exposure independently of beta.

**Formulas:**
- Approach 1: E(Return) = Riskfree Rate + CRP + Beta * (Mature ERP)
- Approach 2: E(Return) = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Mature ERP + CRP)
- Approach 3: E(Return) = Riskfree Rate + Beta * (Mature ERP) + lambda * (CRP)
where Mature ERP = mature-market equity risk premium, CRP = additional country risk premium, Beta = market beta, lambda = firm-specific exposure to country risk.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC once inputs chosen (riskfree, beta, ERP, CRP, lambda → cost of equity); choosing the approach and lambda is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** cost-of-equity, country-risk-premium, capm, lambda

## p.55 — Location-based vs operation-based CRP

**Teaches:** How to decide which country's CRP a company bears. Location-based CRP (the standard practice): assign the CRP of the country of incorporation — an Indian company gets India's CRP, a developed-market company gets none. Operation-based CRP (more reasonable): compute the company's CRP as a weighted average of the CRPs of the countries where it does business, weighting by revenues or operating income; for firms in dozens of countries, weight by region.

**Rules:** Prefer operation-based (revenue/operating-income-weighted) CRP over location-of-incorporation CRP; risk exposure comes from where business is done, not where the firm is incorporated.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (revenue weights x country CRPs → weighted CRP); choosing weights basis is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** country-risk-premium, operation-based, revenue-weights, multinationals

## p.61 — A revenue-based lambda

**Teaches:** A simple estimate of lambda (relative exposure of a firm to country risk): the firm's share of domestic revenues divided by the average firm's share of domestic revenues. Two implications: risk exposure is determined by where a company does business, not where it is incorporated; and firms can actively manage their country risk exposures.

**Formulas:** lambda = (% of revenues earned domestically by the firm) / (% of revenues earned domestically by the average firm in that market).

**Example:** 2008-09 Indian firms, average Indian firm gets ~80% of revenues in India: Tata Motors got 91.37% of revenues in India → lambda = 91%/80% = 1.14; TCS got 7.62% in India → lambda = 7.62%/80% = 0.09.

## p.65 — Implied equity premiums: the concept

**Teaches:** If you know the price paid for an asset and the expected cash flows, the IRR of those cash flows is your expected return. Applied to the whole stock market: assuming stocks are correctly priced in aggregate, solve for the discount rate that sets the present value of expected aggregate cash flows equal to the current index level — that is the expected return on stocks. Subtracting the riskfree rate gives the implied equity risk premium, a forward-looking number that can be updated as often as you like (even minute by minute).

**Formulas:** Implied ERP = IRR that equates PV of expected market cash flows to current index level, minus the riskfree rate.

**Compute:** MIXED — solving for the IRR is DETERMINISTIC given cash flow forecasts; the cash flow/growth forecasts are JUDGMENT.

## p.66 — Implied equity premium: January 2008 computation

## p.74 — Why implied premiums matter (quiz)

**Teaches:** Quiz slide. Many investment banks use historical (often arithmetic-average) premiums. If analysts used the 1928-2019 arithmetic average premium over T-bills of 8.18% to value stocks in January 2019 when the implied premium was 5.20%, their discount rates would be too high, so (a) the values they obtain will be too low and most stocks will look overvalued. Conversely, using the historical geometric average of 4.83% (below the implied 5.20%) would make values too high and stocks look cheap. The premium you choose builds a systematic bias into every valuation.

**Rules:** Using an ERP above the implied premium biases all valuations downward (stocks look overvalued); using one below biases them upward (stocks look undervalued).

## p.75 — Which equity risk premium should you use?

**Teaches:** The right ERP depends on your assumption about markets, and predictive evidence favors implied premiums. Correlations of each ERP predictor with subsequent implied premiums and actual returns show the current implied premium is the best predictor; the historical premium is actually negatively correlated with future returns.

**Rules:**
- If you assume premiums revert to historical norms (and your period yields those norms) → use the historical risk premium.
- If you assume the market is correct in the aggregate, or want a market-neutral valuation → use the current implied equity risk premium.

## p.80 — The CAPM regression beta and its three problems

**Teaches:** The standard procedure for estimating beta is to regress the stock's returns against market returns; the slope is the beta and measures the stock's riskiness. But this regression beta has three problems: (1) it has high standard error; (2) it reflects the firm's business mix over the regression period, not its current mix; (3) it reflects the firm's average financial leverage over the period rather than current leverage.

**Formulas:** Rj = a + b * Rm, where Rj = stock returns, Rm = market returns, a = regression intercept, b = regression slope = beta.

**Rules:** Be skeptical of raw regression betas — high standard error, stale business mix, stale leverage.

**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (return series → regression slope beta); assessing its reliability is JUDGMENT.

**Tags:** beta, capm, regression, estimation-problems
