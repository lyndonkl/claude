## p.161 — A Test: growth rate with negative starting EPS (Time Warner)

**Teaches:** A quiz slide asking what growth rate to report when the base-year number is negative. Time Warner's 1996 EPS was a deficit of -$0.05 and expected 1997 EPS is $0.25; answer choices are (a) -600%, (b) +600%, (c) +120%, (d) cannot be estimated. The point (answered on the next slide) is that a percentage growth rate off a negative base is meaningless.
**Example:** Time Warner: EPS 1996 = -$0.05, expected EPS 1997 = $0.25. Naive computation (0.25-(-0.05))/(-0.05) gives -600%, which is nonsensical; the honest answer is "cannot be estimated."
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (recognizing that arithmetic on a negative base yields a meaningless growth rate).
**Tags:** historical-growth, negative-earnings, growth-rates, eps

## p.162 — Dealing with Negative Earnings

**Teaches:** When earnings in the starting period are negative, the conventional growth rate cannot be estimated (e.g. change of 0.30 divided by -0.05 gives -600%, which is wrong in sign and meaning). Three mechanical workarounds exist, but the deeper message is that when earnings are negative the growth rate is meaningless — even if you can compute a number, it tells you little about the future.
**Rules:** Three fixes for a negative base period. (1) Use the higher of the two earnings numbers as the denominator (0.30/0.25 = 120%). (2) Use the absolute value of starting-period earnings as the denominator (0.30/0.05 = 600%). (3) Run a linear regression of earnings over time and divide the slope coefficient by average earnings. Overriding rule: a growth rate computed off negative earnings should not be trusted for forecasting.
**Compute:** MIXED — each workaround is deterministic arithmetic given the two earnings numbers; deciding whether the result means anything is judgment.
**Tags:** negative-earnings, historical-growth, growth-rates, eps

## p.163 — The Effect of Size on Growth: Callaway Golf

**Teaches:** Historical growth rates decline as a firm gets larger: Callaway Golf's net profit growth fell from over 250% a year in the early 1990s to about 25% by 1995-96 as the profit base grew. A single geometric average (102% here) hides this strong downward trend and is a poor forecast of the future.
**Data:**
| Year | Net Profit ($mm) | Growth Rate |
|---|---|---|
| 1990 | 1.80 | — |
| 1991 | 6.40 | 255.56% |
| 1992 | 19.30 | 201.56% |
| 1993 | 41.20 | 113.47% |
| 1994 | 78.00 | 89.32% |
| 1995 | 97.70 | 25.26% |
| 1996 | 122.30 | 25.18% |

Geometric average growth rate 1990-1996 = 102%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (annual growth = Profit_t/Profit_{t-1} - 1; geometric average = (122.30/1.80)^(1/6) - 1 ≈ 102%) — the caution about using it for forecasting is judgment.

## p.164 — Extrapolation and its Dangers

**Teaches:** Continues the Callaway example. Suppose the 1996 net profit of $122.30 million kept growing at the historical rate (geometric average ~102%) for 5 more years. Projected net income would reach $4.113 billion by 2001. That absurd result shows the danger of extrapolating high historical growth for large or maturing firms.
**Data:** Extrapolated net profit ($mm): 1996: 122.30; 1997: 247.05; 1998: 499.03; 1999: 1,008.05; 2000: 2,036.25; 2001: 4,113.23.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (Profit_{t+1} = Profit_t * (1 + g) with g ≈ 102%) — the lesson that this is implausible is judgment.

**Teaches:** Although an analyst's nominal job is finding under- and over-valued stocks, most of their (non-selling) time goes to forecasting EPS — mainly the next earnings report. ## p.167 — How good are analysts at forecasting growth?

**Teaches:** Analyst EPS forecasts tend to beat simple time-series models, but only by small margins. The analyst advantage decreases with the forecast horizon (large for next quarter, small for 5 years), is greater for larger firms than smaller ones, and is greater at the industry level than the company level. Growth forecasts and revisions are highly correlated across analysts (herding).
**Data:**

**Rules:** Trust analyst forecasts more for short horizons, larger firms, and industry-level (rather than company-level) growth.

## p.168 — Are some analysts more equal than others?

**Teaches:** A study of All-America Analysts (chosen by Institutional Investor) found four things. There is no evidence they were chosen for forecasting skill: median forecast error in the quarter before selection was 30%, vs. 28% for other analysts. After being chosen they become slightly better forecasters, with median forecast error 2% lower than other analysts. Their earnings revisions move stock prices much more than other analysts' revisions. Their recommendations also have a bigger price impact (3% on buys, 4.7% on sells), and the drift continues in the following period (a further 2.4% for buys and 13.8% for sells).
**Data:** Median forecast error pre-selection: All-America 30% vs. others 28%. Post-selection: All-America median error 2% lower. Recommendation price impact: buys +3%, sells 4.7%; subsequent-period continuation: buys +2.4%, sells 13.8%.

## p.169 — The Five Deadly Sins of an Analyst

**Teaches:** Damodaran names five behavioral failings of equity analysts. (1) Tunnel Vision: so focused on the sector and intra-sector valuations that they lose the bigger picture. (2) Lemmingitis: the urge to change recommendations and estimates when other analysts do. (3) Stockholm Syndrome: identifying with the managers of the firms they cover. (4) Factophobia (often paired with delusions of being a famous storyteller): basing recommendations on a "story" while refusing to face facts. (5) Dr. Jekyll/Mr. Hyde: analysts who see their primary job as bringing in investment-banking business.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** analyst-estimates, behavioral-biases, analyst-incentives

## p.170 — Propositions about Analyst Growth Rates

**Teaches:** Proposition 1: analyst forecasts contain far less private information and far more public information than generally claimed. Proposition 2: the biggest source of private information for analysts is the company itself. That explains why buy recommendations outnumber sells (information bias and source preservation). It also explains why forecasts and revisions correlate highly across analysts, and why All-America analysts improve after being chosen. Proposition 3: knowing analyst growth forecasts has value, but there is danger both when analysts agree too much (lemmingitis) and too little (their information is too noisy to be useful).

## p.172 — Fundamental Growth Rates (diagram)

**Teaches:** A diagram showing where earnings growth comes from mechanically. Current earnings = investment in existing projects x current return on those projects ($1000 x 12% = $120). Next period's earnings = existing investment x next period's ROI + new investment x ROI on new projects ($1000 x 12% + $100 x 12% = $132). The change in earnings ($12) decomposes into existing investment x change in ROI (here 0%) plus new investment x ROI on new projects ($100 x 12% = $12). Growth therefore comes from new investment and/or improving returns on existing investment.
**Formulas:** Current Earnings = Existing Investment x Current ROI. Next Period Earnings = Existing Investment x Next Period ROI + New Investment x ROI on new projects. Change in Earnings = Existing Investment x (Change in ROI) + New Investment x ROI on new projects. Symbols: Existing Investment = capital in place; New Investment = capital added this period; ROI = return on investment.
**Example:** Existing investment $1000 at 12% → earnings $120. Add $100 of new investment at 12%, ROI unchanged → next earnings $132; change in earnings = $12, all from new investment.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (existing investment, new investment, current and next ROI → earnings levels and change in earnings).
**Tags:** fundamental-growth, reinvestment, roi, earnings-growth

## p.173 — Growth Rate Derivations

**Teaches:** Derives the fundamental growth equation. When ROI on existing projects is unchanged and equals ROI on new projects: (New Investment / Current Earnings) x ROI = Change in Earnings / Current Earnings, i.e. Growth = Reinvestment Rate x Return on Investment. In the example, 100/120 = 83.33% reinvestment rate x 12% = 10% growth. In the general case where ROI can change: Change in Earnings / Current Earnings = [Existing Investment x (Change in ROI) + New Investment x new ROI] / [Existing Investment x Current ROI].
**Formulas:** g = Reinvestment Rate x ROI, where Reinvestment Rate = Investment in New Projects / Current Earnings and ROI = return on investment. General case: g = [Existing Investment x (ROI_new_period - ROI_current) + New Investment x ROI] / (Existing Investment x Current ROI), where Existing Investment = capital already in place and Current ROI = return currently earned on it.
**Example:** Base case: 100/120 = 83.33%; 83.33% x 12% = 10% growth ($12/$120). If ROI rises from 12% to 13%: [$1,000 x (0.13 - 0.12) + $100 x 0.13] / ($1,000 x 0.12) = $23/$120 = 19.17% expected growth.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (existing investment, new investment, current and next-period ROI → expected growth rate).
**Tags:** fundamental-growth, reinvestment-rate, roi, growth-derivation

## p.174 — Estimating Fundamental Growth from New Investments: Three Variations

**Teaches:** The growth = reinvestment x return framework applies at three levels of earnings, each with its own matched reinvestment and return measures. Consistency matters: pair each earnings measure with its own reinvestment and return definitions.
**Formulas / Data:**
| Earnings Measure | Reinvestment Measure | Return Measure |
|---|---|---|
| Earnings per share | Retention Ratio = % of net income retained = 1 - Payout Ratio | Return on Equity = Net Income / Book Value of Equity |
| Net income from non-cash assets | Equity Reinvestment Rate = (Net Cap Ex + Change in non-cash WC - Change in Debt) / Net Income | Non-cash ROE = Net Income from non-cash assets / (Book Value of Equity - Cash) |
| Operating income | Reinvestment Rate = (Net Cap Ex + Change in non-cash WC) / After-tax Operating Income | Return on Capital (ROIC) = After-tax Operating Income / (Book Value of Equity + Book Value of Debt - Cash) |

Symbols: Net Cap Ex = capital expenditures minus depreciation; WC = working capital; Payout Ratio = dividends / net income.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given financial-statement inputs, each reinvestment rate, return measure, and g = reinvestment x return is arithmetic).
**Tags:** fundamental-growth, reinvestment-rate, roe, roic, retention-ratio

## p.175 — I. Expected Long Term Growth in EPS

**Teaches:** For growth in earnings per share, the reinvestment rate is the retention ratio (retained earnings / current earnings) and the return measure is ROE (net income / book value of equity). In the special case where current ROE stays unchanged, expected EPS growth = retention ratio x ROE. Proposition 1: a company's expected long-term earnings growth cannot exceed its return on equity.
**Formulas:** g_EPS = Retained Earnings_{t-1} / NI_{t-1} x ROE = Retention Ratio x ROE = b x ROE, where b = retention ratio (fraction of net income retained = 1 - payout ratio), ROE = Net Income / Book Value of Equity, NI = net income.
**Rules:** Long-term expected earnings growth ≤ ROE (since b ≤ 1).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (retention ratio and ROE → g_EPS).
**Tags:** eps-growth, retention-ratio, roe, fundamental-growth

## p.177 — Regulatory Effects on Expected EPS Growth (Wells Fargo follow-up)

**Teaches:** An in-class exercise: assume the 2008 banking crisis forces banks to hold 30% more book equity for the same business. With net income unchanged and book equity up 30%, ROE falls (new ROE = 17.56%/1.30 ≈ 13.51%), and with the same retention ratio the expected growth rate falls proportionately (≈ 0.4537 x 13.51% ≈ 6.13%). The slide leaves the blanks ("New Return on Equity =", "Expected growth rate =") for students to fill; the mechanism taught is that higher required capital lowers ROE and hence fundamental growth.
**Formulas:** New ROE = Old ROE / (1 + % increase in required book equity); New g = Retention Ratio x New ROE.

## p.178 — One Way to Pump Up ROE: Use More Debt

**Teaches:** ROE can be decomposed into return on capital plus a leverage bonus: as long as the after-tax return on capital exceeds the after-tax cost of debt, adding debt raises ROE (and thus apparent fundamental growth). Book value of capital here is debt plus equity minus cash.
**Formulas:** ROE = ROC + D/E x (ROC - i(1-t)). Here ROC = EBIT_t (1 - tax rate) / Book Value of Capital_{t-1}. D/E = Book Value of Debt / Book Value of Equity. i = Interest Expense on Debt / Book Value of Debt. t = tax rate on ordinary income. Book Value of Capital = Book Value of Debt + Book Value of Equity - Cash.
**Rules:** If ROC > i(1-t), higher leverage raises ROE; if ROC < i(1-t), leverage drags ROE below ROC.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, D/E, i, t → ROE).
**Tags:** roe, leverage, roc, dupont-decomposition

## p.179 — Decomposing ROE: Brahma in 1998

**Teaches:** Brazilian brewer Brahma (now Ambev) had a very high ROE largely because it borrowed at a rate well below its return on capital — leverage amplified ROC of ~20% into ROE of ~31%. The slide asks the discussion question: this looks like an easy way to deliver higher EPS growth — what is the downside? (Higher leverage also raises equity risk and the cost of equity.)
**Example:** Brahma 1998: ROC = 19.91%; D/E = 77%; after-tax cost of debt = 5.61%. ROE = ROC + D/E(ROC - i(1-t)) = 19.91% + 0.77 x (19.91% - 5.61%) = 30.92%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC, D/E, after-tax cost of debt → ROE); assessing the downside is judgment.
**Tags:** roe, leverage, brahma, emerging-markets

## p.181 — II. Expected Growth in Net Income from Non-cash Assets

**Teaches:** The EPS fundamental growth equation assumes retained earnings are reinvested at the ROE, but firms that pile up cash mute this effect. A more general version substitutes actual equity reinvestment in real assets (net cap ex and working capital, net of new debt) and strips cash out of both net income and book equity.
**Formulas:** Net Income from non-cash assets = Net Income - Interest income from cash x (1 - t), where t = tax rate. Equity Reinvestment Rate = (Net Capital Expenditures + Change in Working Capital) x (1 - Debt Ratio) / Net Income from non-cash assets. Non-cash ROE = Net Income from non-cash assets / (Book Value of Equity - Cash). Expected Growth in Net Income = Equity Reinvestment Rate x Non-cash ROE.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (net income, interest income, tax rate, net cap ex, change in WC, debt ratio, book equity, cash → expected growth).
**Tags:** net-income-growth, equity-reinvestment, non-cash-roe, fundamental-growth

## p.183 — III. Expected Growth in EBIT and Fundamentals: Stable ROC and Reinvestment Rate

**Teaches:** For growth in operating income, the reinvestment rate uses net cap ex plus working-capital changes over after-tax operating income, and the return measure is return on capital. Expected operating income growth = reinvestment rate x ROC. Proposition: for a given growth rate, a firm's net capital expenditure needs should be inversely proportional to the quality (ROC) of its investments — higher-return firms need less reinvestment to grow at the same rate.
**Formulas:** Reinvestment Rate = (Net Capital Expenditures + Change in WC) / [EBIT(1 - t)], where EBIT(1-t) = after-tax operating income, t = tax rate, WC = working capital. ROC = EBIT(1 - t) / (Book Value of Debt + Book Value of Equity - Cash). Expected Growth in Operating Income = Reinvestment Rate x ROC.
**Rules:** For a given growth rate, required reinvestment is inversely proportional to ROC.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (EBIT, tax rate, net cap ex, change in WC, book debt, book equity, cash → expected growth).
**Tags:** ebit-growth, roc, reinvestment-rate, operating-income

## p.185 — The Magical Number: ROIC and its Limits

**Teaches:** A diagram anatomizing Return on Invested Capital = After-tax Operating Income / Capital Invested in existing assets, the proxy for returns on existing assets and their continuation. Numerator caveats: computed from most recent 12-month operating income net of the effective tax rate; last 12 months may be abnormally good or bad; operating income can be skewed by accounting misclassification (leases, R&D) and unusual items; current earnings are unrepresentative for young and infrastructure firms (life-cycle effect). Denominator: Invested Capital = Book Value of Equity + Book Value of Debt - Cash & Cross holdings. Denominator caveats: accounting write-offs shrink invested capital and flatter ROIC; misclassifying capital expenses (R&D) and financial expenses (leases) as operating expenses understates invested capital; without inflation adjustment, capital invested in older assets is understated.
**Formulas:** ROIC = After-tax Operating Income / Invested Capital; Invested Capital = Book Value of Equity + Book Value of Debt - Cash & Cross holdings.
**Compute:** MIXED — the ratio is deterministic from statements; correcting for write-offs, misclassification, abnormal earnings, and inflation is judgment.
**Tags:** roic, accounting-issues, invested-capital, measurement-limits

## p.186 — IV. Operating Income Growth when Return on Capital is Changing

**Teaches:** When ROC is changing, growth has two components: growth from new investments (reinvestment x new ROC) plus an efficiency component from the change in return on existing capital — positive if ROC is rising, negative if falling. If the change occurs over multiple periods, the efficiency component is spread over those periods.
**Formulas:** Expected Growth Rate = ROC_{t+1} x Reinvestment Rate + (ROC_{t+1} - ROC_t) / ROC_t, where ROC_t = return on capital in period t and ROC_{t+1} = return on capital in period t+1. For a change over n periods, the second term is annualized/spread across the periods.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (ROC_t, ROC_{t+1}, reinvestment rate → expected growth).
**Tags:** ebit-growth, changing-roc, efficiency-growth, fundamental-growth

## p.187 — Motorola's Growth Rate (changing ROC example)

**Teaches:** Applies the changing-ROC growth formula to Motorola: current ROC 12.18% is assumed to rise to 17.22% over 5 years (halfway to the industry average). The efficiency gain, annualized over 5 years, adds to growth from new investments, giving total expected growth of 16.29% — versus only 6.45% with stable ROC. Assumption noted: new investments earn 17.22% immediately, while existing assets improve gradually.
**Formulas:** Expected Growth Rate = ROC_new_investments x Reinvestment Rate_current + {[1 + (ROC_in_5_years - ROC_current)/ROC_current]^(1/5) - 1}, symbols as labeled (ROC on new investments, current reinvestment rate, current ROC, target ROC in 5 years).
**Example:** Motorola: ROC current = 12.18%, reinvestment rate = 52.99%, target ROC = 17.22%. Growth = 0.1722 x 0.5299 + {[1 + (0.1722 - 0.1218)/0.1218]^(1/5) - 1} = 16.29%. Decomposition: growth from new investments = 0.1722 x 0.5299 = 9.12%; efficiency growth = 16.29% - 9.12% = 7.17%.
**Compute:** MIXED — arithmetic is deterministic from the inputs; choosing the target ROC (halfway to industry average) is judgment.
**Tags:** changing-roc, motorola, efficiency-growth, ebit-growth

## p.188 — The Value of Growth

**Teaches:** Five hypothetical firms all show the same 10% expected growth rate, but generated in very different ways (heavy reinvestment at low returns vs. light reinvestment at high returns vs. pure efficiency gains). With a 10% cost of capital, growth creates value only when the return earned exceeds the cost of capital: Firm 1 (50% ROIC on new investment) and Firm 5 (pure efficiency, no reinvestment) create the most value; Firm 3 (200% reinvestment at 5% ROIC, below cost of capital) destroys value; Firm 2 and Firm 4 earn returns equal to cost of capital and add little or nothing from new investment. Growth per se is not valuable — its quality (ROIC vs. cost of capital) is.

## p.190 — Estimating Growth when Operating Income is Negative or Margins are Changing

**Teaches:** Fundamental growth equations assume a sustainable ROE/ROC; they break down when operating income is negative or margins are shifting. In those cases use a three-step top-down process: (1) estimate revenue growth over time — size the total market, estimate your company's market share, decrease growth as the firm gets larger, and sanity-check absolute revenue levels for feasibility; (2) estimate operating margins each year — set a target margin the firm converges to and adjust the current margin toward it; (3) estimate the capital needed to deliver the revenue growth and margins — via a sales-to-capital ratio that converts each year's revenue change into reinvestment.
**Rules:** Use top-down (revenue → margin → reinvestment) growth whenever operating income is negative or margins are expected to change; taper revenue growth with size; check absolute revenues against market size.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (market sizing, share, target margin, sales/capital are all estimates; the mechanics that follow from them are deterministic).
**Tags:** top-down-growth, negative-earnings, operating-margin, revenue-growth

## p.193 — Expected Growth Rate: Summary Decision Tree

**Teaches:** A flowchart organizing every growth-estimation approach in the section. ## p.195 — Getting Closure in Valuation

**Teaches:** A publicly traded firm potentially has infinite life, so its value is the present value of cash flows forever. Since cash flows cannot be estimated forever, we forecast a finite "growth period" of N years and capture everything beyond it in a terminal value discounted back from year N.
**Formulas:** Value = sum over t = 1 to infinity of CF_t / (1+r)^t. Practically: Value = sum over t = 1 to N of CF_t / (1+r)^t + Terminal Value / (1+r)^N, where CF_t = expected cash flow in year t, r = discount rate, N = length of the explicit growth period, Terminal Value = value of all cash flows beyond year N.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (cash flows, r, N, terminal value → value); estimating the inputs is judgment.
**Tags:** terminal-value, dcf, present-value, valuation-structure

## p.196 — Ways of Estimating Terminal Value

**Teaches:** Three routes to a terminal value. Liquidation value: most useful when assets are separable and marketable. Multiple approach: the easiest, but it turns your DCF into a relative valuation (the terminal value imports market pricing). Stable growth model: technically soundest, but requires judgments about when the firm will reach a stable growth rate it can sustain forever and what excess returns (if any) it earns in perpetuity.
**Rules:** Prefer the stable growth model for going concerns; use liquidation value for separable, marketable assets; avoid the multiple approach if you want a pure intrinsic valuation.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of approach and its inputs).
**Tags:** terminal-value, stable-growth, liquidation-value, exit-multiples

## p.197 — 1. Obey the Growth Cap

**Teaches:** With cash flows growing at a constant rate forever, value = next period's expected cash flow / (r - g). The stable growth rate cannot exceed the growth rate of the economy, though it can be set lower (stable firms in an economy that also contains high-growth firms will grow below the economy-wide rate) and can even be negative (the firm shrinks toward disappearance, lowering terminal value). Growth must be nominal if cash flows and discount rates are nominal, in the valuation's currency. A simple proxy for the economy's nominal growth rate is the riskfree rate.
**Formulas:** Terminal Value = Expected Cash Flow Next Period / (r - g), where r = discount rate (cost of equity or cost of capital) and g = expected constant growth rate.
**Rules:** g_stable ≤ growth rate of the economy; g may be set lower or negative; nominal cash flows require a nominal g in the valuation currency; proxy for nominal economy growth = riskfree rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (CF next period, r, g → terminal value); choosing g subject to the cap is judgment.
**Tags:** terminal-value, growth-cap, stable-growth, riskfree-rate

## p.198 — Risk Free Rates and Nominal GDP Growth

**Teaches:** Both the riskfree rate and nominal GDP growth decompose into expected inflation plus a real component (real interest rate vs. real growth). The argument for capping stable growth at the riskfree rate: (1) long term, the real growth rate cannot be lower than the real interest rate, since growth in goods/services must cover the promised rate; (2) real growth can exceed the real interest rate to compensate risk-taking, but the gap shrinks as economies mature, and it is prudent to assume the extra growth accrues to the economy's growth companies rather than to a stable firm. U.S. history shows nominal GDP growth and the 10-year T-bond rate tracking closely.
**Formulas:** Risk free Rate = Expected Inflation + Expected Real Interest Rate. Nominal GDP Growth = Expected Inflation + Expected Real Growth.

## p.199 — A Practical Reason for the Risk Free Rate Cap: Preserve Consistency

**Teaches:** Your riskfree rate embeds an implicit assumption about nominal economic growth: a low riskfree rate implies low inflation and low real growth; a high riskfree rate implies high nominal growth. If your explicit terminal growth assumption in the numerator conflicts with the implicit growth assumption in the discount rate, the valuation is internally inconsistent and biased: assuming high nominal growth with a low riskfree rate overvalues businesses; assuming low nominal growth with a high riskfree rate undervalues them. Capping g at the riskfree rate keeps numerator and denominator consistent.
**Rules:** Keep the terminal growth assumption consistent with the riskfree rate: high g + low riskfree rate → overvaluation; low g + high riskfree rate → undervaluation; cap g at the riskfree rate to preserve consistency.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** terminal-value, consistency, riskfree-rate, growth-cap
