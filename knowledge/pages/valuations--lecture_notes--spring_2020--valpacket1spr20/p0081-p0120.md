## p.81 — Regression betas: unreliable when they look bad (GoPro)

**Teaches:** A Bloomberg historical-beta regression of GoPro (GPRO) against the S&P 500 (weekly returns, 5/2014–6/2016) shows how noisy a regression beta can be. Raw beta is 1.604 and adjusted beta 1.403. But R-squared is only 0.095 (just 9.5% of GoPro's risk is market risk) and the standard error of beta is 0.497. The true beta could plausibly lie anywhere from roughly 0.6 to 2.6. A single regression beta with a huge standard error is close to useless as a risk measure.
**Data:** Key regression stats: Raw beta 1.604; Adjusted beta 1.403; Alpha -0.835; R^2 0.095; R 0.309; Std error of beta 0.497; t-test 3.230; 101 observations.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (regression of stock returns on index returns → beta, alpha, R^2, std error), but interpretation of reliability is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** beta, regression-beta, standard-error, gopro

## p.82 — Regression betas: unreliable even when they look good (Nokia)

**Teaches:** Bloomberg's regression of Nokia against the Helsinki HEX index (8/1998–8/2000) looks superb: raw beta 1.27, R^2 = 0.94, std error of beta only 0.03. But it is misleading. Nokia was roughly 70% of the HEX index at the time, so the stock was essentially being regressed against itself. A high R^2 and low standard error do not guarantee a meaningful beta when the stock dominates its own index. Also shown: Bloomberg's adjustment formula ADJ BETA = (0.67)*RAW BETA + (0.33)*1.0.
**Formulas:** Adjusted beta = 0.67 * Raw beta + 0.33 * 1.0 (Bloomberg's mean-reversion adjustment toward 1; Raw beta = regression slope).
**Data:** Nokia vs HEX: Raw beta 1.27; Adjusted beta 1.18; Alpha 0.42; R^2 0.94; Std error of beta 0.03; 103 points.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (regression + adjustment formula); recognizing the index-domination problem is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** beta, regression-beta, index-domination, nokia

## p.85 — Alternatives to regression betas: a decision tree

**Teaches:** A flowchart of relative-risk measures organized by two beliefs. If you believe marginal investors are diversified: with price-based measures use CAPM, APM, or multi-factor models; without price-based measures use accounting betas or cost-of-debt-based models. If you do NOT believe marginal investors are diversified: with price-based measures use relative price volatility, proxy models, CAPM Plus, or implied cost of capital; without price-based measures use relative earnings volatility or accounting-ratio-based models. So rejecting betas or modern portfolio theory does not free you from measuring relative risk — it just changes the instrument.
**Rules:** Diversified marginal investor? Yes + price-based → CAPM/APM/multi-factor; Yes + not price-based → accounting betas, cost-of-debt-based models; No + price-based → relative price volatility, proxy models, CAPM Plus, implied cost of capital; No + not price-based → relative earnings volatility, accounting-ratio-based models.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of framework).
**Tags:** relative-risk, capm-alternatives, risk-measures, decision-tree

## p.86 — Price-based measures for non-diversified investors

**Teaches:** Three price-based relative-risk measures that drop the diversified-investor assumption. (1) Relative standard deviation: Relative volatility = std dev of the stock / average std dev across all stocks; captures total risk, not just market risk. (2) Proxy models: run multiple regressions of historical returns on individual stocks against firm fundamentals (started with market cap / the small-cap effect; later added momentum, liquidity, etc.) to find variables that explain return differences. (3) CAPM Plus models: start with the traditional CAPM (Rf + Beta*ERP) and add extra premiums for proxies (e.g., a small-cap premium).
**Formulas:** Relative volatility = Std dev of stock / Average std dev across all stocks. CAPM Plus: Cost of equity = Rf + Beta*ERP + additional proxy premiums (Rf = riskfree rate, ERP = equity risk premium).
**Compute:** MIXED — relative std dev and regression estimates are deterministic from return data; choosing proxies/premiums is judgment.
**Tags:** relative-volatility, proxy-models, capm-plus, total-risk

## p.87 — Non-price-based risk measures

**Teaches:** Three options if you distrust market-price-based risk measures entirely. (1) Accounting risk measures: compute an accounting beta or relative volatility from accounting earnings. Or build a risk score from balance-sheet ratios (debt ratios, cash holdings), akin to default-risk scores like the Z-score. (2) Qualitative risk models — risk assessments based at least partly on qualitative factors such as management quality. (3) Debt-based measures — infer cost of equity from an observable cost of debt: Cost of equity = Cost of debt * scaling factor, where the scaling factor can be computed from implied volatilities.
**Formulas:** Cost of equity = Cost of debt * Scaling factor (scaling factor derivable from implied volatilities).
**Compute:** MIXED — accounting betas/ratios are deterministic from statements; qualitative models and the scaling factor choice are judgment.
**Tags:** accounting-beta, qualitative-risk, cost-of-debt, risk-measures

## p.88 — Determinants of betas and relative risk

**Teaches:** The equity (levered) beta is driven by three determinants. (1) Nature of the product/service: the more discretionary the product, the higher the beta — cyclical firms > non-cyclical, luxury > basic goods, high-priced > low-priced goods, growth firms have higher betas. (2) Operating leverage (fixed costs as a percent of total costs): more fixed costs → higher beta — firms with rigid cost structures, smaller firms, and young firms should have higher betas. (3) Financial leverage: more debt → higher equity beta; Equity beta (levered) = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(Debt/Equity)). Determinants (1) and (2) drive the unlevered (firm) beta; (3) levers it up.
**Formulas:** Levered (equity) beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)), where t = tax rate, D/E = debt-to-equity ratio.
**Rules:** Higher beta expected for: discretionary/luxury/high-priced/growth products; high-fixed-cost, smaller, younger firms; more highly levered firms.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC for the levering formula (unlevered beta, t, D/E → levered beta); classifying discretionariness/operating leverage is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** beta-determinants, operating-leverage, financial-leverage, levered-beta

## p.89 — The ideal beta-estimation process

**Teaches:** In a perfect world beta would be estimated in three steps. (1) Start with the beta of the business the firm operates in. (2) Adjust that business beta for the firm's operating leverage, giving the firm's unlevered beta. (3) Apply the firm's financial leverage to get the equity beta: Levered beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1 - tax rate)*(Debt/Equity)).
**Formulas:** Levered beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1 - tax rate) * (Debt/Equity)).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC in step 3 (unlevered beta, t, D/E → levered beta); steps 1–2 involve JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** bottom-up-beta, levered-beta, estimation-process

## p.90 — Adjusting for operating leverage

**Teaches:** Within a business, firms with lower fixed costs (as a share of total costs) should have lower unlevered betas. If fixed and variable costs were observable per firm, the unlevered beta could be decomposed: Unlevered beta = Pure business beta * (1 + Fixed costs/Variable costs). The biggest obstacle is informational — fixed/variable cost splits are hard to obtain for individual firms — so in practice we assume firms within a business have similar operating leverage and use the same unlevered beta for every firm.
**Formulas:** Unlevered beta = Pure business beta * (1 + (Fixed costs / Variable costs)).
**Rules:** Practical rule: assume similar operating leverage within a business; apply one sector unlevered beta to all firms in it.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given pure business beta and cost split; in practice JUDGMENT because inputs are unobservable.
**Tags:** operating-leverage, unlevered-beta, fixed-costs

## p.91 — Adjusting for financial leverage: two levering formulas

**Teaches:** Two ways to lever a beta. Conventional approach — assume debt has zero beta (no market risk): beta_L = beta_u * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)); some versions ignore the tax term (1-t). Debt-adjusted approach — if debt carries market risk and you can estimate a debt beta: beta_L = beta_u * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)) - beta_debt * (1-t)*(D/E). The debt-adjusted version is more realistic but debt betas are difficult to estimate.
**Formulas:** Conventional: beta_L = beta_u * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)). Debt-adjusted: beta_L = beta_u * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E)) - beta_debt * (1-t)*(D/E). beta_L = levered/equity beta, beta_u = unlevered beta, beta_debt = beta of debt, t = tax rate, D/E = market debt-to-equity ratio.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (beta_u, t, D/E, optionally beta_debt → beta_L).
**Tags:** levered-beta, unlevered-beta, debt-beta, financial-leverage

## p.92 — Bottom-up betas: the five-step procedure

**Teaches:** How to build a bottom-up beta. Step 1: identify the business(es) the firm operates in. Step 2: find publicly traded firms in each business, average their regression betas, and unlever using the sample's average D/E. Unlevered beta for business = Average beta across public firms / (1 + (1-t)*(Average D/E across firms)). Optionally adjust for operating-leverage and product differences versus your firm. Step 3: estimate how much value the firm derives from each business (value weights are better than revenue or operating-income weights). Step 4: Bottom-up unlevered beta = weighted average of the business unlevered betas (weights can change year to year if the mix changes). Step 5: lever with the firm's own market D/E: Levered bottom-up beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(Debt/Equity)); if D/E is expected to change over time, the levered beta changes too.
**Formulas:** Unlevered beta for business = Average regression beta across public firms / (1 + (1-t)*(Average D/E)). Bottom-up unlevered beta = sum of (business value weight_i * unlevered beta_i). Levered bottom-up beta = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(D/E of the firm)).
**Compute:** MIXED — the unlevering/weighting/relevering arithmetic is deterministic; choosing comparables and business-value weights is judgment.
**Tags:** bottom-up-beta, comparables, unlevering, weighted-average

## p.93 — Why bottom-up betas are better

**Teaches:** Three reasons to prefer bottom-up betas over regression betas. (1) Much lower estimation error: Std error of bottom-up beta ≈ Average std error across the sample betas / sqrt(Number of firms in sample) — averaging across many firms shrinks the noise. (2) The bottom-up beta can be adjusted to reflect current or expected business mix and financial leverage, while regression betas only reflect the past. (3) Bottom-up betas can be estimated even without historical stock prices — for IPOs, private businesses, and divisions of companies.
**Formulas:** Std error of bottom-up beta = Average std error across betas / sqrt(Number of firms in sample).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (sample std errors, n → bottom-up std error).
**Tags:** bottom-up-beta, standard-error, private-firms, estimation

## p.96 — Gross debt versus net debt approaches

**Teaches:** Analysts (commonly in Europe and Latin America) sometimes use net debt (debt minus cash) instead of gross debt in the D/E ratio, producing very different levered betas. For Embraer: gross D/E = 1953/11,042 = 18.95% → levered beta 1.07; net debt ratio = (Debt - Cash)/Market value of equity = (1953-2320)/11,042 = -3.32% → levered beta = 0.95*(1+(1-0.34)*(-0.0332)) = 0.93. The net-debt cost of equity is much lower, but the cost of capital roughly evens out because the debt ratio used in the cost-of-capital weights is then also a net debt ratio.
**Formulas:** Net debt ratio = (Debt - Cash) / Market value of equity. Levered beta (net) = Unlevered beta * (1 + (1-t)*(Net D/E)).
**Example:** Embraer: gross D/E 1953/11,042 = 18.95%, levered beta 1.07; net D/E (1953-2320)/11,042 = -3.32%, levered beta 0.95*(1+0.66*(-0.0332)) = 0.93.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (debt, cash, equity market value, unlevered beta, t → both levered betas).
**Tags:** net-debt, gross-debt, levered-beta, embraer

## p.97 — The cost of equity: a recap

**Teaches:** Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Beta * (Risk premium), with best-practice notes on each input. The riskfree rate must be in the same currency as the cash flows and defined in the same terms (real or nominal). The beta should preferably be a bottom-up beta based on other firms in the business plus the firm's own financial leverage. The risk premium can be a historical premium: the mature-market premium (average premium of stocks over T.Bonds in the US) plus a country risk premium = Country default spread * (sigma_Equity / sigma_CountryBond). Or it can be an implied premium, backed out from how the equity market is priced today via a simple valuation model.
**Formulas:** Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Beta * (Risk premium). Country risk premium = Country default spread * (sigma_Equity / sigma_CountryBond), where sigma_Equity and sigma_CountryBond are the standard deviations of the country's equity market and government bond.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given Rf, beta, ERP; input estimation is JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** cost-of-equity, capm, country-risk-premium, recap

## p.99 — Estimating the cost of debt

**Teaches:** The cost of debt is the rate at which the firm could borrow today; it reflects both the firm's default risk and the current level of market interest rates. There are two main approaches. (1) Look up the yield to maturity on a liquid, long-term straight bond of the firm. This is limited because few firms have such bonds. (2) Look up the firm's bond rating and estimate a default spread from it. This is more robust, but different bonds of the same firm can carry different ratings, so use the firm's median rating. When there is no rating (or conflicting ratings), estimate a synthetic rating from the firm's financials and derive the cost of debt from that.
**Rules:** No rating or multiple ratings → estimate a synthetic rating; multiple bond ratings → use the median rating.
**Compute:** MIXED — spread lookup from rating is deterministic; choosing the approach and rating is judgment.
**Tags:** cost-of-debt, bond-rating, default-spread, synthetic-rating

## p.102 — Embraer's cost of debt with country risk

**Teaches:** With coverage 3.56, Embraer's synthetic rating is A-, implying a company default spread of 1.00%. Companies in low-rated, high-default-risk countries may also bear country default risk — more so if small or with revenues concentrated domestically. Assuming full exposure would add Brazil's 2004 country default spread of 6.01%; because Embraer is large and sells globally, only two-thirds of Brazilian country risk is added (calibrated to traded bond spreads of other large Brazilian companies in 2004). Cost of debt = Riskfree rate + (2/3)*Brazil country default spread + Company default spread = 4.29% + (2/3)(6.01%) + 1.00% = 9.29%.
**Formulas:** Pre-tax cost of debt = Riskfree rate + lambda * Country default spread + Company default spread (lambda = fraction of country risk borne, here 2/3).
**Example:** Embraer: 4.29% + (2/3)(6.01%) + 1.00% = 9.29%.
**Rules:** Large firms with significant global revenues bear less country default risk; small/domestic firms may bear the full country default spread.
**Compute:** MIXED — the sum is deterministic; the country-risk exposure fraction (2/3) is judgment.
**Tags:** cost-of-debt, country-default-spread, embraer, synthetic-rating

## p.103 — Synthetic ratings: caveats

**Teaches:** The coverage-to-rating relationship was developed with US companies and travels well for large manufacturing firms in markets with interest rates close to US rates. It is more problematic for smaller companies and for markets with much higher interest rates: higher rates mechanically depress coverage ratios. One adjustment: modify the coverage table for interest-rate differences — e.g., if emerging-market rates are twice US rates, halve the interest coverage ratio before looking up the rating (equivalently, double the required coverage).
**Rules:** If local interest rates are k times US rates, scale the firm's interest coverage ratio down by k (e.g., rates twice as high → halve the coverage ratio) before using the lookup table.
**Compute:** MIXED — the scaling is deterministic once the adjustment is chosen; deciding when/how to adjust is judgment.
**Tags:** synthetic-rating, emerging-markets, interest-rates, caveats

## p.107 — Weights for the cost of capital: why market value?

**Teaches:** Discussion question: for a publicly traded firm, cost-of-capital weights for debt and equity should be market-value weights, not book-value weights — why? Options: (a) the market is usually right, (b) market values are easy to obtain, (c) book values are meaningless, (d) none of the above. The underlying logic (developed in the course): market values represent what it would cost today to buy out the claimholders — the relevant weights for a current required return — regardless of whether the market is "right."
**Rules:** Use market-value weights (not book-value weights) for debt and equity in the cost of capital.

## p.108 — Worked example: Embraer's cost of capital (2004)

**Teaches:** Full cost-of-capital assembly for Embraer in 2004. Cost of equity = 4.29% + 1.07*(4%) + 0.27*(7.89%) = 10.70%. That is: riskfree 4.29%, levered beta 1.07 times mature-market premium 4%, plus lambda 0.27 times Brazil country risk premium 7.89%. Market value of equity is 11,042 million BR ($3,781M). Cost of debt = 4.29% + 4.00% + 1.00% = 9.29% (riskfree + country spread component + company spread). Market value of debt is 2,083 million BR ($713M). Cost of capital = 10.70%*(0.84) + 9.29%*(1-0.34)*(0.16) = 9.97%. Debt is converted from book to market value: book debt 1,953M BR, interest expense 222M BR, average maturity 4 years → market value of debt = 222*(PV of annuity, 4 years, 9.29%) + 1953/1.0929^4 = 2,083M BR. Book equity was 3,350M BR (vs 11,042 market) — illustrating why market weights differ from book.
**Formulas:** Cost of capital = Cost of equity*(E/(D+E)) + Cost of debt*(1-t)*(D/(D+E)). Market value of debt = Interest expense * (PV of annuity, maturity, cost of debt) + Book debt / (1 + cost of debt)^maturity. In words: treat total book debt as one coupon bond, with coupons = interest expense and maturity = average debt maturity, discounted at the current pre-tax cost of debt.
**Example:** Embraer: cost of equity 10.70%, E-weight 0.84; cost of debt 9.29%, t = 34%, D-weight 0.16 → cost of capital 9.97%. Market value of debt: 222*(PV annuity, 4 yrs, 9.29%) + 1953/1.0929^4 = 2,083M BR.

## p.109 — Converting a dollar cost of capital to local currency

**Teaches:** Two ways to get a nominal Brazilian-real (BR) cost of capital instead of a dollar one. Approach 1: redo everything with a BR riskfree rate. With a 12% BR riskfree rate: Cost of equity = 12% + 1.07*(4%) + 0.27*(7.89%) = 18.41%, and Cost of debt = 12% + 1% = 13%. This assumes the local riskfree rate has no country risk premium embedded. Approach 2: use differential inflation — Cost of capital_BR = (1 + Cost of capital_$)*((1 + Inflation_BR)/(1 + Inflation_$)) - 1; with 8% BR inflation and 2% US inflation: 1.0997*(1.08/1.02) - 1 = 0.1644 = 16.44%.
**Formulas:** Cost of capital (local) = (1 + Cost of capital_$) * ((1 + Inflation_local)/(1 + Inflation_$)) - 1.
**Example:** Embraer: 1.0997*(1.08/1.02) - 1 = 16.44% BR cost of capital (from the 9.97% dollar cost of capital). Approach 1 gives cost of equity 18.41%, cost of debt 13% with a 12% BR riskfree rate.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (dollar cost of capital + both inflation rates → local cost of capital; or local Rf + beta + premiums → local costs).

## p.110 — Hybrids and preferred stock

**Teaches:** Hybrid securities (e.g., convertible bonds) should be decomposed into their debt and equity components and allocated accordingly. For $125 million of convertible debt, split it into a straight-debt piece and a conversion-option piece; the conversion option counts as equity. Preferred stock is best kept as a separate third component of capital with its own cost = the preferred dividend yield. Rule of thumb: if preferred stock is less than 5% of the firm's outstanding market value, lumping it with debt causes no significant valuation impact.
**Formulas:** Cost of preferred stock = Preferred dividend / Price of preferred (the preferred dividend yield).
**Rules:** Decompose convertibles into straight debt + conversion option (option = equity). Keep preferred as a separate component unless it is < 5% of firm market value, in which case merging it with debt is acceptable.
**Compute:** MIXED — the decomposition arithmetic is deterministic (see p.111); classification choices are rules of thumb.

## p.111 — Worked example: decomposing a convertible bond

**Teaches:** A firm has $125 million face value of convertible debt, 4% stated coupon, 10-year maturity, market value $140 million, firm rated A with 8% market rate on A-rated straight debt. Straight-debt component = coupon stream plus principal discounted at the straight-bond rate: (4% of $125M) * (PV of annuity, 10 years, 8%) + 125/1.08^10 = $91.45 million. Equity (conversion option) component = market value minus straight debt = $140M - $91.45M = $48.55 million. The $91.45M goes into debt and the $48.55M is added to market capitalization when forming cost-of-capital weights.
**Formulas:** Straight debt value = Coupon * (PV of annuity, n years, r) + Face value/(1+r)^n, with r = market rate on same-rated straight debt, n = maturity. Equity portion = Market value of convertible - Straight debt value.
**Example:** $5M coupons (4% of 125), 10 yrs at 8%: straight debt = $91.45M; equity option = 140 - 91.45 = $48.55M.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (face, coupon, maturity, market value, straight-bond rate → debt and equity components).

## p.114 — Steps in cash flow estimation

**Teaches:** Three steps to estimate cash flows. (1) Estimate current earnings: for cash flows to equity use earnings after interest expense (net income); for cash flows to the firm use operating earnings after taxes. (2) Account for reinvestment made to create future growth: investment not expensed shows up as capital expenditures (partly funded by the depreciation cash flow), and increases in working capital are also growth investments. (3) For cash flows to equity only, add the cash flows from net debt issues (debt issued minus debt repaid).

## p.115 — Measuring cash flows: firm vs equity

**Teaches:** Cash flows can be measured to all claimholders or to equity investors only. To all claimholders (FCFF): EBIT*(1 - tax rate) - (Capital expenditures - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash working capital = Free Cash Flow to Firm. To just equity investors, either the full FCFE definition: Net income - (Capital expenditures - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash working capital - (Principal repaid - New debt issues) - Preferred dividend; or the actual cash returned: Dividends + Stock buybacks.
**Formulas:** FCFF = EBIT*(1-t) - (CapEx - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash WC. FCFE = Net income - (CapEx - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash WC - (Principal repaid - New debt issues) - Preferred dividends. Cash to equity (actual) = Dividends + Stock buybacks.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (EBIT, t, CapEx, depreciation, WC change, debt flows, preferred dividends → FCFF/FCFE).

## p.116 — Three equivalent pathways to FCFF

**Teaches:** Three algebraically equivalent ways to compute free cash flow to the firm. (1) FCFF = EBIT*(1 - tax rate) - [(CapEx - Depreciation) + Change in non-cash working capital]. (2) FCFF = EBIT*(1-t) - Reinvestment, where Reinvestment = net capex + WC change. (3) FCFF = EBIT*(1-t) * (1 - Reinvestment rate), where Reinvestment rate = Reinvestment / EBIT*(1-t). Closing question: where are the tax savings from interest expenses? They are not in the cash flows. The tax benefit of debt is captured in the (1-t) term of the cost of capital, so putting it in FCFF too would double count.
**Formulas:** FCFF = EBIT*(1-t) - (CapEx - Depreciation) - Change in non-cash WC = EBIT*(1-t) - Reinvestment = EBIT*(1-t)*(1 - Reinvestment rate); Reinvestment rate = (Net CapEx + Change in non-cash WC) / (EBIT*(1-t)).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (any pathway's inputs → FCFF).
**Tags:** fcff, reinvestment-rate, tax-shield, cash-flows

## p.118 — From reported to actual earnings (roadmap)

**Teaches:** Roadmap diagram for converting reported earnings into earnings usable in valuation. Three operations feed "Measuring Earnings". (1) Normalize earnings, using the firm's own history and comparable firms (for firms with temporarily depressed or inflated earnings). (2) Cleanse operating items of financial expenses, capital expenses, and non-recurring expenses. (3) Update, using trailing 12-month earnings and unofficial numbers rather than stale annual reports.
**Rules:** Three earnings fixes: update to trailing numbers; cleanse operating income of financial (operating leases), capital (R&D), and non-recurring items; normalize when current earnings are unrepresentative.

## p.119 — I. Update earnings

**Teaches:** Annual reports are often outdated; update earnings with trailing 12-month data built from quarterly reports, or with informal/unofficial news reports if quarterlies are unavailable. Updating matters most for smaller, more volatile firms and firms that have recently restructured. Time saver: a trailing 12-month figure needs only the last 10K and the most recent 10Q, using the 10Q's year-to-date numbers. For a third-quarter 10Q: Trailing 12-month revenue = Revenues (last 10K) - Revenues from first 3 quarters of last year + Revenues from first 3 quarters of this year. The same construction applies to any line item.
**Formulas:** Trailing 12-month figure = Last 10K annual figure - Year-to-date figure from same period last year + Year-to-date figure from this year's latest 10Q.
**Rules:** Update earnings especially for small, volatile, or recently restructured firms.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (10K annual + two YTD figures → trailing 12-month figure).
**Tags:** trailing-earnings, 10k-10q, updating, earnings

## p.120 — II. Correcting accounting earnings

**Teaches:** Two cleansing checks on operating earnings. (1) No financial expenses mixed into operating expenses. A financial expense is any tax-deductible commitment that must be met regardless of operating results; failure to pay means loss of control of the business. Operating leases are the prime example. Accounting treats them as operating expenses, but they are really financial expenses and must be reclassified. This changes operating earnings but not equity earnings. (2) No capital expenses mixed into operating expenses. A capital expense is any expense expected to generate benefits over multiple periods. R&D is the prime example: since R&D is really a capital expenditure, operating income must be adjusted to reflect capitalizing it.
**Rules:** Definitions/classification tests: financial expense = tax-deductible, unavoidable commitment whose failure to meet causes loss of control (→ treat operating leases as debt); capital expense = expense generating benefits over multiple periods (→ capitalize R&D).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT in classification; the resulting adjustments (later pages) are deterministic.
**Tags:** operating-leases, r-and-d, operating-income, earnings-adjustments
