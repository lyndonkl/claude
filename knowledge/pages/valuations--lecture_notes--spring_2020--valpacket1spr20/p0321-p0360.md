## p.321 — Lesson 3: The "corporate governance" drag

**Teaches:** Stockholders in Asian, Latin American and many European companies have little or no power over managers; insiders often own voting shares and control the firm, creating large conflict-of-interest potential. This weak corporate governance is often given as a reason for using higher discount rates or discounting the estimated value of these companies. The slide poses a Yes/No question: would you discount the value estimated for an emerging market company to allow for this absence of stockholder power? It sets up the answer that the effect belongs in expected cash flows and returns (see the following Tube Investments slides), not in an arbitrary discount.

## p.325 — Lesson 4: Watch out for cross holdings

**Teaches:** Emerging market companies are more prone to cross holdings than developed-market companies. This is partly historical — many large public firms were family-owned until a few decades ago. It is also partly because those running the companies value control and use cross holdings to preserve it. In many emerging market companies, the real valuation work begins after the DCF is done: numerous cross holdings must be valued, often with minimal information.

## p.331 — Adjusting for regime change

**Teaches:** How to handle political/regime risk in the Aramco valuation as a discrete scenario rather than a discount-rate fudge. If no chance of regime change, expected value stays $1.65 trillion; if regime change is imminent with full expropriation, expected value is zero; with a non-trivial probability (e.g. 20%) of regime change that reduces but does not extinguish the equity claim, take a probability-weighted expected value.
**Formulas:** Expected value of equity = Value under status quo × P(status quo) + Value under regime change × P(regime change) = $1.65T × 0.8 + $0.825T × 0.2 = $1.485 trillion. (Regime-change value = DCF with higher royalties & taxes = $0.825 trillion.)
**Example:** Aramco: 80% probability House of Saud rules ($1.65T equity), 20% probability regime change ($0.825T) → expected equity value $1.485 trillion.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (scenario values and probabilities → expected value); the probabilities and scenario values are JUDGMENT.

## p.332 — V. Valuing Financial Service Companies (framework)

**Teaches:** Section opener mapping the standard valuation questions onto the special problems of financial service firms.
- Existing assets are usually financial assets or loans marked to market. Earnings therefore tell little about underlying risk.
- Defining capital expenditures and working capital is a challenge. Growth is strongly influenced by regulatory limits — both the amount of new investment and the returns on it can change with regulation.
- For a financial service firm, debt is raw material rather than a source of capital. It is hard to define, and broad definitions produce high financial leverage that magnifies small operating changes into big equity-risk changes.
- Preferred stock is a significant source of capital.
- Beyond normal constraints, financial firms must maintain regulator-acceptable capital ratios or be taken over and shut down.

## p.334 — Lesson 1: Financial service companies are opaque

**Teaches:** With financial service firms we enter a Faustian bargain. They reveal very little about asset quality — bank loans are not broken down by default-risk status. We accept this because assets are marked to market by accountants who presumably see what we can't. Estimating cash flows for financial firms is difficult, so we trust them to pay out their cash flows as dividends — hence the use of the dividend discount model. In crises, or when you don't trust banks to pay out what they can afford, the DDM may not give a reliable value.
**Rules:** Default model for banks = dividend discount model; distrust it during crises or when payout does not reflect capacity.

## p.336 — Lesson 2: For financial service companies, book value matters

**Teaches:** Book value of assets/equity is mostly irrelevant for non-financial companies (it is historical and can be nonsensical — negative for more than 1,000 publicly traded US companies). For financial service firms, book equity is relevant for two reasons. First, mark-to-market accounting makes book value more likely to reflect what firms own right now. Second, regulatory capital ratios are based on book equity, so a bank with negative or low book equity will be shut down by regulators. Consequently, reinvestment for a bank can be defined as the amount it must add to book equity to sustain growth ambitions and safety requirements.
**Formulas:** FCFE (bank) = Net Income − Reinvestment in regulatory capital (book equity).
**Rules:** For banks, treat additions to regulatory (book) equity as the reinvestment; a bank with negative or low book equity faces regulatory shutdown.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (concept), with the FCFE identity DETERMINISTIC given net income and regulatory-capital investment.

## p.338 — VI. Valuing Companies with "intangible" assets (framework)

**Teaches:** Section opener for intangible-asset companies (technology, pharma, human-capital and brand firms). Capital expenditures that acquire intangible assets are mis-categorized as operating expenses. This distorts accounting earnings and measures of capital invested. It also makes it very difficult to assess how much the firm is reinvesting for growth and how well its investments are doing. It can be harder to borrow against intangible assets than tangible ones, and operating risk depends on how stable the intangible asset is. Intangibles like brand name and customer loyalty can last very long or dissipate overnight, affecting when the firm matures.

## p.339 — Lesson: Accounting rules are cluttered with inconsistencies

**Teaches:** By accounting first principles, capital expenditures create benefits over many periods, so they should be depreciated/amortized over their life and appear as assets on the balance sheet, not expensed against current operating income. Accounting is consistent about cap ex only for manufacturing firms. The inconsistencies: for pharmaceutical and technology firms, R&D is the ultimate cap ex but is treated as an operating expense. For consulting and other human-capital firms, recruiting and training expenses are the long-term investments, yet they are expensed. For brand-name consumer product companies, part of advertising builds brand name and is the real capital expenditure, yet it too is expensed.
**Rules:** Reclassify as capital expenditure: R&D (pharma/tech), recruiting & training (human-capital firms), brand-building portion of advertising (consumer products).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.

## p.340 — Exhibit 11.1: Converting R&D expenses to R&D assets — Amgen

**Teaches:** Three-step procedure to capitalize R&D, applied to Amgen. Step 1: pick an amortizable life for R&D (10 years for Amgen, given the FDA approval process length). Step 2: capitalize historical R&D. For each of the past 10 years, the unamortized portion is (remaining life fraction) × that year's R&D. This year's amortization from each past year is (1/life) × that year's R&D. Sum the unamortized portions to get the R&D asset. Sum the amortizations to get R&D amortization. Step 3: restate earnings (add back current R&D, subtract amortization), book values (add unamortized R&D), and return numbers. ROE and ROC both fall despite higher income because capital rises proportionately more.
**Formulas:** With straight-line amortization over life N: Unamortized portion of R&D from year −k = R&D_(−k) × (N − k)/N; Amortization this year from year −k = R&D_(−k)/N. Adjusted net income = Net income + current R&D − R&D amortization. Adjusted book equity = Book equity + unamortized R&D. Adjusted operating income = Operating income + current R&D − R&D amortization. Adjusted invested capital = Invested capital + unamortized R&D.

## p.344 — Lesson 1: With "macro" companies, it is easy to get lost in macro assumptions

**Teaches:** With cyclical and commodity companies your value will inevitably be affected by your views on the economy or the commodity price. **Rules:** Use current market-based macro inputs in the valuation; state macro disagreements separately rather than embedding them.

## p.350 — Are you valuing or pricing?

**Teaches:** Framework contrasting intrinsic value and price. Intrinsic value is the value of cash flows adjusted for time and risk. Its drivers: cash flows from existing assets, growth in cash flows, and quality of growth. Its tools: DCF, intrinsic multiples, book-value-based approaches, and excess return models. Price is set by demand and supply; its drivers are market moods & momentum and surface stories about fundamentals; its tools are multiples and comparables, charting and technical indicators, and "pseudo DCF." Between them sits "the gap" — is there one and will it close? — whose drivers are information, liquidity, and corporate governance, and whose tools are behavioral finance and price catalysts.

## p.353 — The drivers of value

**Teaches:** The four questions that drive intrinsic value, split by equity vs firm perspective.
1. Cash flows from existing assets. Equity: cash flows after debt payments. Firm: cash flows before debt payments.
2. Value added by growth assets. Equity: growth in equity earnings/cash flows. Firm: growth in operating earnings/cash flows.
3. Riskiness of cash flows from existing and growth assets. Equity: risk in equity in the company. Firm: risk in the firm's operations.
4. When will the firm become a mature firm, and what are the potential roadblocks?

## p.354 — The determinants of price

**Teaches:** Four determinants of market price.
- Mood and momentum: price is driven in large part by behavioral factors — panic, fear, greed.
- Liquidity & trading ease: value may not change period to period, but liquidity does, and price moves with it.
- Incremental information: money is made on price changes, not levels, so focus falls on news, rumors and gossip relative to expectations.
- Group think: to the extent pricing means gauging what other investors will do, the herd sets the price.

## p.357 — The valuer's dilemma and ways of dealing with it

**Teaches:** Valuers face two uncertainties.
1. Uncertainty about the magnitude of the gap. Four ways to deal with it. Use a margin of safety — many value investors' protection against risk and uncertainty. Collect more information about the company. Ask what-if questions via scenario analysis. Confront uncertainty by bringing it into the analysis and dealing with the consequences.
2. Uncertainty about the gap closing. This is tougher. Reduce exposure by lengthening your time horizon, and by providing or looking for a catalyst that will cause the gap to close.
**Rules:** Handle gap-magnitude uncertainty with margin of safety, more information, scenario analysis, or explicit uncertainty modeling; handle gap-closing uncertainty with longer horizons and catalysts.

## p.358 — Strategies for managing the risk in the "closing" of the gap

**Teaches:** Two strategies for the gap-closing risk. The "karmic" approach: buy (short) under (over) valued companies and wait, implicitly assuming that given time the market will see and fix its error. The catalyst approach: for price to converge on value there usually must be a catalyst. If you are an activist investor, you may be the catalyst yourself — your buying can be a sufficient signal for the market to reassess. If not, watch for catalysts such as a new CEO or management team, a "blockbuster" new product, or an acquisition bid targeting the firm.
