## p.201 — Growth value and competitive advantages

**Teaches:** It is not growth per se that creates value, but growth with excess returns — return on capital above cost of capital. To keep creating value from growth, growth firms must hold rivals at bay. Proposition 1: the stronger and more durable the moat, the longer a growth firm can sustain "value creating" growth. Proposition 2: growth firms with strong, durable moats are rare.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (assessing strength/sustainability of competitive advantages).
**Tags:** terminal-value, excess-returns, competitive-advantage, growth

## p.202 — Terminal value: growth has to be earned (stable-period reinvestment)

**Teaches:** The fundamental growth equation is Growth rate = Reinvestment Rate × Return on invested capital + growth from improved efficiency. In stable growth you cannot count on efficiency gains, so you must reinvest to deliver the forecast growth rate. The stable-period reinvestment rate is therefore forced by your stable growth rate and the perpetual return on capital. The terminal value equation should embed that reinvestment.
**Formulas:**
- Growth rate = Reinvestment Rate * Return on invested capital + Growth rate from improved efficiency
- Reinvestment Rate (stable) = Stable growth rate / Stable period ROC = g / ROC
- Terminal Value in year n = EBIT_(n+1) * (1 - t) * (1 - g/ROC) / (Cost of Capital - g), where EBIT_(n+1) = operating income in the first stable year, t = tax rate, g = stable growth rate, ROC = stable-period return on capital.
**Rules:** In stable growth, never assume growth without reinvestment; reinvestment rate must equal g/ROC.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs EBIT_(n+1), t, g, ROC, cost of capital → terminal value).
**Tags:** terminal-value, reinvestment-rate, stable-growth, dcf

## p.203 — The Big Assumption: terminal value sensitivity to ROC vs growth

**Teaches:** Terminal value for a firm with expected after-tax operating income of $100 million in year n+1 and cost of capital of 10%, at different perpetual growth rates and perpetual returns on capital. When ROC = cost of capital (10%), terminal value is $1,000 regardless of growth — growth adds no value. When ROC > cost of capital, higher growth raises value; when ROC < cost of capital, higher growth destroys value.

## p.204 — Excess returns to zero?

**Teaches:** Some (McKinsey, for instance) argue return on capital should always equal cost of capital in stable growth. But empirically excess returns persist for very long periods: a McKinsey chart of the median top-500 US companies (cohorts of 1965, 1975, 1985, 1995) shows ROIC is sustained around 8-12% over time, while real revenue growth inevitably declines toward GDP growth. Implication: growth fades but excess returns need not, so assuming ROC = WACC in perpetuity is a choice, not a necessity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choosing perpetual excess-return assumption).
**Tags:** excess-returns, stable-growth, roic, empirical-evidence

## p.205 — Don't fall for sleight of hand in terminal value

**Teaches:** A common DCF trick assumes that in stable growth capex merely offsets depreciation and there are no working capital needs (zero net reinvestment). If reinvestment is zero, the only defensible expected growth in the terminal value is zero (or arguably the inflation rate if you believe purely inflationary growth requires no net reinvestment — the slide poses this as a question). Assuming positive real growth with no reinvestment double counts.
**Rules:** If you assume capex = depreciation and no working capital needs in stable growth, you cannot use a positive real growth rate in the terminal value.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (consistency check on assumptions).
**Tags:** terminal-value, reinvestment, dcf-errors, stable-growth

## p.206 — Internal consistency in stable growth

**Teaches:** Stable-growth inputs must be internally consistent. Stable firms tend to have betas closer to one, debt ratios closer to industry/mature-company averages, and country risk premiums (especially emerging markets) that evolve toward maturity over time. Excess returns should approach or become zero: ROC → cost of capital and ROE → cost of equity. Reinvestment and payout must reflect the lower growth and excess returns.
**Formulas:**
- Stable period payout ratio = 1 − g/ROE, where g = stable growth rate, ROE = stable-period return on equity.
- Stable period reinvestment rate = g/ROC, where ROC = stable-period return on capital.
**Rules:** In stable growth: beta ≈ 1, debt ratio ≈ industry average, excess returns → 0 (ROC → WACC, ROE → cost of equity).
**Compute:** MIXED (payout/reinvestment formulas are deterministic given g, ROE, ROC; choosing stable beta, debt ratio, and excess returns is judgment).
**Tags:** stable-growth, internal-consistency, payout-ratio, terminal-value

## p.209 — Which cash flow should I discount? Equity vs firm valuation

**Teaches:** Decision rule for equity vs firm valuation. Equity valuation discounts cash flows to equity at the cost of equity; firm valuation discounts cash flows to the firm at the cost of capital and avoids forecasting debt payments/issues when leverage is changing.
**Rules:**
- Use EQUITY valuation: (a) for firms with stable leverage (whether high or not), and (b) if equity (stock) is what is being valued.
- Use FIRM valuation: (a) for firms with leverage that is too high or too low and expected to change over time (debt payments/issues need not be forecast in cash flows and cost of capital does not change dramatically); (b) when you have only partial information on leverage (e.g. interest expenses missing); (c) in all other cases where you are more interested in valuing the firm than the equity (e.g. value consulting).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of the firm's leverage situation).
**Tags:** equity-valuation, firm-valuation, fcff, fcfe, model-choice

## p.210 — Dividends or FCFE?

**Teaches:** Given a decision to discount cash flows to equity, choose between the dividend discount model and the FCFE model based on how close dividends (plus buybacks) are to FCFE and on data availability.
**Rules:**
- Use the DIVIDEND DISCOUNT MODEL: (a) when dividends (and stock repurchases) are close to FCFE over an extended period; (b) when FCFE is difficult to estimate (e.g. banks and financial service companies).
- Use the FCFE MODEL: (a) when dividends are significantly higher or lower than FCFE — rule of thumb: if dividends < 80% of FCFE or dividends > 110% of FCFE over a 5-year period, use FCFE; (b) when dividends are not available (private companies, IPOs).
**Compute:** MIXED (the 80%/110% screen is deterministic given 5-year dividends and FCFE; the overall model choice is judgment).
**Tags:** ddm, fcfe, model-choice, dividends

## p.211 — What discount rate should I use?

**Teaches:** Match the discount rate to the cash flow: cash flows to equity → cost of equity; cash flows to the firm → cost of capital. Match the currency of the risk-free rate (and discount rate) to the currency of the cash flows. Match real cash flows to a real cost of capital and nominal cash flows to a nominal cost of capital.
**Rules:**
- Discounting equity cash flows → use cost of equity; firm cash flows → use cost of capital.
- Currency of the risk-free rate must match the currency of the cash flows.
- Real cash flows → real cost of capital; nominal cash flows → nominal cost of capital.
- If inflation is low (<10%), stay with nominal cash flows (taxes are based on nominal income); if inflation is high (>10%), switch to real cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of consistent framework; the 10% inflation threshold is a deterministic screen).
**Tags:** discount-rate, currency-consistency, real-vs-nominal, dcf

## p.212 — Which growth pattern should I use?

**Teaches:** Choose the number of growth stages based on firm size, growth rate relative to the economy, and barriers to entry.
**Rules:**
- Use a STABLE GROWTH model if the firm is large and growing at close to or below the economy's growth rate. Also if regulation constrains it from growing faster than the economy, or if it has stable-firm characteristics (average risk and reinvestment rates).
- Use a 2-STAGE model if the firm is large and growing at a moderate rate (≤ overall economy growth rate + 10%), or has a single product and barriers to entry with a finite life (e.g. patents).
- Use a 3-STAGE or n-stage model if the firm is small and growing at a very high rate (> overall growth rate + 10%), or has significant barriers to entry, or has firm characteristics very different from the norm.
**Compute:** MIXED (growth-rate thresholds are deterministic screens; assessing barriers/characteristics is judgment).
**Tags:** growth-patterns, two-stage-model, model-choice, stable-growth

## p.213 — The building blocks of valuation

**Teaches:** Summary matrix of the three choices in any DCF: a cash flow, a discount rate, and a growth pattern. Cash flow choices: dividends, FCFE, or FCFF (formulas below). Discount rate choices: cost of equity for equity cash flows (the riskier the investment, the greater the cost of equity), estimated via CAPM or APM; cost of capital (WACC) for firm cash flows. Growth pattern choices: stable growth (constant g), two-stage (high growth then stable), or three-stage (high growth, transition, stable).
**Formulas:**
- FCFE = Net Income − (1−δ)(CapEx − Depreciation) − (1−δ)(ΔWorking Capital); δ = debt ratio.
- FCFF = EBIT(1−t) − (CapEx − Depreciation) − ΔWorking Capital; t = tax rate.
- CAPM cost of equity = Riskfree Rate + Beta × Risk Premium.
- APM cost of equity = Riskfree Rate + Σ_j Beta_j × Risk Premium_j (n factors).
- WACC = k_e × E/(D+E) + k_d × D/(D+E); k_d = current borrowing rate × (1−t); E and D = market values of equity and debt.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given the accounting and market inputs, each cash flow and discount rate formula computes directly; choosing among them is judgment).
**Tags:** fcff, fcfe, wacc, capm, building-blocks, dcf

## p.216 — 1. The value of cash

**Teaches:** The cleanest treatment of cash and marketable securities is to keep them out of the operating valuation. Cash flows should be before interest income from cash. The discount rate should use the beta of the operating assets alone. Then add the value of cash and marketable securities back after valuing the operating assets. Some equity valuations instead include interest income from cash in the cash flows. The discount rate must then be adjusted: the beta is weighted down by the cash holdings. Unless cash stays a fixed percentage of value over time, such valuations tend to break down.
**Rules:** Value operating assets excluding cash (pre-interest-income cash flows, operating-asset beta), then add cash back separately.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (methodological rule).
**Tags:** cash, non-operating-assets, dcf, loose-ends

## p.218 — Should you ever discount cash for its low returns?

**Teaches:** Some analysts want to discount excess cash (cash beyond operating needs) because it earns a low return relative to the firm's non-cash investments. That is the wrong reason: cash invested in riskless securities should earn a low rate, and as long as the return is fair given the riskless nature of the investment, cash does not destroy value. The right reason to discount cash, applicable to some companies: managers can do stupid things with it (overpriced acquisitions, pie-in-the-sky projects), and you should discount for that possibility.
**Rules:** Discount cash only for the risk of poor managerial deployment, never merely because its (riskless) return is below the firm's return on operating investments.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** cash, cash-discount, corporate-governance, loose-ends

## p.222 — 2. Dealing with holdings in other firms

**Teaches:** Cross holdings come in three accounting categories. Minority passive holdings: only the dividends from the holdings show up in the financial statements. Minority active holdings: the proportional share of the subsidiary's equity income appears in the income statement. Majority active holdings: the financial statements are fully consolidated, with a minority-interest entry for the part not owned.
**Rules:** Classification: minority passive → dividends only; minority active → share of equity income shown; majority active → full consolidation.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (accounting classification).

## p.223 — An exercise in valuing cross holdings

**Teaches:** Worked setup showing how cross holdings change equity value. (1) Company A is valued from consolidated financials at $1 billion (FCFF at cost of capital) with $200M debt. Equity = $1,000 − $200 = $800M. This includes 100% of the consolidated subsidiary even though A does not own 100%. (2) A also owns 10% of Company B as a passive holding, and B's market cap is $500M. Add 10% × 500 = $50M → equity ≈ $850M. (3) A owns 60% of Company C, fully consolidated. Minority interest is recorded at $40M book value on A's balance sheet. Subtract the minority interest to remove the 40% of C that A does not own — at least its $40M book value, properly its market value.
**Example:** Consolidated firm value of A = $1,000M; debt = $200M; 10% passive stake in B (market cap $500M); 60% consolidated stake in C with minority interest on books at $40M. Equity in A ≈ 1,000 − 200 + 0.10(500) − 40 = $810M using book minority interest.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (given firm value, debt, stake percentages, subsidiary values/minority interest → equity value).

## p.224 — More on cross holding valuation

## p.225 — Valuing cross holdings right: the three-step method

**Teaches:** The correct treatment of cross holdings: Step 1 — value the parent company without any cross holdings, using unconsolidated financial statements. Step 2 — value each cross holding individually with its own DCF (using market values of the holdings imports the market's valuation errors). Step 3 — combine.
**Formulas:** Value of equity in parent with N cross holdings = Value of un-consolidated parent company − Debt of un-consolidated parent company + Σ_{j=1..N} [% owned of Company j × (Value of Company j − Debt of Company j)].
**Rules:** Use unconsolidated statements for the parent; value each holding intrinsically rather than at market value.
**Compute:** MIXED (the aggregation formula is deterministic; valuing the parent and each holding is judgment/DCF).
**Tags:** cross-holdings, sum-of-parts, consolidation, valuation-method

## p.227 — If you have to settle for an approximation

**Teaches:** When unconsolidated statements or full DCFs of the holdings are unavailable, approximate market values from book values using sector price-to-book ratios. For majority (consolidated) holdings, convert the minority interest from book to market value and subtract it from the consolidated firm value to get parent equity. For minority holdings, convert their book value on the balance sheet to market value and add it to the value of operating assets.
**Formulas:** Estimated market value of minority interest = Minority interest on balance sheet × Price-to-Book ratio for the subsidiary's sector. Estimated market value of minority holdings = Book value of holdings × sector Price-to-Book ratio.
**Rules:** Majority holdings: subtract P/B-adjusted minority interest from consolidated value. Minority holdings: add P/B-adjusted book value of holdings to operating asset value.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (book values and sector P/B ratios → market-value adjustments).

## p.229 — 3. Other assets that have not been counted yet

**Teaches:** Which non-operating assets may be added to a DCF value. Do NOT add assets already contributing to cash flows: the real estate under your offices, PP&E (factories, productive assets), or brand names and customer lists (their value is in the cash flows). Definitely do not add goodwill, which is a non-asset. You CAN add overfunded defined-benefit pension plans (assets exceed expected liabilities), with two caveats. Collective bargaining agreements may block your claim to the excess, and pension withdrawals are often taxed at much higher rates. You can also add unutilized assets that generate no cash flows (e.g. vacant land): assess a market value and add it to firm value.
**Rules:** Add an asset to DCF value only if it does not contribute to the forecast cash flows; never add goodwill or brand value on top of a DCF.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (identifying double counting; assessing market values).
**Tags:** non-operating-assets, pension-plans, double-counting, goodwill

## p.234 — Dealing with complexity

**Teaches:** In DCF valuation there are three stances toward complexity. The aggressive analyst trusts the firm's statements about its value. The conservative analyst refuses to value what cannot be seen. The compromise adjusts the valuation for complexity — via cash flows, the discount rate, the growth rate or growth period, or a final complexity discount on value. In relative valuation you can estimate the market's price for complexity. A regression on the hundred largest market-cap firms shows each extra 10K page costs about 0.003 of price-to-book.
**Formulas:** PBV = 0.65 + 15.31 ROE − 0.55 Beta + 3.04 Expected growth rate − 0.003 (# Pages in 10K), where PBV = price-to-book ratio, ROE = return on equity, Beta = market beta (regression over the 100 largest market-cap firms).
**Rules:** Complexity adjustment options in DCF: adjust cash flows, discount rate, growth rate/period, or apply a final complexity discount to value.
**Compute:** MIXED (the PBV regression is deterministic given ROE, beta, growth, page count; choosing the DCF complexity adjustment is judgment).
**Tags:** complexity-discount, price-to-book, regression, relative-valuation

## p.235 — 5. Defining debt for cost of capital purposes

**Teaches:** Debt for cost-of-capital purposes has three characteristics. There is a commitment to make fixed payments in the future. The fixed payments are tax deductible. Failure to pay can lead to default or loss of control of the firm to the payee. So defined, debt should include all interest-bearing liabilities (short- and long-term) and all leases (operating as well as capital). It should NOT include accounts payable or supplier credit. Beware conservative impulses to count everything as debt: that pushes up the debt ratio and understates the cost of capital.
**Rules:** Include in debt: all interest-bearing liabilities (short and long term) and all leases (operating and capital). Exclude: accounts payable and supplier credit. Test: fixed contractual payments + tax deductibility + default/loss-of-control consequences.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of liabilities), with a deterministic three-part test.
**Tags:** debt-definition, cost-of-capital, leases, wacc

## p.236 — Book value or market value of debt?

**Teaches:** Exercise: a distressed telecom has DCF enterprise value $1 billion and face value of debt $1 billion, but the debt trades at 50% of face. Value of equity to an investor: (a) zero if you subtract face value; (b) $500 million if you subtract market value of debt. The market value answer is correct for a going concern — existing debtholders' claim is worth only its market value. The answer changes if the liquidation value of the assets today is $1.2 billion and you plan to liquidate today. Debtholders would then be paid face value first, so equity in liquidation = 1,200 − 1,000 = $200M.
**Rules:** For going-concern valuation, subtract the market value of debt from enterprise value; in liquidation, debt is repaid at face value before equity.
**Example:** EV = $1,000M, face debt = $1,000M, debt trades at 50% → market value of debt = $500M → going-concern equity = $500M. Liquidation value $1,200M with immediate liquidation → equity = 1,200 − 1,000 = $200M.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given EV/liquidation value and debt price (choice of scenario is judgment).
**Tags:** market-value-of-debt, distress, equity-value-bridge, liquidation

## p.237 — Other potential liabilities on the way to equity value

**Teaches:** Subtract underfunded pension or health-care plans from firm value at this stage, when going from firm value to equity value. Do not double count by also including a cash-flow line for funding the shortfall. Do not count these items as debt in the cost of capital either. Contingent liabilities (e.g. an undecided lawsuit) should be subtracted at expected value.
**Formulas:** Value of contingent liability = Probability that the liability will occur × Expected value of the liability.
**Rules:** Subtract pension/health-care underfunding once (never also as a cash flow or as cost-of-capital debt); subtract contingent liabilities at probability-weighted expected value.
**Compute:** MIXED (expected-value multiplication is deterministic; probability and liability estimates are judgment).
**Tags:** pension-underfunding, contingent-liabilities, equity-value-bridge, double-counting

## p.238 — 6. Equity to employees: effect on value

**Teaches:** Firms now often pay staff (and top managers most of all) with equity options or restricted stock. The options are usually long term and on volatile stocks; restricted stock limits trading. These packages are clearly worth money. Two key questions follow. How do grants made in the PAST affect equity value per share today? How do EXPECTED FUTURE grants affect equity value today?

## p.239 — The easier problem: restricted stock grants

**Teaches:** Restricted stock is the easy case. Past grants: count already-granted restricted stock in shares outstanding today, which reduces value per share. Expected future grants: estimate their value as a percent of revenue and forecast it as part of compensation expense, reducing future income and cash flows.
**Rules:** Past restricted stock → include in the share count; future restricted stock → expense as % of revenues in forecasts.
**Compute:** MIXED (share-count inclusion is deterministic; forecasting future grants as % of revenue is judgment).
**Tags:** restricted-stock, share-count, equity-compensation, forecasting

## p.240 — The bigger challenge: employee options

**Teaches:** Options can raise the share count, but dilution per se is not the problem. Options hurt equity value at exercise because shares are issued below the prevailing market price; options are exercised only when in the money. Equivalently, the company uses cash flows that would have gone to equity investors to buy back shares to deliver at exercise. The lower cash flows reduce equity value. Options also affect equity value BEFORE exercise: valuation must build in the probability of, and the cost of, future exercise.
**Rules:** Do not treat employee options as a pure dilution/share-count problem; value the expected cost of exercise (probability × cost) against equity.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (option valuation approach; the option-pricing itself would be deterministic given a model and inputs).
**Tags:** employee-options, dilution, equity-value, option-exercise
