## p.241 — Employee options setup: a simple example (XYZ company)

**Teaches:** Sets up the baseline problem of how employee stock options affect value of equity per share. XYZ company has $100 million in free cash flow to the firm (FCFF) growing 3% forever, cost of capital 8%, 100 million shares, $1 billion debt, giving a $10/share value before any option grant. The question posed: if XYZ grants its CEO 10 million at-the-money options (strike $10), what happens to equity value per share? The answer previewed is (c): value per share decreases by LESS than 10% — options are not in-the-money yet, but they have time value, and exercise would bring cash into the firm.
**Formulas:** Value of firm = FCFF next period / (cost of capital − growth rate) = 100 / (0.08 − 0.03) = 2000. Equity = Value of firm − Debt = 2000 − 1000 = 1000. Value per share = Equity / shares = 1000/100 = $10.
**Rules:** At-the-money options still reduce value per share (they have time value); the reduction is less than the naive dilution percentage because exercise brings in cash.
**Example:** XYZ: FCFF $100M, g 3%, cost of capital 8%, 100M shares, $1B debt → firm value $2,000M, equity $1,000M, $10/share. Then a grant of 10M options at strike $10 is introduced.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (FCFF, g, cost of capital, debt, shares → value per share via growing perpetuity).

## p.242 — I. The Diluted Share Count Approach

**Teaches:** The simplest way to handle employee options is to divide equity value by the fully diluted share count (adding shares that would be issued on exercise). For XYZ this gives 1000/110 = $9.09/share. This approach fails because it ignores the cash that exercise brings into the firm, so it OVERESTIMATES the option impact and UNDERSTATES value of equity per share.
**Formulas:** Value per share (diluted approach) = Value of equity / (current shares + option shares) = 1000 / (100 + 10) = $9.09.
**Rules:** Diluted share count approach biases value per share downward — it treats options as free shares and ignores exercise proceeds.
**Example:** XYZ: equity $1,000M, diluted shares 110M → $9.09 per share.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (equity value, share count, option count → diluted value per share).
**Tags:** employee-options, dilution, diluted-shares, equity-value-per-share

## p.243 — II. The Treasury Stock Approach

**Teaches:** The treasury stock approach adds expected exercise proceeds (options × strike price) to equity value before dividing by diluted shares. For XYZ this gives (1000 + 100)/110 = $10.00 — i.e., no dilution effect at all for at-the-money options. It fails because it ignores the TIME PREMIUM on the options (so it overstates value per share), and it handles out-of-the-money options badly: if included they can perversely RAISE value per share, if excluded they are treated as nonexistent.
**Formulas:** Value per share (treasury stock) = (Value of equity + Proceeds from option exercise) / diluted shares. Proceeds = number of options × strike price = 10 × 10 = 100. Value per share = (1000 + 100)/110 = $10.
**Rules:** Treasury stock approach biases value per share upward (ignores option time value); it is unreliable for out-of-the-money options.
**Example:** XYZ: equity $1,000M + proceeds $100M, diluted shares 110M → $10.00 per share.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (equity value, options, strike, shares → value per share).
**Tags:** employee-options, treasury-stock-approach, dilution, equity-value

## p.244 — III. Option Value Drag (recommended approach)

**Teaches:** The preferred approach: value the options as options and subtract their value from equity before dividing by CURRENT (undiluted) shares. Four steps: (1) value the firm by DCF or another model; (2) subtract debt to get equity (or estimate equity directly); (3) subtract the market value or estimated value of other equity claims — warrants, conversion options in convertibles, employee options; (4) divide remaining equity by shares outstanding.
**Formulas:** Value of Warrants = Market Price per Warrant × Number of Warrants (or estimate via option pricing model). Value of Conversion Option = Market Value of Convertible Bonds − Value of Straight Debt Portion of Convertible Bonds. Employee options: value using average exercise price and maturity. Value per share = (Equity − value of all option claims) / shares outstanding.
**Rules:** Subtract option value from equity, then divide by actual (not diluted) share count.
**Compute:** MIXED — arithmetic of the four steps is deterministic; valuing the option claims (option pricing model inputs) requires estimation.
**Tags:** employee-options, option-value-drag, warrants, convertibles, equity-value-per-share

## p.245 — Caveats in valuing employee options (the dilution problem)

**Teaches:** Option pricing models can value employee options, but with four caveats: (1) employee options are long-term, so constant-variance and constant-dividend-yield assumptions are shakier; (2) they cause stock dilution; (3) they are often exercised early, making European option models dangerous; (4) they cannot be exercised until vested. These problems are partially fixed by using a model that allows shifting variance and early exercise, factors in dilution, and adjusts the value for the probability the employee will not vest.
**Rules:** Use a dilution-adjusted, early-exercise-capable model; multiply by probability of vesting.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (choice of model adjustments, vesting probability, variance shifts).
**Tags:** employee-options, option-pricing, vesting, dilution

## p.246 — Valuing Employee Options with dilution-adjusted Black-Scholes

**Teaches:** Applies a dilution-adjusted Black-Scholes model to the XYZ options. Inputs: stock price $10 (adjusted for dilution to $9.58), strike $10, maturity 10 years (can shorten for early exercise), standard deviation of stock price 40%, riskless rate 4%. Output: N(d1) = 0.8199, N(d2) = 0.3624, value per call = $5.42.
**Formulas:** Value per call = S_adj × N(d1) − K × e^(−r×t) × N(d2) = 9.58 × 0.8199 − 10 × e^(−0.04×10) × 0.3624 = $5.42, where S_adj = dilution-adjusted stock price, K = strike, r = riskless rate, t = maturity in years, N(d1), N(d2) = cumulative normal probabilities from Black-Scholes.
**Example:** XYZ CEO options: S = $10 (dilution-adjusted $9.58), K = $10, t = 10 yr, sigma = 40%, r = 4% → value per option $5.42.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC given inputs (S, K, t, sigma, r → option value via Black-Scholes); choosing sigma and effective maturity is judgment.
**Tags:** black-scholes, employee-options, option-pricing, dilution-adjustment

## p.248 — To tax adjust or not to tax adjust

**Teaches:** If option exercise creates tax advantages for the firm, the true cost of the options is lower by the tax savings. A simple adjustment: multiply the option value by (1 − tax rate) to get an after-tax option cost before subtracting from equity.
**Formulas:** After-tax option cost = Value of options × (1 − tax rate).
**Rules:** Tax-adjust option cost only when exercise creates tax deductions for the firm.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (option value, tax rate → after-tax cost).

## p.249 — Option grants in the future

**Teaches:** Expected FUTURE option grants also reduce value. Simplest mechanism: (1) estimate the value of options granted per year over recent years as a percent of revenues; (2) forecast that percentage forward, letting it shrink as revenues grow; (3) treat this as an operating expense every forecast year, which lowers operating margin and cash flow. This handles ongoing option compensation, distinct from the existing-option overhang handled by the drag approach.
**Rules:** Existing options → subtract value from equity; expected future grants → build into operating expenses (lower margins) in the forecasts.
**Compute:** MIXED — the mechanics (grant value % of revenue applied to forecast revenues) are deterministic; forecasting the declining percentage is judgment.
**Tags:** employee-options, future-grants, operating-margin, forecasting

## p.250 — When options affect equity value per share the most

**Teaches:** Option grants matter more for value per share: the lower the strike price relative to the stock price, the longer the maturity, and the more volatile the stock. The effect is magnified further if firms can reset (reprice) exercise prices downward when the stock falls, since that transfers even more value from shareholders to option holders.
**Rules:** Option drag increases with: low strike/price ratio, long maturity, high volatility, and repricing ability.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (qualitative comparative statics).

## p.252 — Valuation as a bridge between number crunchers and storytellers

**Teaches:** A good valuation is a bridge between two tribes. Number crunchers favor accounting statements, Excel spreadsheets, statistical measures, and pricing data; their delusions are precision (data is precise), objectivity (data has no bias), and control (data can control reality). Storytellers favor anecdotes, experience (own or others'), and behavioral evidence; their delusions are that creativity cannot be quantified, that if the story is good the investment will be, and that experience is the best teacher. A good valuation connects both.

## p.257 — Step 3: Check the narrative — possible, plausible, probable

**Teaches:** Test the narrative against history, economic first principles, and common sense by classifying claims along a probability-of-occurrence spectrum. "IT IS POSSIBLE" (probability cannot be assessed): could happen but you don't know what, when, or what it will look like → VALUATION RESPONSE: value as an OPTION, with value increasing in the size of the possible market and the exclusivity of the firm's access to it. "IT IS PLAUSIBLE" (low probability): a reasoned argument can be made though no tangible evidence yet → RESPONSE: show as expected GROWTH, adjusting for risk in the expected return; value increases with market size and the firm's competitive advantages. "IT IS PROBABLE" (increasing probability): expected to happen with some basis or evidence, though uncertainty remains → RESPONSE: show in BASE YEAR numbers and expected cash flows, adjusting for risk in expected return. Moving from possible to plausible comes from gauging market potential and testing products; from plausible to probable comes from product success and financial results.
**Rules:** Possible → option value; Plausible → expected growth; Probable → base-year numbers and expected cash flows.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification of narrative claims).
**Tags:** narrative-and-numbers, possible-plausible-probable, real-options, growth

## p.258 — The Impossible, the Implausible and the Improbable

**Teaches:** Screens for broken narratives. THE IMPOSSIBLE (never allow): (a) Bigger than the economy — perpetual growth rate for the company > growth rate of the economy; (b) Bigger than the total market — revenues implying more than 100% market share; (c) Profit margin > 100% — earnings growth exceeding revenue growth long enough to push margins above 100%; (d) Depreciation without cap ex — depreciation exceeding cap ex in perpetuity. THE IMPLAUSIBLE (needs strong justification): growth forever without reinvestment; profits/growth with no competition; high returns with no risk. THE IMPROBABLE: a value narrative must trade off among the triangle of Growth, Risk, and Reinvestment — combinations like high growth AND low risk, high growth AND low reinvestment, or low risk AND high reinvestment are improbable together.
**Rules:** Perpetual g ≤ economy growth rate; market share ≤ 100%; margin ≤ 100%; perpetuity depreciation ≤ cap ex; be suspicious of growth-without-reinvestment, profits-without-competition, returns-without-risk, and of narratives combining two "good" corners of the growth/risk/reinvestment triangle.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (consistency screening of assumptions).
**Tags:** narrative-consistency, terminal-growth, market-share, reinvestment, sanity-checks

## p.260 — The Runaway Story: when you want a story to be true

**Teaches:** A "runaway" business story typically has three ingredients: (1) a charismatic, likeable narrator you want to see succeed (you like them or they'd be a good role model); (2) a story about disrupting a business whose status quo everyone dislikes; (3) a societal benefit as a bonus — if the story holds, society and humanity benefit. Because you WANT the story to work out, you stop asking questions, since the answers might put the story at risk. This is the behavioral trap behind overhyped ventures.
**Rules:** Warning signs of a runaway story: charismatic narrator + disliked status quo being disrupted + claimed societal benefit → discipline yourself to keep asking questions.

## p.262 — When runaway stories melt down

**Teaches:** The Meltdown Story equation has three ingredients that add up to a collapse:
- UNTRUSTWORTHY STORYTELLER: a narrator whose words or actions have made them untrustworthy.
- STORY AT WAR WITH NUMBERS: the company's narrative conflicts with its own actions and/or its reported results.
- BAD BUSINESS MODEL: a fundamental flaw threatens profitability or survival, and management denies it or is opaque about fixing it.

Sum = MELTDOWN STORY: investors, lenders and observers question the story, refuse the company's spin on numbers, and push the price down.
**Rules:** Meltdown = untrustworthy storyteller + narrative/numbers conflict + unaddressed business-model flaw.

## p.266 — Step 4: Value the company (Uber intrinsic valuation, June 8 2014)

**Teaches:** The full Uber DCF. Global taxi market $100B growing 6%/yr for 10 years; Uber's share rises to 10% by year 10; Uber keeps 20% of gross receipts; operating margin ramps from 7.00% to 40.00%; tax rate rises 31% → 40%; sales/capital ratio 5.00 drives reinvestment ($5 incremental revenue per $1 incremental capital); cost of capital 12% (top decile of US firms) for years 1–5 declining to 8% by year 10. Discounting cash flows plus terminal value at the cumulated cost of capital gives value of operating assets = $6,595M; adjusting for 10% failure probability: expected value = 6,595 × 0.9 = $5,895M. ## p.270 — Step 6: Be ready to modify the narrative as events unfold

**Teaches:** Narratives must be revisited. Three kinds of narrative revision: NARRATIVE BREAK/END — external (legal, political, economic) or internal (management, competitive, default) events kill the narrative; your valuation estimates (cash flows, risk, growth, value) are no longer operative; handle by estimating the probability it occurs and its consequences. NARRATIVE SHIFT — improvement or deterioration in the initial business model, changing market size, market share and/or profitability; modify valuation estimates for the new data; handle with Monte Carlo simulations or scenario analysis. NARRATIVE CHANGE (expansion or contraction) — unexpected entry/success in a new market or unexpected exit/failure in an existing one; redo valuation with new overall market potential and characteristics; handle with real options.
**Rules:** Break/end → probability × consequences; shift → Monte Carlo / scenario analysis; change → real options.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** narrative-revision, scenario-analysis, monte-carlo, real-options

## p.275 — Training wheels valuation: Con Ed in August 2008 (stable-growth DDM)

**Teaches:** Values Consolidated Edison with a stable-growth Gordon dividend discount model, with three consistency tests. Test 1 (paying dividends like a stable firm?): trailing-12-month (through June 2008) EPS $3.17, DPS $2.32, payout ratio 73%. Test 2 (growth consistent with fundamentals?): retention ratio 27%, ROE = cost of equity = 7.7%, so expected growth = 0.27 × 0.077 = 2.1% forever. Test 3 (risk consistent with stability?): beta 0.80 is at the low end of the stable-company beta range 0.8–1.2. Cost of equity = 4.1% + 0.8 × 4.5% = 7.70% (riskfree = 10-yr T-bond 4.10%; beta 0.80 for regulated power utilities; ERP 4.5% = implied US premium 8/2008). Value per share = 2.32 × 1.021 / (0.077 − 0.021) = $42.30, versus a market price of $40.76 on August 12, 2008 — modestly undervalued. Why this model: stable growth because a regulated utility is restricted from new growth markets and NY population/power needs grow slowly (g ≈ 2%); equity because the debt ratio has been stable (~70% equity / 30% debt) for decades; dividends because the firm has paid out ~97% of FCFE as dividends over five years.
**Formulas:** Value per share today = Expected DPS next year / (Cost of equity − growth rate) = 2.32 × (1.021)/(0.077 − 0.021) = $42.30. Cost of equity = riskfree rate + beta × ERP = 4.1% + 0.8 × 4.5% = 7.70%. Expected growth = retention ratio × ROE = 0.27 × 0.077 = 2.1%.
**Rules:** Use a stable-growth DDM only when: payout resembles a stable firm (high payout), fundamental growth (retention × ROE) matches assumed g, beta lies in the stable range 0.8–1.2, capital structure is stable, and dividends ≈ FCFE (here ~97%).
**Example:** Con Ed, Aug 2008: EPS $3.17, DPS $2.32, payout 73%, beta 0.80, riskfree 4.10%, ERP 4.5%, cost of equity 7.70%, g 2.1% → value $42.30 vs price $40.76 (Aug 12, 2008).
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (DPS, g, riskfree, beta, ERP → cost of equity and value per share); model-choice tests are judgment.

## p.277 — From DCF value to target price and expected returns (Con Ed)
