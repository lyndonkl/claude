## p.281 — Valuing the S&P 500 with an Augmented Dividend Discount Model (Jan 2020)

**Teaches:** Damodaran values the entire S&P 500 index on January 1, 2020 using a 2-stage augmented dividend discount model. Dividends are "augmented" by adding stock buybacks because companies increasingly return cash via buybacks; a 2-stage model is used because near-term expected growth (analyst estimate 3.96% for 5 years) exceeds stable growth. In stable growth, index earnings are assumed to grow at the riskfree rate (g = 1.92%), on the logic that the economy grows at roughly that rate. Discounting the 5 years of augmented dividends plus the terminal value at the cost of equity gives an intrinsic index value of 3357.86, versus an actual index level of 3230.78 on Jan 1, 2020 — the index looked slightly undervalued.
**Formulas:**
- Cost of equity = Riskfree rate + Beta * Equity risk premium = 1.92% + 1.00*(5.00%) = 6.92%. (Beta = 1.00 because the S&P 500 reflects the overall market; ERP of 5% set just above the average ERP over the last 20 years.)
- Terminal value = Augmented dividends in year 6 / (r - g) = (182.73 * 1.0192) / (0.0692 - 0.0192) = 3274.75, where r = cost of equity, g = stable growth rate.
- Value of index = PV of expected augmented dividends years 1-5 + PV of terminal value, all discounted at cost of equity.

## p.285 — The Fundamental Determinants of Value

**Teaches:** Every valuation must answer four questions: (1) What are the cash flows from existing assets? (equity view: cash flows after debt payments; firm view: cash flows before debt payments). (2) What is the value added by growth assets? (equity: growth in equity earnings/cash flows; firm: growth in operating earnings/cash flows). (3) How risky are the cash flows from both existing and growth assets? (equity: risk in the company's equity; firm: risk in the firm's operations). (4) When will the firm become a mature firm, and what are the potential roadblocks? This four-question framework is reused throughout the "dark side" section to diagnose why each difficult company type is hard to value.

## p.286 — The Dark Side of Valuation: When Valuation Gets Hard

**Teaches:** Valuing stable, money-making companies with consistent, clear accounting statements, a long stable history, and many comparable firms is easy. The true test of valuation skill is "difficult" companies. The challenges are greatest when valuing: (1) young companies early in the life cycle in young businesses; (2) companies that do not fit the accounting mold; (3) companies facing substantial truncation risk (default or nationalization risk).

## p.287 — Taxonomy of Difficult-to-Value Companies

**Teaches:** Difficult-to-value companies arise along three dimensions. Across the life cycle: young growth firms (limited history, small revenues with big operating losses, high failure propensity); mature companies in transition (history must be abandoned and parameters reestimated when change happens or is forced); declining/distressed firms (long but irrelevant history, declining markets, high debt loads, likelihood of distress). Across markets: emerging-market companies are hard because of their structures, country-risk exposure, and poor corporate governance. Across sectors: financial-service firms (opaque statements, hard-to-estimate basic inputs — you end up trusting managers); commodity and cyclical firms (valuations hostage to commodity prices and macro growth); firms with intangible assets (accounting principles break down).

## p.288 — I. The Challenge with Young Companies

**Teaches:** Applies the four-question framework (p.285) to young companies. Existing assets: cash flows are non-existent or negative. Growth assets: judgments on revenues/profits are difficult because there is no history; with no product/service it is hard to gauge market potential or profitability — the entire value lies in future growth with little to base estimates on. Risk: limited historical earnings data and no market prices for securities make risk hard to assess. Maturity: will the firm survive the gauntlet of market demand and competition, and even if it does, when it matures is hard to pin down. ## p.289 — Young Companies in Young Businesses: Why Information Runs Out

**Teaches:** Valuation normally draws on three information sources: (1) the firm's current financial statements (how much did it sell, how much did it earn); (2) the firm's financial history (revenue/earnings growth over time, cost structure, profitability, macro susceptibility for cyclical firms); (3) industry and comparable-firm data (what happens to margins, revenue growth, reinvestment needs, and risk as firms mature). Young companies in young businesses lack all three, and it is precisely then that analysts are tempted by the dark side: "paradigm shifts" are declared, new metrics are invented, and the story dominates while the numbers lag.

## p.291 — Lesson 1: Don't Sweat the Small Stuff (Discount Rates for Young Firms)

**Teaches:** For young companies, do not obsess over the regression beta — Amazon's Bloomberg historical beta (weekly, 2/27/98-2/18/00 vs S&P 500) had raw beta 2.23, adjusted beta 1.82, R-squared only 0.17, and standard error of beta 0.50, making it useless. Rules for young-company discount rates: (1) spotlight the business the company is in and use the beta of that business (bottom-up); (2) do NOT try to incorporate failure risk into the discount rate; (3) let the cost of capital change over time as the company changes; (4) if desperate, use the cross-section of costs of capital across all companies and take the 90th or 95th percentile as a starting estimate.
**Rules:** Use sector/bottom-up beta, not regression beta; keep failure risk out of the discount rate (handle it separately in value); make cost of capital time-varying; fallback = 90th-95th percentile of the cost-of-capital distribution across all firms.

## p.295 — Lesson 5: Dilution Is Already Taken Care Of in DCF

**Teaches:** Young growth companies will almost certainly increase shares outstanding, for two reasons: they must issue new shares to raise cash for projects or to pay for acquisitions, and they grant employee/manager options that get exercised if the company succeeds. In a properly done DCF, both effects are already in value per share even though the current share count is used: (1) the need for new equity issues shows up as negative FCFF in early years, and the present value of those negative cash flows drags down current equity value — that IS the dilution effect; (2) outstanding options are valued with an option pricing model (capturing exercise likelihood and exercise price) and netted out against equity value. Do not additionally inflate the share count for expected future issuance.
**Rules:** Never double-count dilution: use current shares, let negative early FCFF capture future equity issuance, and subtract the option value of existing options from equity value.

## p.299 — Lesson 8: You Will Be Wrong 100% of the Time — and It's Not Your Fault

**Teaches:** No matter how careful the inputs or how well-structured the model, the estimated value will change as new information arrives about the company, the business, and the economy. Rather than being defensive about changing valuations, recognize that this is the essence of risk: adjust and adapt the model as information comes out. A test of bias: if your valuations are unbiased, you should raise estimated values about as often as you lower them — good and bad news should hit your valuations in equal doses over time.
**Rules:** Bias test — over time, upward revisions to your valuations should be roughly as frequent as downward revisions.

## p.306 — Hormel Foods: The Value of Changing Control (Status Quo vs Restructured)

**Teaches:** Hormel Foods (packaged meats, public ~80 years; 2008 after-tax operating income $315m, 5% compounded growth over prior 5 years) is valued twice. ## p.311 — a. Dealing with Decline

**Teaches:** In decline, firms see falling revenues and lower margins, translating into negative expected growth. If run by good managers, they will not fight the decline: they adapt, shutting down or selling investments that do not earn the cost of capital — producing negative net capital expenditures (depreciation exceeds capex), declining working capital, and an overall negative reinvestment rate. Best case: the firm sheds bad assets, becomes much smaller and healthier, and settles into long-term stable growth. The investor's worst case: managers in denial keep expanding through bad investments that earn below the cost of capital — such firms may grow revenues and operating income but destroy value along the way.
**Rules:** For a well-managed declining firm, expect negative reinvestment (asset shedding) and negative growth; growth funded by sub-cost-of-capital investments destroys value.

## p.313 — b. Dealing with the Downside of Distress

**Teaches:** A DCF values a firm as a going concern; if there is a significant likelihood of the firm failing before reaching stable growth, and its assets would then be sold for less than the present value of expected cash flows (a distress sale), a straight DCF OVERSTATES value. Fix: probability-weight going-concern and distress outcomes. Three ways to estimate the probability of distress: (1) use the bond rating and the historical cumulative default probability over 10 years for that rating; (2) estimate it with a probit model; (3) back it out of the market value/price of the firm's bonds. The distress sale value of equity is usually best estimated as a percent of book value, and it will be lower if the economy is doing badly and other firms in the same business are also in distress.
**Formulas:** Value of equity = DCF value of equity * (1 - Probability of distress) + Distress sale value of equity * (Probability of distress).
**Rules:** Three estimation routes for P(distress): bond-rating cumulative default tables, probit, or implied from bond prices; distress sale value ≈ percent of book value, lowered in bad economies/crowded distress.
**Compute:** MIXED — the weighting formula is deterministic given P(distress) and distress-sale value; estimating those inputs is judgment.

## p.315 — Adjusting Las Vegas Sands' Value for Distress

**Teaches:** LVS was rated B+ in February 2009; historically 28.25% of B+ bonds default within 10 years, but the market implied worse. ## p.317 — Lesson 1: Country Risk — Scalpel, Not Bludgeon

**Teaches:** Emerging-market companies are undoubtedly exposed to extra country risk because they are incorporated in countries with more political and economic risk. But exposure is company-specific, not uniform: not all emerging-market companies are equally exposed, and many developed-market companies have emerging-market exposure through their operations. Two ways to capture it: (1) the "weighted country risk premium," weighting country ERPs by where the company gets its revenues; (2) the lambda approach, which can incorporate more than revenues (e.g., production facilities, risk-management) to scale exposure to the country risk premium.
**Rules:** Attach country risk by exposure (revenue-weighted premium or lambda), not by country of incorporation.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** country-risk, lambda, equity-risk-premium, emerging-markets

## p.319 — Lesson 2: Currency Should Not Matter

**Teaches:** You can value any company in any currency — a Brazilian company in nominal reais, US dollars, or Swiss francs — and the value should be invariant. The consistency requirement: cash flows and discount rates must be in the same currency; a high-inflation currency means both growth rates and discount rates will be much higher, and the effects offset. For cash-flow consistency across currencies, use expected exchange rates that reflect purchasing power parity: the higher-inflation currency must depreciate by the inflation differential each year.
**Rules:** Match currency of cash flows and discount rate; convert with PPP-consistent expected exchange rates (annual depreciation = inflation differential).
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (principle; the PPP conversion itself is deterministic given inflation rates).
**Tags:** currency-consistency, purchasing-power-parity, inflation, emerging-markets
