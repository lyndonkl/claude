## p.2 — The nature of contractual claims
**Teaches:** A contractual claim cash flow is set at the time a contract or transaction is initiated, with the promisor committing to deliver that cash flow at the specified time. The contracted cash flow can be either a constant cash flow or tied to an observable and specific index or value. A conventional fixed rate coupon bond is an example of the first (constant cash flow); a floating rate bond is an example of the second (index-tied cash flow).

## p.3 — The Effect of Default
**Teaches:** Even though a claim is contractually set, the promisor may default. If the promisor has no default risk, the claim is a risk free claim. The only entities that can possibly be default free are governments that control the printing of their currency. Even then, not all governments are default free. If there is default risk, you must adjust for the likelihood of default when valuing the claim.
**Rules:** Only currency-printing governments can be default free; do not assume all government debt is risk free. Any claim with default risk must be valued with an adjustment for the likelihood of default.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (assessing default risk requires judgment)

## p.4 — A fixed rate, risk free bond
**Teaches:** To value a fixed rate, risk free bond (issued by a default free government), discount the coupons (an annuity) and the face value at the risk free rate. When the market interest rate is below the coupon rate, the bond trades above par (par = face value, here $1000).
**Formulas:** Price of Bond = C * PV(A, n, r) + F / (1+r)^n. Here C = annual coupon in dollars (coupon rate x face value); n = years to maturity; r = risk free rate; F = face value. PV(A, n, r) = present value of a $1 annuity for n periods at rate r = (1 - (1+r)^(-n)) / r.
**Rules:** If market interest rate < coupon rate, bond trades above par; if market rate > coupon rate, bond trades below par.
**Example:** 3% coupon, 10-year US Treasury bond, face value $1000, US$ risk free rate for 10 years = 2%: Price = 30 * PV(A, 10, 2%) + 1000/1.02^10 = $1,089.83. Trades above par because market rate (2%) < coupon rate (3%).

## p.5 — The Yield to Maturity and Yield on a Bond
**Teaches:** Given a bond's price, you can solve for the interest rate that makes the present value of coupons plus face value equal to the price; that IRR is the bond's yield to maturity (YTM). The bond yield is a related but less involved concept: the coupon divided by the price of the bond (current yield).
**Formulas:** Price = C * PV(A, n, r) + F/(1+r)^n solved for r gives YTM, where C = annual coupon, F = face value, n = years to maturity, r = YTM (the IRR). Bond Yield = Coupon / Price of Bond.
**Example:** 10-year, 3% coupon bond ($30 coupon, $1000 face) priced at $1043.76: solve 1043.76 = 30 * PV(A, 10, r) + 1000/(1+r)^10 → YTM = 2.50%. Bond Yield = 30/1043.76 = 2.87%.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (price, coupon, face, maturity → YTM via IRR root-finding; coupon/price → yield)
**Tags:** yield-to-maturity, current-yield, irr, bond-pricing

## p.6 — Bond Convexity
**Teaches:** Bond prices do not respond evenly to equal-sized up and down moves in interest rates: for the same 1% rate change, the price gain from a rate fall is larger than the price loss from a rate rise. This asymmetric response to interest rate changes is called convexity.
**Formulas:** Same pricing formula: Price = C * PV(A, n, r) + F/(1+r)^n (C = coupon, n = periods, r = market rate, F = face value); convexity is observed by repricing at shifted rates.
**Example:** 3% coupon, 10-year, $1000 face bond at r = 2.5%: Price = 30*PV(A,10,2.5%) + 1000/1.025^10 = $1043.76. Rate rises to 3.5%: Price = 30*PV(A,10,3.5%) + 1000/1.035^10 = $958.41, a -8.18% change. Rate falls to 1.5%: Price = 30*PV(A,10,1.5%) + 1000/1.015^10 = $1138.33, a +9.06% change. Gain (+9.06%) exceeds loss (-8.18%): convexity.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (bond terms + two rate scenarios → prices and percentage changes)
**Tags:** convexity, interest-rate-risk, bond-pricing

## p.9 — Default Risk
**Teaches:** When a fixed rate bond or loan is issued by an entity with default risk, there is a likelihood you will not receive the promised coupon or principal payments. The risk of not being paid contractually due payments is called default risk or credit risk. To compensate, the lender charges a higher interest rate, with the addition over the risk free rate called the default spread.
**Formulas:** Default-risk adjusted rate = Risk free rate + Default Spread.
**Compute:** MIXED (adding the spread is deterministic; estimating the appropriate default spread is judgment)
**Tags:** default-risk, credit-risk, default-spread

## p.11 — Pricing a bond with default risk
**Teaches:** To price a bond with default risk, discount the promised cash flows (coupons and face value) at a rate that includes the default spread (risk free rate + default spread).
**Formulas:** Price of Bond = C * PV(A, n, r_f + s) + F/(1 + r_f + s)^n. Here C = annual coupon; n = years to maturity; r_f = risk free rate; s = default spread for the bond's rating; F = face value; PV(A, n, r) = annuity present value factor.
**Example:** BBB-rated, 3% coupon, 10-year corporate bond in January 2017; risk free rate = 2.5%, default spread = 1.75%, so discount rate = 4.25%: Price = 30 * PV(A, 10, 4.25%) + 1000/1.0425^10 = $899.87.
**Compute:** MIXED (given coupon, maturity, face, risk free rate, and spread the price is deterministic; choosing the spread/rating is judgment)
**Tags:** bond-pricing, default-spread, corporate-bonds

## p.12 — Changing Default Risk
**Teaches:** Because bonds of riskier firms are priced lower, prices of corporate bonds issued by firms that slip into distress will drop even if overall market interest rates do not change — default risk changes alone move bond prices. Bonds rated below BBB are considered below investment grade, and bonds in the lower rating classes are classified as "high yield" bonds.
**Rules:** Rating below BBB = below investment grade; lower rating classes = "high yield" bonds. Rising default risk lowers a bond's price independently of market rate movements.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (judging distress and rating changes and their price effect)
**Tags:** default-risk, investment-grade, high-yield, credit-ratings

## p.13 — Pricing Floating Rate Bonds
**Teaches:** A floating rate bond whose interest rate resets to the market interest rate each period should trade at par, because its coupon always equals the current required rate. Even when the reset is imperfect, floating rate bonds should trade much closer to par than otherwise similar fixed rate bonds.
**Rules:** Perfect periodic reset to market rate → bond trades at par; imperfect reset → price stays much closer to par than a comparable fixed rate bond.
