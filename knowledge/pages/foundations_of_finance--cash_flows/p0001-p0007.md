## p.2 — Cash flows versus earnings

**Teaches:** When people judge a firm's health, they almost always look at the bottom line: earnings. But earnings can differ a lot from cash flows, for two reasons. First, accrual accounting books a sale or a cost when it happens, not when cash comes in or goes out. Second, accounting splits costs into operating, capital, and financing expenses. Operating and financing costs are taken out of revenues to get earnings. Capital costs are not taken out right away; they are spread over time as depreciation or amortization. So earnings can misstate the cash a firm truly made in a period.

## p.3 — Getting from earnings to cash flows

**Teaches:** Three steps convert accounting earnings into cash flows for a period. (1) Add back non-cash accounting expenses, such as depreciation. (2) Subtract the capital expenses (capex) incurred in that period. (3) Subtract the change in non-cash working capital during that period. The resulting cash flow can be much higher than earnings, close to them, or much lower. When earnings and cash flows disagree, you go with the cash flows.
**Formulas:** Cash flow = Earnings + Non-cash expenses (e.g., depreciation and amortization) − Capital expenditures − Change in non-cash working capital. Where:
- Earnings = accounting earnings for the period.
- Non-cash expenses = expenses deducted in accounting with no cash outlay.
- Capital expenditures = spending on long-lived assets during the period.
- Change in non-cash working capital = the rise (or fall) in working capital, excluding cash, during the period.
**Rules:** When earnings and cash flows disagree, trust the cash flows.

## p.4 — Types of cash flows

**Teaches:** Cash flows come in three types. Contractually set cash flows are fixed when a contract begins; they are sometimes a constant amount and sometimes tied to a scalar both sides agree on. Residual cash flows are what is left over after contract claims have been met. Contingent cash flows occur only if a stated event happens.
**Rules:** How to classify a claim: fixed by contract → contractual; leftover after claims are paid → residual; depends on an event → contingent.

## p.5 — Contractual cash flow claims

**Teaches:** Contractual cash flow claims can be constant or variable. Constant: the easiest and most common cases are a fixed-rate bank loan and a corporate bond. The interest payment or coupon is set up front. The principal must also be paid back, either over the life of the loan (term bank loan) or at the end of its life (balloon payment). Variable: the terms are set up front, but the amount moves with a named variable. In a floating-rate loan, each period's interest payment is linked to a market rate you can watch, such as LIBOR or the T-Bond rate. In a commodity loan, the interest payment may be tied to the price of a commodity.
**Rules:** Principal repayment structures: repaid over the loan's life = term bank loan; repaid at the end of life = balloon payment.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (classification/definitions; actual payment amounts on such loans would be deterministic given contract terms).

## p.6 — Residual cash flow claims

**Teaches:** A residual cash flow is the cash left over after contract claims have been met. For an equity investor, it is the cash flow after taxes, capital investments, and debt payments. It can be negative if the contract claims exceed the cash that operations generate.
**Formulas:** Residual cash flow (to equity) = Cash from operations − Taxes − Capital investments − Debt payments. The result may be negative. Where:
- Cash from operations = operating cash made in the period.
- Debt payments = interest and principal owed under the debt contract.
**Compute:** DETERMINISTIC (inputs: operating cash, taxes, capital investments, debt payments → output: residual cash flow to equity).

## p.7 — Contingent cash flow claims

**Teaches:** When a cash flow depends on an event, you can compute its expected value by putting a probability on the event. In some cases the best tool is an option pricing model instead. This holds above all when you would have to judge odds for many events at once.
**Formulas:** Expected contingent cash flow = Probability of event × Cash flow if the event occurs (implied by the text). Where:
- Probability of event = your judged odds that the trigger event happens.
- Cash flow if the event occurs = the payoff given the event.
**Rules:** If the odds for many events are too hard to judge, use an option pricing model instead of a probability-weighted expected value.
**Compute:** MIXED (the expected-value product is deterministic once you have a probability; judging that probability, or setting up an option pricing model, is judgment).
