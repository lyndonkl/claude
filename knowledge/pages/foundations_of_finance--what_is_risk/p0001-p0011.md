## p.2 — Risk is ubiquitous and has always been around
**Teaches:** Risk has always been part of human life. In humanity's earliest days, the main risks were physical, and physical risk was tied directly to material reward. Shipping and trade began to split physical risk from economic reward. Seamen still earned rewards through physical exposure — scurvy, pirates, storms. Wealthy merchants, by contrast, bet money on ships returning with bounty and bore only economic risk. Modern financial markets and the leisure business have widened this split even more.

## p.3 — Knight's risk vs. uncertainty distinction
**Teaches:** In 1921 Frank Knight split risk from uncertainty. If the uncertainty can be quantified, treat it as risk. If not, call it uncertainty. His illustration: two people draw from an urn of red and black balls. The first does not know the color mix, so he faces uncertainty. The second knows there are three red balls for each black ball, so he faces risk. Damodaran argues this focus on measurable versus unmeasurable is misplaced. Measurable risk is easier to insure, but in finance we care about all uncertainty, measurable or not.
**Rules:** Knight's classification — quantifiable uncertainty = "risk"; unquantifiable uncertainty = "uncertainty". Damodaran's position: treat both as relevant.

## p.4 — A good definition of risk: both danger and opportunity
**Teaches:** Traditional definitions treat risk purely as a negative. Webster's dictionary, for example, defines risk as "exposing to danger or hazard." In investing and business, risk cannot be only a negative. If it were, the logical defense would be to avoid it altogether. Risk is both positive and negative — it produces good and bad outcomes. Whether to take or avoid a risk depends on whether the positives outweigh the negatives.
**Rules:** Take a risk when its expected upside outweighs its downside; avoid it otherwise.

## p.5 — Coin-flip experiment: are you risk averse?
**Teaches:** A thought experiment that reveals risk aversion. A coin is flipped: you win $1 if the first flip is tails, and the game stops on heads. If you win, you get another flip that doubles your winnings on tails. The game continues, with the prize doubling at each stage, until heads appears. The question posed: how much would you pay to enter this gamble? This sets up the St. Petersburg Paradox on the next page.
**Example:** Payoff structure — $1 after one tails, $2 after two, $4 after three; in general 2^(n-1) dollars for n straight tails, ending at the first heads.
**Compute:** MIXED — the gamble's expected value is DETERMINISTIC (chance 1/2^n of winning 2^(n-1) at stage n → $0.50 per stage, infinite sum). The price a person would pay is JUDGMENT.

## p.6 — The Bernoulli experiment and the St. Petersburg Paradox
**Teaches:** Nicholas Bernoulli ran this coin-flip experiment in the 1700s. The gamble's expected value is infinite, yet people paid on average only about $2 to play. This gap is the St. Petersburg Paradox. Bernoulli noted two other phenomena. First, the value attached to the gamble varies across individuals. Second, the utility from an extra dollar falls as wealth rises — diminishing marginal utility, the root of risk aversion.
**Rules:** Diminishing marginal utility: each extra dollar of wealth adds less utility than the one before. Willingness to pay for a gamble varies by person.
**Example:** St. Petersburg gamble — expected value infinite; average observed price paid ≈ $2.
**Compute:** MIXED — the expected value is DETERMINISTIC; the observed $2 price and its utility interpretation are empirical/JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** st-petersburg-paradox, bernoulli, marginal-utility, risk-aversion

## p.7 — Measuring risk aversion: certainty equivalents and coefficients
**Teaches:** Two technical ways to measure risk aversion. (a) Certainty equivalents. The price a person will pay for an uncertain bet with a known expected value is the certainty equivalent value. The gap between the expected value and the certainty equivalent measures risk aversion. (b) Risk aversion coefficients. If utility can be written as a function of wealth, the risk aversion coefficient measures how much utility is gained or lost as wealth is added or subtracted.
**Formulas:** Risk aversion measure = Expected value of gamble − Certainty equivalent. The certainty equivalent (CE) is the sure amount a person treats as equal in value to the risky bet. A larger gap means greater risk aversion; a zero gap means risk neutrality.

## p.8 — Four sources of evidence on risk aversion
**Teaches:** Four ways economists gather evidence on risk aversion. (I) Experimental studies: controlled experiments offer subjects choices between gambles and record how they choose. (II) Surveys: these look at actual behavior across large samples — portfolio choices and insurance decisions, for instance — unlike experiments, which observe few subjects. (III) Pricing of risky assets: financial markets are experiments in progress, with millions of people expressing risk preferences through the prices they set. (IV) Game shows, race tracks and gambling: economists study data from gambling events to see how people behave when facing risky choices.
**Compute:** JUDGMENT (methodological taxonomy).

## p.9 — Findings: universally risk averse, with differences across people
**Teaches:** People are generally risk averse, with systematic group differences. Male vs. female: men are less risk averse than women for small bets. For larger, consequential bets they are as risk averse, if not more. Naive vs. experienced: a study compared bids for an asset and found naive students more risk averse than construction-industry experts. Young vs. old: risk aversion rises with age, and single people are less risk averse than married people. Racial and cultural differences: humans share far more in common on risk aversion than they differ.
**Rules:** Risk aversion rises with age. Experience lowers measured risk aversion. Gender gaps shrink or reverse as stakes grow. Cultural differences are second-order.

## p.10 — Behavioral quirks in risk taking
**Teaches:** Five documented quirks in risk behavior. Framing: people prefer "save 200 of 600 people" over "a one-third chance everyone is saved," though the options are mathematically the same — presentation changes choices. Loss aversion: most people take $750 for sure over a 75% chance of $1,000, yet gamble on a 75% chance of losing $1,000 rather than lose $750 for sure. Losses loom larger than equal gains. Myopic loss aversion: more frequent feedback on where they stand makes people more risk averse. House money effect: people take more risk with found money (money obtained easily) than with earned money. Breakeven effect: subjects who have lost money will gamble on otherwise unattractive lotteries that offer a chance to break even.
**Rules:** Framing changes choices among equivalent options. People are risk averse over gains but risk seeking over losses. Frequent monitoring raises risk aversion. Found money is risked more freely than earned money. Prior losses push people toward gambles that could break even.
**Example:** Loss aversion pair — (a) $750 certain vs. 75% chance of a $1,000 gain (both expect $750); (b) lose $750 certain vs. 75% chance of losing $1,000 (both expect −$750). Framing pair — save 200 of 600 for sure vs. a 1/3 chance all 600 are saved (both expect 200 saved).
**Compute:** MIXED — expected values of the gambles are DETERMINISTIC (probabilities and payoffs → EV); the behavioral effects are empirical/JUDGMENT.
**Tags:** loss-aversion, framing, house-money-effect, breakeven-effect, behavioral-finance

## p.11 — In summary: risk aversion and its quirks
**Teaches:** Session summary. People are generally risk averse, and more so when stakes are large than when small. Risk aversion varies widely across the population and across sub-groups. Four quirks stand out. (1) Losses hurt far more than equal gains help (loss aversion), and frequent monitoring makes this worse. (2) Choices among risky options depend on how the choice is presented (framing). (3) People risk "found money" more freely than earned money (house money effect). (4) Risk seeking replaces risk aversion in two cases: chasing a large payoff with a tiny chance of success (long shot bias), and gambling to recover prior losses (break even effect).
**Rules:** Risk aversion grows with stake size. Frequent monitoring amplifies loss aversion. Long shot bias and the break-even effect are the two cases where risk seeking replaces risk aversion.
